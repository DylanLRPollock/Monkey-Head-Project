Connectivity and Interoperability in the Apple
Ecosystem (2017–2020)
Introduction
From 2017 through 2020, Apple’s hardware ecosystem underwent significant transitions in connectivity and
interoperability. This period saw the standardization of Thunderbolt 3 across Macs, the maturation of
wireless protocols like 802.11ac Wi-Fi and Bluetooth 5.0, and the expansion of Apple’s Continuity features
linking  Macs  with  iPhones,  iPads,  and  peripherals.  This  paper  provides  a  technical  analysis  of  these
developments, focusing on how two example machines – a 2017 iMac 5K (27-inch) and a 2019 16‑inch
MacBook Pro – leverage Apple’s connectivity advancements. We compare wired interfaces (Thunderbolt
versions and USB), wireless capabilities (Wi-Fi and Bluetooth standards), peer-to-peer sharing mechanisms
(notably AirDrop over Apple Wireless Direct Link), and ecosystem integration features (Continuity, Sidecar,
etc.). We also examine how these Macs fit into multi-device workflows, their roles in OS deployment and
security (e.g. the T2 security chip), peripheral compatibility (external displays, eGPUs, etc.), and upgrade
paths. Through detailed specifications and comparative tables, we highlight both the commonalities and
key differences that define the connectivity performance of the 2017 iMac and 2019 MacBook Pro within
Apple’s broader ecosystem.
Wired Connectivity: Thunderbolt and USB
Thunderbolt Evolution (TB1 to TB3): Apple has consistently pushed high-bandwidth interfaces, beginning
with Thunderbolt 1 in 2011 (10 Gb/s per channel) and Thunderbolt 2 in 2013 (20 Gb/s). Thunderbolt 3,
introduced in late 2015, doubled the bandwidth to 40 Gb/s and crucially adopted the USB-C connector,
merging data, video, and power into one port
. A single Thunderbolt 3 (TB3) port carries a PCI
Express (PCIe) data bus and dual DisplayPort 1.2 streams, enabling simultaneous high-speed peripheral and
display connections
. For example, one TB3 link supports two 4K monitors or one 5K monitor at 60 Hz
from a single port
. The 40 Gb/s bandwidth is also enough to handle massive data transfers while driving
displays, making TB3 a “computer port nirvana” that fulfills the promise of USB-C for one-cable docking,
external GPUs, and more
. TB3 introduced support for USB 3.1 Gen2 (10 Gb/s) natively over the USB-
C plug, meaning the same port can connect billions of USB devices at up to 10 Gb/s
. Furthermore, TB3
integrates 100 W power delivery for charging and can supply up to 15 W to bus-powered accessories
.
Notably,  Thunderbolt  remains  backward-compatible:  older  Thunderbolt   1/2  devices  (Mini  DisplayPort
connector) work via a USB-C adapter without performance loss
. By 2017, Apple had fully embraced
Thunderbolt 3 on Macs – including the iMac and MacBook Pro in this study – standardizing on the USB-C
port for all high-speed wired connectivity.
2017 iMac 5K Wired Interfaces: The Mid-2017 iMac 27″ was Apple’s first 5K iMac to feature Thunderbolt 3/
USB-C ports (replacing the Thunderbolt 2 ports of the 2015 model). It provides two TB3 (USB-C) ports on the
rear, each supporting the full 40 Gb/s throughput, DisplayPort alt-mode, USB 3.1 Gen2 (10 Gb/s), and daisy-
chaining of up to 6 devices
. In addition, the iMac caters to legacy peripherals with four USB-A 3.0
ports (5 Gb/s each), an SDXC card slot, and a Gigabit Ethernet RJ-45 port
. The TB3 ports on iMac can

of legacy ports: the MBP offers no built-in USB-A, SD card, or Ethernet. Professional users must use
adapters or docks (e.g. USB-C to USB-A or SD readers) for those needs
. This design reflects Apple’s
aggressive adoption of USB-C and reliance on wireless alternatives, but it drew some criticism since “the port
selection has not changed – you’re still living the dongle life” for things like USB sticks or SD cards
. In
summary, the MacBook Pro’s wired connectivity is entirely Thunderbolt/USB-C centric, whereas the iMac
provides a broader mix of new and legacy ports – an important distinction in connectivity use-cases.
Use  of  Thunderbolt  for  Peripherals  and  Networking: Both  the  iMac  and  MacBook  Pro  exploit
Thunderbolt 3 for high-performance peripherals. External Thunderbolt RAID drives or NVMe SSD enclosures
can achieve transfer speeds in the 2–3 GB/s range, leveraging the 40 Gb/s (~5 GB/s) raw bandwidth – far
beyond the 5 Gb/s of the iMac’s internal Fusion Drive HDD portion. Professionals frequently use TB3 docks
or hubs to connect multiple devices: a single TB3 port can daisy-chain a 5K display, external GPU (eGPU),
audio interface, and 10 Gb Ethernet adapter together. In fact, Apple officially supported eGPUs starting in
macOS High Sierra 10.13.4 (2018) for TB3 Macs. Both the 2017 iMac and 2019 MBP can connect to an
external  AMD  Radeon  eGPU  chassis  to  accelerate  graphics  for  tasks  like  3D  rendering  or  driving  VR
headsets. Apple’s Metal API and apps like Final Cut Pro utilize eGPU horsepower when available. Another
capability of Thunderbolt 3 is peer-to-peer networking: two Macs can be connected directly via a TB3 cable
for a 10 Gigabit Ethernet (10 GbE) network link
. This is useful for fast data migration or clustering
machines – effectively treating the TB cable as a virtual 10 Gb network interface. Both example Macs
support this “Thunderbolt Networking” feature (the MacBook Pro even has TB3 on both sides, offering
flexibility in cabling). Finally, Thunderbolt 3’s ability to carry DisplayPort makes it the default way to connect
external monitors on these Macs. The 2017 iMac’s TB3 ports double as outputs for second displays, and the
MacBook Pro uses its TB3/USB-C ports for all video output (with USB-C–to–DisplayPort/HDMI dongles as
needed). In sum, Thunderbolt 3 in the 2017–2020 Apple lineup represents a unification of connectivity –
combining PCIe, USB, display, and power – that enabled new classes of peripherals (eGPUs, one-cable
docks) and simplified multi-monitor setups
.

This “Gigabit-class” Wi-Fi was critical for Apple’s ecosystem features like AirDrop and iCloud syncing to feel

machines support personal hotspot tethering to an iPhone via Wi-Fi or Thunderbolt, providing flexibility for
internet access on the go.
During 2017–2020, the newer  802.11ax Wi-Fi (Wi‑Fi 6) standard emerged, promising up to 40% higher
throughput, lower latency, and better performance in dense environments. Notably, Apple introduced
Wi‑Fi 6 in the iPhone 11 (2019) and iPad Pro (2020), but no Intel-based Mac through 2020 included Wi‑Fi 6
support
. The first Macs to get 802.11ax were the Apple Silicon M1 models released in late 2020
.
Thus, our iMac and MBP (being Intel-based) are limited to Wi‑Fi 5. In practical terms, this means their
wireless speed and range are very similar to one another. The iMac’s larger chassis may confer some RF
advantage (potentially housing higher-gain antennas), whereas the MacBook Pro’s all-metal body and
portability mean it might have slightly less Wi-Fi range. Nonetheless, both can comfortably exceed 500 Mb/s
under ideal conditions on 5 GHz 802.11ac networks – more than sufficient for tasks like high-bitrate video
streaming or fast Time Machine backups to a network drive. It’s worth noting that Apple’s implementation
of 802.11ac also leverages beamforming and other features of the standard to improve reliability. 
Apple Wireless Direct Link (AWDL): In addition to infrastructure Wi-Fi, Apple devices in this era make
heavy use of a proprietary peer-to-peer Wi-Fi protocol called AWDL. AWDL is a mesh-capable ad-hoc protocol
built on top of Wi-Fi that allows devices to discover and communicate with each other directly (without an
access point)
. It underpins features like AirDrop, AirPlay peer-to-peer, and GameKit networking.
AWDL works by channel hopping: even while a Mac or iPhone is connected to a normal Wi-Fi network, the
wireless chipset can rapidly switch to a special “social” channel to send discovery broadcasts and then jump
to a designated channel for direct data transfer between peers
. This happens in the background, fast
enough that the user’s main Wi-Fi connectivity is not disrupted beyond a minor performance impact
.
Both the 2017 iMac and 2019 MBP support AWDL through their Broadcom (or similar) Wi-Fi chipsets. For
instance, if one wants to AirDrop a large file from the MacBook Pro to the iMac, the two will use Bluetooth
Low Energy to locate each other, then seamlessly create an AWDL link over Wi-Fi for the file transfer (more
on AirDrop below). The advantage of AWDL is that it doesn’t require the devices to be on the same Wi-Fi
network – they can form a private, high-speed link anywhere, even in a field with no router
. Apple’s
use of AWDL in this period was pioneering, although it is a proprietary protocol. (It was later revealed that
regulatory pressure in some regions might push Apple to adopt the open Wi-Fi Aware standard instead of
AWDL in the future, to ensure interoperability
.)
Bluetooth Classic and 5.0: Alongside Wi-Fi, Apple continued to update Bluetooth capabilities in Macs for
short-range connectivity. The 2017 iMac comes with  Bluetooth 4.2, an update over BT 4.0 that offers
improved speed (Bluetooth 4.2 supports up to ~1 Mb/s for Low Energy data) and privacy features
.
Bluetooth is crucial for connecting input devices (the iMac’s included Magic Keyboard and Magic Mouse 2
use BT), as well as Continuity features like Handoff and Universal Clipboard (which use BLE advertisements).

would stem more from antenna design than protocol, since both are using the same 802.11ac standard. For
critical tasks, both machines could fall back to wired Gigabit Ethernet (the iMac directly, the MBP via an
adapter). By 2020, users anticipating Wi‑Fi 6 had to wait for the Apple Silicon Macs – an important note for
“future-proofing,” as Apple did not refresh Intel Macs with Wi‑Fi 6 at all
. Thus, in an enterprise or home
setup with a new Wi‑Fi 6 router, these 2017–2019 Macs would operate at 802.11ac speeds (albeit still very
fast). 
Peer-to-Peer Sharing: AirDrop and AirPlay
One of the hallmark interoperability features in Apple’s ecosystem is AirDrop, introduced on Mac in 2011
and on iOS in 2013. AirDrop allows quick, ad-hoc file transfer between nearby Apple devices (Mac, iPhone,
iPad) using a combination of Bluetooth and Wi-Fi. By the time of macOS High Sierra / iOS 11 (circa 2017),
AirDrop had evolved into a seamless cross-platform protocol leveraging Apple’s AWDL technology. Here is
how it works technically:
Discovery: When  AirDrop  is  enabled,  a  device  advertises  itself  via  Bluetooth  Low  Energy.  For
example, the 2019 MacBook Pro will periodically send BLE advertisement packets that include
information (encrypted) about the device. Nearby Apple devices pick up these adverts. Bluetooth
provides a power-efficient way to discover devices in proximity (within ~30 ft). On iOS, starting
iOS 13, an ultra-wideband chip (U1) can augment this discovery with directional awareness (though
Macs in 2019 didn’t have U1, only iPhones did). The 2017 iMac can similarly see nearby AirDrop-
enabled devices via BLE, despite being a desktop – this allows an iMac to appear in the AirDrop share
sheet on an iPhone, for instance.
Connection setup: Once two devices mutually discover each other via Bluetooth and determine
(through  exchanged  info)  that  they  both  support  AirDrop  and  that  the  user  permits  receiving
(contacts or everyone), they initiate a direct Wi-Fi link. This is where AWDL comes in. The devices
coordinate to create a peer-to-peer Wi-Fi session on a channel that both set aside for AirDrop
.
They switch their Wi-Fi radios to that channel, establish a link-local IPv6 network between them, and
then switch back and forth as needed (if they also remain on an infrastructure network)
. They

Data transfer: The sending device (say, the MacBook Pro) then streams the file data directly over the
Wi-Fi link to the receiver (the iMac). Because this uses Wi-Fi (802.11ac) with no internet bottleneck,
the transfer speeds can be very high – often on the order of tens of megabytes per second. In ideal
cases, AirDrop can send a multi-gigabyte video file in under a minute or two. There is effectively no
fixed file size limit (Apple doesn’t specify a limit, though extremely large transfers may falter due to
timing out or memory usage)
. Both of our example Macs, with 802.11ac 3×3 radios, can achieve
top AirDrop throughput when talking to each other. Users have reported AirDrop sending, say, a
1 GB file in ~30 seconds under good conditions – vastly outpacing legacy methods like Bluetooth file
transfer or copying via USB 2.0 stick.
Security and privacy: AirDrop has a few security layers. First, as noted, all transfers are encrypted
(TLS). Second, if the receiving device is set to “Contacts Only” mode, an authentication occurs where
the devices exchange hashed identifiers derived from the users’ contact info to verify they know each
other. Importantly, researchers in 2019 identified that during this handshake, devices send out a
SHA-256 hash of their owner’s phone number and email to each other to check for a match in
contacts
. While the hashes are not plaintext, a determined attacker could potentially brute-force
phone numbers or small email dictionaries to find a match, raising privacy concerns. Apple has since
provided warnings about this and in iOS 16.2 introduced a time limit for “Everyone” mode (to
mitigate unwanted sharing). But in our 2017–2020 timeframe, users needed to be aware that
AirDrop  being  set  to  Everyone  could  allow  unknown  devices  to  send  prompts,  and  the
aforementioned hash exposure was a theoretical risk if an attacker was sniffing BLE traffic. On the
whole, however, AirDrop’s security model – requiring acceptance for each file and using encrypted
transfer – kept casual usage safe
.
Protocol  evolution: Initially,  AirDrop  on  Mac  (OS   X  Lion  through  Mavericks)  was  a  separate
implementation incompatible with iOS AirDrop. In 2014, Apple unified the protocol: since OS X
Yosemite, Macs use the same AirDrop protocol as iPhones
. Thus our iMac and MBP can AirDrop
with iPhones (running iOS 8+). There was a “legacy mode” on older Macs to still transfer to pre-2012
Macs over an ad-hoc Wi-Fi (without BLE), but by macOS Mojave in 2018 that legacy mode was
removed
. Both the 2017 iMac and 2019 MBP, being new machines, simply use the modern
BLE+AWDL AirDrop method.
In practical ecosystem workflows, AirDrop became a go-to method for quick sharing: e.g. a user editing
photos on the MacBook Pro could AirDrop selected images to the iMac for further editing on a larger screen
(or vice-versa). The peer-to-peer link is typically faster than uploading to iCloud Drive and redownloading,
and doesn’t require any cables or intermediate devices. This emphasizes Apple’s strategy of making local
connectivity effortless and fast, encouraging users with multiple Apple devices to use them synergistically. 
Another peer-to-peer feature is  AirPlay (Mirroring and direct streaming). In our timeframe, a Mac could
AirPlay its screen or media to an Apple TV or AirPlay-enabled speaker. This usually runs over a Wi-Fi network
(using Bonjour for discovery and then RTSP streaming). However, Apple TVs from 2015 onward support
peer-to-peer AirPlay, which again uses BLE for discovery and AWDL for streaming if no common Wi-Fi is

Apple Continuity and Inter-Device Integration
Beyond raw network connections, Apple’s ecosystem is distinguished by Continuity – a suite of features
that allow multiple Apple devices to act as an integrated workflow. Between 2017 and 2020, Apple expanded
Continuity with new capabilities like Universal Clipboard (2016), Auto Unlock with Apple Watch (2016), and
Sidecar (2019). These features typically require a mix of Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, and iCloud connectivity to function.
They are designed to be seamless for the user, leveraging whatever connection is available (often BLE and
AWDL behind the scenes) to link devices. Our 2017 iMac and 2019 MacBook Pro, running modern macOS
(e.g. Ventura in 2023, which they both support), benefit from the full Continuity feature set available in that
era.
Some key Continuity features and how they work:
Handoff: Arguably the cornerstone of Continuity, Handoff lets you start a task on one device and
continue on another. For instance, you could start writing an email on the MacBook Pro, then pick up
your iPhone and swipe up to instantly continue composing that email in iOS Mail. Technically, when
you have a Handoff-capable app open (Mail, Safari, Pages, etc.), the device advertises a Handoff BLE
signal containing an encrypted “activity” token. Another device logged into the same iCloud account
picks up the signal and queries iCloud to authenticate and obtain the handoff data (like the URL or
document state)
. The transfer of context might occur via an iCloud handshake or directly over
Wi-Fi if both are on the same network. Both example Macs support Handoff (all Macs 2012 and
newer do)
. So the iMac 5K and MBP can handoff documents or web browsing sessions to each
other or to an iPhone/iPad. They use Bluetooth to know they’re near each other and internet (or peer
Wi-Fi) to send the actual data. Handoff was extended in 2020 to include FaceTime calls (continuity of
calls) and even media playing (Handoff music from iPhone to HomePod, etc.), but during 2017–2019
it mostly covered app activities. The presence of Bluetooth 4.2 vs 5.0 isn’t really noticeable for
Handoff – both work as long as devices are in range. 
Universal Clipboard: Introduced with macOS Sierra and iOS 10, this feature allows you to copy on
one device and paste on another. For example, on the iMac you copy a snippet of text or an image,
and then on the MacBook Pro you can simply hit paste and the content appears. Universal Clipboard
transfers the clipboard data securely over Bluetooth/Wi-Fi between devices logged into the same

Auto Unlock with Apple Watch: If the user has an Apple Watch, their Mac (iMac or MacBook) can
automatically unlock when the Watch is nearby and authenticated. This feature, added in 2016, uses
Bluetooth proximity to detect the watch and then a Wi-Fi direct connection to quickly exchange

authentication (the watch must be on the user’s wrist, unlocked, and recently authenticated by
passcode or unlock of iPhone). The Mac senses the watch’s presence at wake via BLE and then uses a
Wi-Fi session (or internet) to validate credentials with Apple’s servers, finally unlocking the Mac
almost instantly.
Phone and Messaging Continuity: With an iPhone on the same network, the iMac and MacBook
Pro can make and receive cellular calls (routing through the iPhone) and send/receive SMS texts (via
the Text Message Forwarding feature). This works via Wi-Fi or wired network: the Mac and iPhone
must be on the same LAN (or use AWDL if no router). When a call comes to the iPhone, it uses
Bonjour over Wi-Fi to alert the Mac, which then uses the iPhone as a VOIP relay. Both example Macs
fully support this (requirement: iPhone on iOS 8.1+ and Mac on Yosemite+ on same network)
. It
effectively turns the Mac into a speakerphone for the iPhone. Similarly, an SMS sent to your phone
can appear in the Mac’s Messages app and you can reply from the Mac – the iPhone will forward the
SMS. These features cement the multi-device workflow: one can stay on the Mac for communication
instead of constantly picking up the phone.
Sidecar: A major addition in macOS Catalina (2019) was Sidecar, which allows a modern iPad to act
as a secondary display (and drawing tablet) for a Mac. The 2019 MacBook Pro and 2017 iMac both
meet the supported hardware list for Sidecar (which requires relatively recent models and OS
Catalina+)
. Sidecar can work wired (via USB cable) or wirelessly. Wirelessly, it again leverages a
direct point-to-point Wi-Fi connection (likely AWDL or a form of Wi-Fi Direct) between Mac and iPad,
with Bluetooth used to initiate/handshake
. This is essentially Apple’s answer to products like
Duet Display but built-in and optimized. The MacBook Pro 16″, being quite powerful, can drive the
iPad at 60 fps as an external display with minimal lag, and supports the Apple Pencil input from the
iPad as an input to Mac apps. The iMac 2017, while slightly older, also supports Sidecar (Apple’s
criteria included iMac late-2015 5K or newer)
. Using Sidecar, an artist could use the iPad Pro as a
drawing tablet for the iMac, or a user could have an iPad as a portable second monitor for the
MacBook  Pro  when  traveling.  It’s  a  prime  example  of  Apple-specific  inter-device  integration
leveraging wireless connectivity – effectively streaming an interactive display over Wi-Fi.

Overall, these Continuity features are enabled by a combination of technologies: iCloud (to tie devices to
one user and sync some data), Bluetooth LE (for proximity and triggers), Wi-Fi (for bulk data or direct
connections), and increasingly the T2/secure enclave for authentication. Both the 2017 iMac and 2019
MacBook Pro are peers in this ecosystem – meaning neither is left out. They can interchange roles freely
(e.g., either one can be the source or target of a Handoff or AirDrop). The main difference is the MacBook
Pro, with a T2 chip, supports “Hey Siri” hands-free (the T2’s always-on audio processor listens for the Siri
trigger). The iMac 2017, lacking T2, did not initially support always-listening Siri until macOS Catalina
allowed using “Hey Siri” if an iPhone/HomePod was nearby. Also, the MacBook’s Touch Bar (if relevant) could
be  considered  an  integration  with  Touch   ID  –  but  that’s  internal  to  the  device.  From  an  ecosystem
perspective, what stands out is how these Macs can act as extensions of the user’s other Apple devices. For
instance, one could start editing a document on the MacBook Pro on the go, then come home and use
Handoff or iCloud Drive to seamlessly continue on the iMac’s big screen and keyboard. They could copy text
from a website on the iMac and paste it into a note on the MacBook. If the user gets a phone call, all their
devices ring and they can pick it up on the MacBook’s microphone. Such continuity makes the sum of
owning multiple Apple products greater than the parts – a strategic advantage Apple has cultivated.
Technically, the tight synchronization relies on those connectivity advances (fast Wi-Fi, BLE broadcasts, etc.)
and  sophisticated  software  coordination  (e.g.,  the  Continuity  frameworks  introduced  in  iOS   8/OS   X
Yosemite).
System Architecture, Security, and Deployment Considerations
During 2017–2020, Apple made significant changes under-the-hood that affected how Macs interface with
operating systems and peripherals. A major development was the introduction of the Apple T2 Security
Chip (Apple’s custom ARM co-processor) in Mac models. The 2019 16″ MacBook Pro features a T2 chip (as
do all 2018–2019 MacBook Pros), whereas the 2017 iMac does not have a T2 (the T2 debuted in the iMac Pro
later in 2017, but the standard iMac retained the traditional architecture). This difference has implications
for security, I/O processing, and deployment workflows:
T2 Chip and I/O: The T2 in the MacBook Pro acts as a controller for several hardware functions: it’s
essentially an embedded computer running a variant of iOS (“bridgeOS”) that handles the SSD controller,
Touch ID sensor, FaceTime HD camera processing, audio codec, and the Secure Enclave for cryptographic
operations. From a connectivity standpoint, one notable aspect is that the T2 presents the internal SSD as
an encrypted APFS volume to the main Intel CPU – providing on-the-fly encryption/decryption with almost
no performance loss. It also means that removing the SSD (which on T2 laptops is actually soldered, not

removable) would not let you read the data without the T2. The T2 also can block certain ports or boot
options for security. For example, by default the T2’s  Startup Security Utility disallows booting from
external media
. This is a stark change from earlier Macs: on the 2017 iMac, one can plug in an external
USB or Thunderbolt drive and boot from it freely (or netboot from a network server) as long as you have the
right boot selection. On the MBP 2019, unless the user goes into Recovery and lowers the security settings,
the  T2  will  refuse  to  boot  from  an  external  disk  or  network
.  This  affects  workflows  like  macOS
installation and troubleshooting – for instance, deploying a lab of 2019 MacBook Pros via NetBoot/Imaging
is not possible by default, whereas the 2017 iMac can still use older NetInstall/DeployStudio workflows
.
Apple’s intention with T2 was to enforce Secure Boot: by default the T2 in Full Security mode will only boot a
legit, signed copy of macOS and only from the internal disk (which it cryptographically seals)
. This
improves security (no Evil Maid attacks via USB stick) but did “kill off” the traditional monolithic imaging
deployment  method  in  IT  departments
.  Instead,  institutions  had  to  transition  to  Apple’s  Device
Enrollment Program (DEP) or use Internet Recovery installation. The 2017 iMac, lacking T2, can still be
imaged or booted from external media without such hurdles.
Deployment & Target Disk Mode: Another related feature is  Target Disk Mode, historically used to
connect a Mac as an external drive to another Mac (via FireWire or Thunderbolt). The iMac 2017 supports
Thunderbolt Target Disk Mode – you could connect it to, say, the MacBook Pro with a TB3 cable, restart the
iMac in Target Disk Mode, and it would appear as an external volume on the MBP for data transfer or even
booting. On T2 Macs, Target Disk Mode behavior changed into “Share Disk” in the macOS Recovery, where
the T2 Mac can expose its internal drive over USB or Thunderbolt but only after authenticating in Recovery.
The MBP 16 can do a form of target disk mode, but because of Secure Enclave tied to the disk, you might
need to input the password before it will share. Apple also introduced a special DFU mode on T2: if the T2
firmware is corrupted, the MacBook Pro can be plugged into another Mac running Apple Configurator to
revive the T2. The iMac 2017 doesn’t have this complexity – it has a standard EFI firmware that can be
revived  through  a  normal  USB  restore  if  needed,  and  no  second  computer/Configurator  workflow  is
necessary. From an IT support viewpoint, the iMac was simpler to recover (Internet Recovery or netboot
imaging),  whereas  the  T2-equipped  MacBook  required  new  procedures  (internet  recovery  with  T2
verification, etc.). Indeed, by 2019 Apple was steering customers toward using  Internet Recovery and
MDM to deploy Macs. Imaging an OS onto the MBP 2019 would require first booting to Recovery and
disabling secure boot or allowing external boot, which is an extra manual step
. In contrast, one could
network-boot 20 iMacs and blast an image to them (though Apple didn’t “support” it officially anymore, it
was still technically possible).
Security Benefits: The T2 chip brings strong security benefits to the MacBook Pro. It has a Secure Enclave
that  stores  Touch  ID  data  and  encryption  keys.  FileVault  disk  encryption  is  automatic  and  hardware-
accelerated. The T2 also enforces that the OS hasn’t been tampered with (it will only boot a macOS signed
by Apple – in fact, in Full Security, the T2 will personalize the macOS installer to that specific Mac using the
unique chip ID
). This means the MBP’s system integrity at boot time is extremely high. By contrast,
the iMac 2017 relies on the older firmware password and EFI Secure Boot which was much less rigorous
(Apple did not implement UEFI Secure Boot on most Intel Macs except via the T2). The iMac can boot
unsigned OSes (Linux, Windows via BootCamp, etc.) freely. The MBP 2019 can boot Windows via Boot Camp,
but even Windows had to be signed by Microsoft in a way Apple’s T2 would accept (and Boot Camp Assistant
configures the T2 to allow it)
. Booting Linux or other OS on a T2 Mac required setting the T2 to Reduced
Security or No Security mode in the utility
. So Apple drew a line in the sand: post-2018 Macs would be
much  more  appliance-like  and  secure  by  default,  whereas  2017  and  prior  were  more  open  but  less
protected. This is something enterprises had to adapt to – e.g., Firmware updates: on the iMac, firmware

Legacy Device Integration: The iMac, with more ports, can interface with older devices directly – for

be imaged en masse by plugging into a switch. The MBP, if an institution wanted to netboot it (for, say,
diagnostics), simply cannot netboot at all in a traditional sense due to the T2’s restrictions
. They would
instead have to use Internet Recovery or bring up macOS and then use management tools. This was a
paradigm shift in Mac deployment between 2017 and 2019: imaging was effectively deprecated by Apple
by the time of the MBP 16″
. Organizations had to adapt with workflows like creating a bootstrap
package and using DEP/MDM for zero-touch setup, rather than clone images.
In everyday use for a consumer, these differences are subtle – the MBP may prompt for Touch ID or Watch
to unlock, the iMac always requires a password or Watch. The MBP might be slightly more locked down if
trying to boot alternative OS. But both are extremely capable macOS machines and part of the same
ecosystem. The iMac’s lack of T2 doesn’t impede any consumer features (Continuity, etc., all work); it’s mainly
security and future OS implications. 
To summarize, in the 2017–2020 window the iMac 5K (2017) represents the last generation of Mac that
used  only  standard  PC  architectures (Intel  CPU  +  legacy  EFI  +  software-based  security),  while  the
MacBook  Pro  (2019)  showcases  Apple’s  transition  to  integrated  security  and  control  chips  (T2).
Connectivity-wise,  the  T2  doesn’t  change  external  I/O  speeds  (both  have  TB3  40   Gb/s),  but  it  does
intermediate some of those connections (the SSD, camera, etc. go through T2). For IT deployment and
multi-device management, the MBP needed new strategies (no netboot, require MDM) whereas the iMac
could be managed with older techniques. Apple’s ecosystem benefited from T2 in terms of user experience

Peripheral Compatibility and Expansion
Both the 2017 iMac and 2019 MacBook Pro support a wide array of external peripherals, though the types
of peripherals and how they connect differ due to port configurations:
Displays: The iMac 5K has an internal high-resolution display and can additionally drive external monitors
via Thunderbolt 3/DisplayPort. As noted, it supports one 5K external or two 4K externals
. These would
typically be connected using a USB-C to DisplayPort cable or an Apple Thunderbolt 3 to Thunderbolt 2
adapter (for older Thunderbolt displays). Many users took advantage of the iMac’s TB3 ports to hook up an
LG UltraFine 5K display (co-developed by Apple) which connects via a single TB3 cable. The 2019 MacBook
Pro, lacking an internal 5K panel but with a very capable GPU, is often seen driving multi-monitor setups. It
can output to dual 6K displays – for instance, two Pro Display XDRs at full 6016×3384 resolution
 – a
capability only the highest-end Intel Macs of that era had. This makes the MBP 16″ suitable for advanced
workflows like video editing on multiple reference monitors. Both machines support use of  Docks: a
Thunderbolt  3  dock  can  be  attached  to  provide  additional  HDMI  ports,  USB-A  ports,  etc.,  which  can
particularly  benefit  the  MacBook  Pro  (e.g.  a  CalDigit  TS3  dock  can  turn  one  TB3  port  into  multiple
connections). The iMac, already having many ports, might use a dock mainly to add extra display outputs or
faster UHS-II SD card readers, etc.
One thing the iMac cannot do is act as an external display for another Mac (“Target Display Mode” was only
supported on iMacs up to 2014 and not with Retina 5K iMacs). So you cannot plug the MacBook Pro into the
2017 iMac and use the iMac as a monitor – a feature some earlier iMacs had via Thunderbolt Target Display.
Apple removed that once displays went 5K (citing technical limitations). In 2020+, Apple introduced Sidecar
and later Universal Control to let an iMac and MacBook share inputs or screens in a way, but that’s not a
direct monitor connection. So in our scenario, the iMac and MBP are more likely to use Sidecar with an iPad
or external monitors than to directly combine with each other’s display.
External GPUs: Both Macs, thanks to Thunderbolt 3, support external GPU enclosures (eGPU) using PCIe
bandwidth over TB3. Apple’s macOS High Sierra and Mojave provided official support for AMD GPU eGPUs.
A 2017 iMac user might use an eGPU to supplement the internal Radeon Pro 575/580, for example, adding
a Vega 64 in an enclosure to accelerate VR or improve gaming performance. The 2019 MBP with an already
powerful Radeon Pro 5500M could still benefit from a desktop-class GPU (like a Radeon 5700XT in eGPU) for
tasks like driving multiple 4K game engines or speeding up certain OpenCL/Metal computations. Both
machines connect to eGPUs via a TB3 cable; the performance is slightly limited by the 40 Gb/s (~< 4 GB/s
after  overhead)  of  Thunderbolt,  but  still  very  useful.  Apple  even  sold  a  Blackmagic  eGPU  which  was
commonly paired with MacBook Pros. The iMac being static might be less in need of an eGPU (since it has
discrete graphics and one can opt for higher-tier GPU in BTO), but it’s still a viable upgrade path, especially
as new GPUs came out after 2017. The MacBook Pro, being non-upgradable internally, relies on eGPU if the
user needs more graphics horsepower down the line. This demonstrates how Thunderbolt 3 expands the

convenient for photographers. The MBP 16″ notably lacks an SD slot (Apple brought it back in the 2021
models after feedback)
. So an SD card from a camera would require a USB-C SD reader on the MBP,
whereas the iMac accepts it directly. This difference is frequently cited by photographers – the 2019 MBP
not having SD slot was a pain point.

the iMac’s TB3 can adapt to Thunderbolt 2 (for an old Thunderbolt Display or a FireWire RAID via TB2->FW

storage target devices). Neither machine has optical drives, but one can use a USB external DVD drive on
both (works via the iMac’s USB-A or the MBP’s USB-C-with-dongle). Bluetooth 4.2 vs 5.0 doesn’t affect
accessory compatibility – a Bluetooth 5.0 device like newer BLE sensors or a BLE stylus will still work with the

.  So  practically,  all  Bluetooth

Conclusion
The 2017–2020 period was pivotal for Apple’s hardware connectivity – blending the old and new. The 2017
iMac 5K embodies a bridge between generations: it introduced the high-bandwidth Thunderbolt 3/USB-C
standard to the iMac line while retaining familiar ports like USB-A and SD card, thus servicing both cutting-
edge and legacy peripherals in a desktop built for power users. In contrast, the 2019 16-inch MacBook Pro
represents  Apple’s  uncompromising  vision  of  the  future:  a  streamlined  port  selection  (four  versatile
Thunderbolt 3 ports) and a reliance on wireless and adaptors for everything else, underpinned by the T2
security/control  chip.  Despite  these  differences,  both  machines  leverage  common  connectivity
technologies – Thunderbolt 3, 802.11ac Wi-Fi, and Bluetooth LE – to interoperate seamlessly within the
Apple ecosystem.
From  a  Thunderbolt  and  bandwidth perspective,  both  the  iMac  and  MacBook  Pro  deliver  40   Gb/s
connectivity that in 2017–2019 was state-of-the-art in the PC industry, enabling workflows with 5K/6K
displays, ultra-fast external SSDs, and network speeds rivaling wired servers
. The analysis showed
that  Thunderbolt   3’s  adoption  (and  USB-C  connector)  was  a  unifying  step  –  one  cable  could  handle
monitors, data, and charging – especially beneficial for the laptop. The iMac’s use of TB3 mainly adds
expansion  (more  displays,  faster  externals)  to  a  stationary  setup,  whereas  the  MBP’s  use  of  TB3  is
fundamental (it only has TB3 for I/O, aside from audio). This highlights how Apple tailored connectivity to
device roles: the desktop got a complementary mix, the pro laptop went all-in on a single modern standard.
In  wireless connectivity, both devices exemplify Apple’s commitment to robust Wi-Fi and personal area
networking. Sticking with 802.11ac through 2020 ensured solid Gigabit wireless performance for tasks like
Time Machine backups to AirPort base stations or streaming to Apple TV. Bluetooth 4.2 vs 5.0 differences
aside, both Macs fully support Apple’s BLE-driven Continuity framework, which is crucial for multi-device
experiences
. We discussed how AirDrop, as a peer-to-peer service riding on AWDL, enables rapid file
sharing – essentially treating ad-hoc Wi-Fi as a quick, on-demand personal network
. In an office
scenario, one could AirDrop files from the MacBook Pro to the iMac far faster than using a USB stick, thanks
to the direct 5 GHz link. Similarly, Sidecar and Handoff leverage the tight wireless integration between
macOS and iOS: our MacBook Pro could extend its display to an iPad Pro for on-the-go dual-screen work,
then back at the desk, the iMac could pick up the same project via Handoff and allow the user to continue
with a full keyboard and mouse. This fluidity is a product of meticulous integration: the devices share
context through iCloud and connect through BLE/Wi-Fi without user friction
. 
One can observe how Apple’s ecosystem ethos – “It just works” – is underpinned by these technical
capabilities. For example, Auto Unlock uses the Watch’s presence to unlock the Mac, saving time daily;
Universal Clipboard and phone call relay blur the lines between devices, making two Macs (or a Mac and
iPhone)  feel  like  one  cohesive  environment
.  The  2017  iMac  and  2019  MBP,  despite  hardware
differences, both serve as first-class citizens in this ecosystem. Indeed, a user in 2020 with both machines
could use them interchangeably: iCloud Drive syncs files, AirDrop shuttles one-off data, and Continuity
features sync clipboard, messages, and more in real-time. The notable differences (like the MBP’s Touch ID)

macOS, iOS, watchOS together), and convergence of security and functionality (the T2 chip blending roles of
many controllers). The 2017 iMac and 2019 MacBook Pro show these trends in action, each with distinct
strengths yet working in tandem. Apple achieved an enviable balance where a user could focus on creative
workflow rather than file transfer or compatibility issues – a direct result of the high-bandwidth, low-friction
technologies described in this analysis. As Apple moved past 2020 into Apple Silicon Macs, many of these
connectivity foundations remained (Thunderbolt 3 evolved to TB4, Wi-Fi 6 became standard, Bluetooth 5.x
continued,  Continuity  expanded  further  with  Universal  Control,  etc.),  proving  that  the  2017–2020  era
successfully set the stage for the next generation of Apple’s interconnected ecosystem.
