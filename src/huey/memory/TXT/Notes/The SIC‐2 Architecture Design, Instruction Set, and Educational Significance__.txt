The SIC‐2 Architecture: Design, Instruction Set,
and Educational Significance
Introduction and Historical Context
The Simplified Instructional Computer (SIC) is a hypothetical computer architecture introduced by Leland L.
Beck in his textbook “System Software: An Introduction to Systems Programming.” Beck developed SIC to strip
away the complexities of modern processors and provide a clear, accessible model for teaching system
software concepts
. The original SIC (sometimes called  SIC Standard Model) includes only the most
essential  hardware  features  common  to  real  machines,  making  it  easier  for  students  to  learn  how
assemblers, linkers, and operating systems interact with computer architecture
. In response to the
need for a more capable yet still educational platform, Beck also defined an enhanced version of this
machine. This enhanced architecture – referred to here as SIC 2 (and known in Beck’s text as SIC/XE, for
“eXtended Equipment” or “Extra Expensive”) – builds upon the standard SIC by adding significant features
and resources while maintaining backward compatibility
. Object programs assembled for the original
SIC can execute correctly on SIC 2 (SIC/XE), demonstrating full upward compatibility between the two
versions
.
The creation of SIC and SIC 2 was motivated by educational needs. By the late 20th century, real CPUs had
grown complex – with pipelines, caches, microcode, and elaborate instruction sets – making them less ideal
for beginner-level system programming exercises. Beck’s SIC architecture abstracts away those complexities
in favor of clarity
. The later SIC 2 variant introduces more advanced architectural concepts (such as
additional addressing modes, registers, and I/O mechanisms) in a controlled manner, allowing students to
transition into more realistic system-level programming without the full complexity of a commercial CPU.
Historically, SIC (first appearing in Beck’s work around the 1980s) and SIC 2 have been widely adopted in
academia  as  a  sandbox  for  teaching  assembly  language,  compiler  design,  and  operating  system
fundamentals.  These  architectures  are  not  implemented  in  silicon  commercially,  but  they  have  been
brought to life in simulators and even FPGA-based experimental “chips” in academic projects
. In the
sections that follow, we provide an in-depth exploration of the SIC 2 architecture – covering its processor
design,  instruction  set,  addressing  modes,  execution  model,  memory  and  I/O  organization  –  and  we
highlight the enhancements of SIC 2 over the original SIC. We also discuss the role of SIC 2 in computer
architecture education, its use in simulation environments, and how it has been implemented in emulators
for teaching and research.

Overall Architecture of SIC and SIC 2
Processor and Registers: Both SIC and SIC 2 are single-accumulator, binary, stored-program machines with
a 24-bit word size. The processor of the standard SIC includes five core registers, each 24 bits long
.
These are: 
A (Accumulator, reg# 0): General-purpose accumulator used for arithmetic/logical operations
.
Many instructions implicitly use register A for data manipulation (e.g. arithmetic instructions take an
operand from memory and combine it with A, leaving the result in A). 
X (Index Register, reg# 1): Used for indexed addressing; it holds an index value that can be added
to addresses for data access (useful for array traversal, loop indexing, etc.)
. X can also serve as a
general counter or offset register in programs. 
L (Linkage Register, reg# 2): Used for subroutine linkage. The Jump to Subroutine instruction (JSUB)
saves the return address (the next instruction’s address) in L, and the Return Subroutine instruction
(RSUB) jumps back to the address in L
. In absence of a hardware stack, L provides a mechanism
for subroutine returns; however, this means recursive or reentrant calls require software conventions
to save/restore L if needed. 
PC (Program Counter, reg# 8): Holds the address of the next instruction to fetch
. The PC is
automatically updated as instructions are fetched and can be altered by branch/jump instructions. It
functions like the instruction pointer in real CPUs, enabling sequential execution flow through
memory. 
SW (Status Word, reg# 9): A status register containing condition codes and other status flags
. In
SIC, the SW’s primary defined field is the Condition Code (CC), which reflects the result of the most
recent comparison or test (indicating “<”, “=”, or “>” condition)
. The CC is used by conditional
jump instructions (like JLT, JEQ, JGT) to determine program flow
. In SIC 2, the SW register may be
expanded to include additional flags or mode bits (for example, some educational implementations
include bits for supervisor/user mode, interrupt masks, and an interrupt code field to simulate basic
OS support
). These additional status bits are not heavily used in Beck’s original SIC examples
aside from the CC, but they provide hooks for implementing operating system features (e.g.,
privilege levels or simple interrupt handling) in more advanced educational exercises.
SIC 2 extends the register set to include several new registers, reflecting features found in more advanced
machines
:
B (Base Register, reg# 3): Introduced in SIC 2 as a dedicated base pointer for addressing
. B
enables base-relative addressing (described later) and allows the program to access memory beyond
the reach of 12-bit displacements by using a register as a segment base. It essentially serves as a
relocation register for one segment of memory, a concept similar to base registers in real-world
architectures. 
S (General Register, reg# 4) and T (General Register, reg# 5): Two additional 24-bit general-
purpose registers with no special hardware-defined usage
. These can be used by the
programmer (or compiler) for any needed purpose. S and T increase the CPU’s general register pool,
making SIC 2 a more versatile 24-bit two’s-complement machine (contrasting with the minimalistic 1-
accumulator design of the original SIC). For instance, S and T can hold intermediate values, loop
counters, or serve as base addresses for data structures if the programmer chooses. 
F (Floating-Point Accumulator, reg# 6): A 48-bit register for floating-point arithmetic
. This
register (present only in SIC 2) enables the machine to handle a new numeric data type: 48-bit

It should be noted that the register numbers (codes) for PC, SW, and the new registers are not consecutive;
PC and SW remain identified by numeric codes 8 and 9, respectively, preserving their special roles
.
Meanwhile, B, S, T, and F take the numeric codes 3, 4, 5, and 6
. This scheme ensures that none of the
new registers conflict with the original ones. In assembly language, these registers are referred to by
mnemonic (A, X, L, B, S, T, F, etc.), but their numeric identifiers are used in machine code and in certain
instruction formats.
Memory Organization and Data Representation: Memory in both SIC and SIC 2 is a flat, byte-addressable
array of bytes, with no separate I/O or specialty memory areas (unless defined by software conventions). On
the standard SIC, the address space is  15 bits, allowing for 2^15 =  32,768 bytes of memory
. Each
memory address refers to a single 8-bit byte. Groupings of three consecutive bytes form a 24-bit  word,
which is the natural word size of the machine
. Words are aligned on any byte boundary (i.e., word
addresses are defined by the lowest-numbered byte of the three-byte group)
. Because addresses are
byte-oriented, incrementing an address by 1 moves to the next byte in memory, and reading a word from
address N will fetch bytes at addresses N, N+1, and N+2 as a combined 24-bit value. By convention, these
three bytes are typically interpreted in  big-endian style (the byte at the lowest address is the high-order
portion of the word) – this is implied by the definition that the “lowest numbered byte” is used as the word’s
address
, similar to many mainframe and RISC architectures.
In SIC, memory is limited to 32K bytes and addresses wrap around within that range. SIC 2 dramatically
expands memory capacity to 20-bit addresses, giving a maximum of 2^20 = 1,048,576 bytes (1 MiB) of
memory
. This expansion mirrors real trends in computing (where later architectures broke the 16-bit
address barrier into 20-bit or 24-bit ranges). The larger memory necessitated new instruction formats and
addressing modes, which we detail below. Aside from size, memory in SIC 2 remains byte-addressable and
word-oriented in the same manner as SIC; the fundamental memory structure is unchanged
. Both
versions treat memory as a linear address space with no hardware-enforced partition between code and
data. This means, for educational purposes, one can load instructions and data anywhere in memory
(subject to program design), and the architecture doesn’t impose protection or segmentation – those
concepts can be introduced at a higher level if needed.
The data formats supported by SIC are deliberately minimal. The machine operates primarily on integer
and character data: 
Integer numbers are 24-bit binary values, stored in a word (3 bytes). SIC uses standard two’s
complement representation for signed integers, allowing both positive and negative values in the
range –2^23 to 2^23–1
. There is no separate support for larger integers or for packed binary-
coded decimals – only basic 24-bit binary arithmetic is directly provided by hardware. 
Character data uses the ASCII encoding, with one character per byte (8 bits)
. This choice reflects
common practice and ensures that SIC’s memory can represent text as well as numbers. Since a
word is 3 bytes, up to three ASCII characters can fit in one word, though typical usage would treat
character strings as byte arrays.

Floating-point numbers are not supported in the standard SIC; any needed real-number
calculations would have to be done in software (e.g., fixed-point or via simulation). This was a
simplification to avoid dealing with complex floating hardware in the basic model
. 
SIC 2 introduces a  48-bit floating-point format to broaden the range of applications and to simulate a
common feature of real machines
. The floating-point representation in SIC 2 is a custom 48-bit format
consisting of a 1-bit sign, an 11-bit exponent, and a 36-bit fraction (mantissa)
. The exponent is stored
with a bias of 1024 (i.e., exponents are in the range 0–2047, corresponding to actual exponents of –1024
through +1023)
. The fraction is a normalized fractional part (with an implied leading 1 for non-zero
numbers, similar to IEEE normalization, though the base is 2). In effect, a floating-point number’s value is: 
For example, an F-register value with sign 0, exponent 1024, fraction 0 represents +1.0; an exponent of
1024+10 with a given mantissa represents that number ×2^10, etc. This format allows SIC 2 to handle a
reasonable range of real numbers and perform floating arithmetic operations in hardware. The inclusion of
floating-point in SIC 2 is a pedagogical step to expose students to FP operations and representation issues
(e.g., precision and bias) without having to adopt the full IEEE 754 standard. In summary, SIC 2 supports
integers and characters identically to SIC
, but adds a new numeric type (48-bit float) with corresponding
instructions and the dedicated F register for computation.
Word Alignment and Endianness: The architecture does not impose strict alignment rules for words – any
address can be the start of a word, though naturally word-aligned addresses (divisible by 3) are convenient.
Given the educational nature, alignment issues are typically glossed over. Endianness, as mentioned, is
effectively big-endian for word access (address points to the high-order byte). To illustrate: if the word value
0x123456  is  stored  at  memory  address  1000,  then  memory[1000]=0x12,  memory[1001]=0x34,
memory[1002]=0x56 (in hex). If the CPU loads a word from address 1000 into register A, A will contain the
value 0x123456. This scheme is consistent across SIC and SIC 2.
Instruction Set and Addressing Modes
Instruction Formats: All SIC instructions are encoded in a fixed 24-bit format (3 bytes per instruction)
.
The basic format can be visualized as follows: 
Opcode: 8 bits 
X (index flag): 1 bit 
Address: 15 bits 
In the standard SIC, the 8-bit opcode identifies the operation (e.g., LOAD, ADD, JUMP, etc.), and the 15-bit
address field provides the operand’s memory address. The single X flag bit (the 24th bit of the instruction)
indicates indexed addressing; if x=1, the machine will add the contents of register X to the given address to
compute the effective address (this supports simple index-based memory access)
. If x=0, the address
field is taken as an absolute address (direct addressing). Thus, the standard SIC has two addressing modes
for memory operands: direct and indexed, distinguished by the X bit
. The target address (TA) calculation
in SIC is straightforward: 
If x = 0: TA = address (the 15-bit address field zero-extended to 24 bits). 
If x = 1: TA = address + (X) (the content of index register X added to the address field)
.

All memory-reference instructions in SIC follow this format. (We note that not all SIC instructions have a
memory operand; a few, like RSUB, ignore the address field, but they still occupy the same 24-bit format in
memory. RSUB simply sets PC ← L to return from a subroutine, and its address field is typically filled with
zeros by the assembler.)
SIC 2 greatly expands the variety of instruction formats to accommodate new types of instructions (register-
to-register operations, larger addresses, etc.)
. SIC 2 defines four instruction formats: Format 1, 2, 3,
and 4
. A summary of these formats is given in  Table 1 below, which also shows how the 24-bit SIC
format relates to the new format3/4 in SIC 2.
Table 1: SIC 2 Instruction Formats. Format 3 corresponds to the original SIC’s 24-bit format extended with flag bits,
and Format 4 is an extended 32-bit format for full 20-bit addressing
.
Format 1: A 1-byte instruction containing only an 8-bit opcode
. Format1 instructions have no
operand fields; the opcode alone completely specifies the operation. This format is used for simple, 
implied-address operations – typically machine control instructions or other operations that don’t
need a separate operand. For example, in SIC 2, the FLOAT (convert integer in A to floating in F) or FIX
(convert F to integer in A) instructions, if they existed, could be Format1. More concretely, the I/O
channel control instructions (HIO, SIO, TIO described later) are defined as Format1 in SIC 2
,
since they use an implied operand (like the device or channel number in register A) and thus can be
encoded solely by an opcode byte. 
Format 2: A 2-byte instruction, consisting of an 8-bit opcode and two 4-bit operand fields (often
interpreted as register identifiers)
. Format2 is used for instructions that involve register-to-
register operations or other operations with small operand specifiers. In SIC 2, this format is
employed by instructions such as ADDR (add registers, e.g., ADDR R1,R2  adds the contents of R2
into R1), SUBR, MULR, DIVR (register-register arithmetic), as well as COMPR (compare registers) and
data movement like RMO (“Register Move”, copy one register’s contents to another). Each of these
instructions has two register operands encoded in the single extra byte (the first 4-bit field is R1,
second 4-bit is R2)
. Another group of Format2 instructions are those that operate on a
register and a constant nibble; for instance, TIXR (an instruction to increment index and compare to
a register) might be defined to use one register operand and one unused 4-bit field. In summary,
Format2 handles all operations that deal primarily with registers rather than memory. 
Format 3: A 3-byte instruction format that extends the original SIC format with additional flag bits. In
format3, the instruction layout is: 6-bit opcode, followed by six 1-bit flags, then a 12-bit displacement
field
. The total is 24 bits (3 bytes). The six flags (bits n, i, x, b, p, e) introduce powerful
addressing mode features (detailed below). Format3 instructions are used for most memory-
reference operations in SIC 2 when the target address can be expressed in 12 bits relative to some
base or the PC. The 6-bit opcode space supports up to 64 opcodes, which is sufficient because many
of the original SIC opcodes can be represented within that (the original had on the order of 25–30
distinct operations). Notably, by using 6 bits for the opcode, SIC 2 repurposes what used to be bits of
the opcode in SIC for the new addressing mode flags. This clever encoding allows backward
compatibility: legacy SIC instructions (which all had opcodes ending in binary 00 ) can be treated as
format3 with the flags implicitly indicating simple addressing. (In fact, original SIC code can be viewed
as format3 with the n,i flags both 0, which SIC 2 interprets as the same direct addressing semantics
as old SIC
. We will explain n,i flags shortly.) The 12-bit displacement field in format3 is
interpreted as a signed integer when used with PC-relative addressing (allowing jumps or references
±2048 bytes from the current PC) or as an unsigned offset when used with base-relative addressing

. Format3 is the workhorse format for most typical
instructions on SIC 2, as it offers a compact encoding for local jumps and data access while
supporting multiple addressing modes. 
Format 4: A 4-byte instruction (32 bits) that is essentially an augmented version of format3. It has
the same layout of fields except that the address field is 20 bits long instead of 12 (and the e flag bit
is set to 1 to indicate format4)
. Specifically: 6-bit opcode, n,i,x,b,p,e flags, and a 20-bit address.
Format4 provides extended addressing: the full 20-bit address allows direct addressing of the entire 1
MB memory space of SIC 2 without relying on base or PC relative modes
. In assembly
language, format4 instructions are often denoted by a plus sign prefix (e.g., +LDT #40980  might
produce a format4 instruction to Load T with an immediate large constant). Format4 is used when a
target address is out of reach for format3’s 12-bit displacement or when the programmer explicitly
wants an absolute address. Because it occupies an extra byte, format4 instructions should be used
judiciously – the assembler or compiler might choose format3 by default and only use format4 when
necessary to optimize code density, mimicking the idea of long and short instruction forms. All
memory-reference opcodes that can be format3 have a corresponding format4 form (distinguished
only by the e bit in machine code).
With four formats, SIC 2’s instruction set achieves flexibility similar to real-world architectures like IBM
System/360 (which also had 3- and 4-byte instructions for different addressing needs). Table 1 above
illustrates the bit allocations in each format
. Formats 1 and 2 are used for instructions that do not
reference memory, whereas formats 3 and 4 are used for instructions that do reference memory (with the
extended mode bit e distinguishing the 3-byte vs 4-byte variant)
.
Addressing Modes in SIC 2: The addition of new flag bits in format3/4 gives SIC 2 a rich set of addressing
modes beyond the basic direct and indexed modes of SIC. The six flag bits are: n (indirect), i (immediate), x
(indexed),  b (base-relative),  p (PC-relative),  and  e (extended  format  indicator)
.  By  setting
combinations of these bits, the programmer can specify how the effective address of an operand is
calculated. The roles of these flags are summarized below:
n (Indirect) and i (Immediate) flags: These two bits determine the fundamental addressing method
for the operand, akin to the addressing mode bits on some CISC machines. In SIC 2, there are three
primary cases: immediate, indirect, and simple/direct. If i=1 and n=0, the instruction uses Immediate
addressing – the operand value is taken immediately from the address field itself, rather than from
memory
. In other words, the 12- or 20-bit address field is not an address at all, but an actual
constant value (e.g., LDA #5  loads the literal value 5 into A, using immediate mode). The assembler
indicates immediate mode by a  #  prefix on the operand. If  n=1 and i=0, the instruction uses
Indirect addressing – the address field gives the  location of the real operand address
. The
machine will first fetch the word at the given address (plus indexing/base adjustments, if applicable)
to obtain the actual target address, then fetch the operand from that target address. Indirect mode
is indicated in assembly with an  @  prefix (e.g.,  LDA @BUFFER  means load A from the memory
location  stored  in  the  address  labeled  BUFFER).  Finally,  if  n=1  and  i=1 (both  bits  set),  this  is
interpreted as Simple direct addressing in SIC 2
. In this case, the operand is taken directly
from  the  effective  address  calculated  (with  base/PC/index  applied  as  needed).  This  “n=i=1”
combination yields the same behavior as the original SIC’s direct addressing. The assembler by
default produces n=1,i=1 for an operand written without #  or @  (unless it’s an address that fits the
original SIC’s legacy pattern; but generally in SIC/XE assembly, unmarked operands are treated as

original SIC object code, all opcodes had their two low bits 00 (which correspond to n=0,i=0 when
interpreted  in  SIC   2  format3).  This  combination  is  treated  as  “SIC  compatibility  mode” or
sometimes called  implied simple addressing
. Practically, n=0,i=0 is rarely generated by SIC 2
assemblers for normal instructions (since direct mode would be n=i=1), but the SIC 2 hardware
considers n=0,i=0 as equivalent to n=1,i=1 (direct) for operand fetching
. This ensures that
object code assembled under the old SIC rules (where those bits were essentially not used and
remained 0) will still execute correctly, maintaining upward compatibility
. In addition, a few
SIC 2 instructions use n=0,i=0 intentionally to denote no operand at all (for example, the machine
control instructions like SVC or the channel I/O instructions might be represented with n=0,i=0 and
no address, which effectively means the operand is “implied” by the instruction). 
x (Indexed) flag: This functions similarly to the original SIC’s X-bit. If x=1, the content of register X is
added to the computed address (after considering immediate/indirect and before the final memory
reference)
. Indexed addressing can be combined with any of the above modes except where
it doesn’t logically apply (for example, indexing an immediate operand doesn’t make sense, and
indeed  in  SIC   2  the  assembler  will  not  allow  #  or  @  on  the  same  operand  as  ,X ).  The
architecture simply defines that if x=1 and the addressing mode is  direct or base/PC relative, X is
added to the target address; if the addressing mode is immediate (n=0,i=1), the X flag is ignored or
considered invalid (immediate values are not addresses). In summary, Indexed addressing in SIC 2 is
an option that can augment direct, indirect, or base/PC-relative addressing (just as original SIC
allowed indexing on its direct mode)
. This is useful for accessing array elements or iterating
through structures. 
b (Base) and p (Program Counter) flags: These two flags control relative addressing modes for
the 12-bit displacement field in format3 instructions. They are mutually exclusive in most normal
situations (it wouldn’t make sense to have both b and p = 1 at the same time). If p=1 (and b=0), the
12-bit displacement is interpreted as a signed offset relative to the current PC value (specifically, the
PC after incrementing past the current instruction)
. This is  PC-relative addressing, which
allows efficient encoding of jumps or data references that are near the current instruction. The range
is –2048 to +2047, since 12 bits with sign can represent offsets in that range. If b=1 (and p=0), the
12-bit displacement is taken as an unsigned offset added to the content of the Base register B
.  This  is  Base-relative  addressing.  Base-relative  mode  requires  that  the  programmer  (or
compiler/linker) load the B register with a suitable base address (often the start of a data area or the
beginning of the program) before using B-relative instructions. The offset can range from 0 to 4095,
covering any address in a 4KB window defined by B. Base-relative addressing is extremely useful for
accessing static data or larger program ranges in SIC 2’s expanded memory – by changing the base
register, code can conveniently access different memory regions without needing 20-bit absolute
addresses in each instruction. Only one of p or b is typically set for a given instruction: if b=0 and
p=0, then no base register or PC relative addressing is used, and the address field is interpreted as
an absolute address (direct addressing as in SIC, but now limited to 12-bit magnitude in format3)
. This case allows accessing the lowest 0–4095 memory range directly. If b=1 and p=1 were to
occur, it’s generally undefined in normal usage (assemblers wouldn’t produce that combination); we
can assume the architecture would not use both simultaneously – one might imagine a scenario of
p=1, b=1 being reserved or illegal. In practice, the assembler sets b or p depending on how it can
resolve a given target address during code generation.

e (Extended) flag: This flag is set to 1 for Format4 (4-byte) instructions and 0 for Format3 (3-byte)
. When e=1, the address field is 20 bits long and no PC-relative or base-relative displacement is
used (the b and p bits are treated as part of the 20-bit address in format4). In other words, in
format4, the instruction always uses  direct addressing with a full 20-bit address, whether that
address is target of a jump or a data reference. The n,i flags still apply (immediate vs indirect vs
direct), and x can still be used to add indexing, but b and p are not meaningful in format4 (in fact,
the hardware might simply ignore b,p when e=1, or assemblers enforce b=0, p=0 in that case).
Format4 extended mode is typically employed for long jumps or references that cannot be encoded
in 12 bits. For instance, +JSUB PROCLONG  might generate a format4 instruction if the subroutine
PROCLONG is far away in memory; this would allow a direct 20-bit jump. It comes at the cost of one
extra byte in the instruction stream. All format4 instructions are explicitly indicated in assembly
(commonly with a  +  as mentioned), ensuring the assembler sets e=1 appropriately. Format4
effectively gives an Absolute addressing mode spanning the full 1 MB address space
, whereas
format3 relies on either base or PC relative to reach the higher addresses if possible.
Combining these flags yields the full set of addressing capabilities of SIC 2. To summarize the addressing
modes available on SIC 2 (and how they compare to SIC):
Direct (Absolute) Addressing: The simplest mode; the operand’s address is explicitly stated (either
in the 15-bit field for SIC or in the 12/20-bit field for SIC 2). In SIC 2 format3, this corresponds to
n=1,i=1 with b=0,p=0 (and e=0) so that the 12-bit address is taken as absolute
. In format4, direct
addressing is the only option (e=1 gives a 20-bit absolute address)
. Example: LDA ITEM  (no
special symbols) on SIC 2 might use PC-relative if ITEM is close, but if forced to absolute: 
+LDA ITEM  would ensure a format4 absolute address. In SIC (original), all addressing was
effectively direct unless X was used for indexing. 
Indexed Addressing: Not a separate mode by itself, but an option on top of direct or relative modes.
In SIC, indexed was the only modification available (x flag)
. In SIC 2, indexing (,X) can be applied
to direct, base, or PC-relative modes (but not to immediate or indirect). The effective address
becomes computed_address + X. For example, LDA ARRAY,X  will take the address of ARRAY, add X’s
content, and load A from that location. This is akin to using X as an array index register. 
Immediate Addressing: Operand is a literal constant embedded in the instruction (n=0,i=1). SIC did
not support this mode (all SIC instructions that needed constants had to load from memory, or use a
trick with LDB if one existed – but since SIC has no immediate, typically one would have to place
constants in memory). SIC 2’s immediate mode is a major convenience: e.g., LDA #100  loads the
value 100 into A without any memory reference besides the instruction itself
. This is useful for
initializing registers or using small constants in arithmetic. The operand field is interpreted as an
unsigned value (for the sake of assembly syntax, usually written as decimal or hex with a # ). If the
constant doesn’t fit in 12 bits, the assembler might automatically choose format4 to supply a 20-bit
immediate, or else load it from a literal pool. 
Indirect Addressing: Operand is accessed through a pointer. The address field gives the location
where the actual target address is stored (n=1,i=0). SIC lacked this mode; pointers had to be handled
by manual two-step loads (load address into a register, then use register-indirect via an indexed
trick). In SIC 2, LDA @PTR  will go to memory at address “PTR”, fetch the 3-byte value there as the
real address, then load A from that real address
. Indirect addressing allows dynamic data
structures like linked lists or jump tables in a more straightforward way, and is another feature
typical of real hardware (similar to “indirect” mode on PDP-11 or x86’s pointer follows).

PC-Relative Addressing: A mode where the address field is interpreted as a displacement added to
the program counter (n=1,i=1, p=1, b=0, e=0)
. This is new in SIC 2 and improves code
relocatability and density. For example, if a data variable is within 2KB of the instruction, the
assembler can use PC-relative mode to refer to it, avoiding the need for a full 20-bit address or use
of base register. The hardware will take the 12-bit displacement, sign-extend it to 24 bits, and add to
the content of PC (which at that moment points to the next instruction). If the displacement is
positive and small, this effectively encodes a forward reference; if negative (two’s complement), it
encodes a backward reference. Jump instructions heavily use PC-relative mode: e.g., JEQ LOOP  can
be assembled to a PC-relative jump if LOOP is within ±2048 bytes, setting p=1 to encode the branch
offset. This mode contributes to position-independent code principles and is analogous to the
relative branches on many architectures (e.g., x86 short jumps or ARM PC-relative addressing). 
Base-Relative Addressing: Uses the base register B as a reference (n=1,i=1, b=1, p=0, e=0)
. This
mode is particularly useful for managing a larger program or data space. A common strategy is to
load B with the start address of a data segment or the beginning of the program. Then instructions
can reference any location in that 4K region via a 12-bit offset. For example, if B holds the address of
an array ARR , an instruction LDA ARR  might be assembled as base-relative (b=1) with disp = 0,
meaning load A from B+0 (the start of ARR). Another data element 100 bytes into ARR could be
referenced with disp=100. Base relative addressing thus provides a way to handle program
relocation: if the program is loaded at a different address, the loader can simply set B accordingly,
and all base-relative references automatically adjust. In educational OS projects, B might also serve
as a general purpose base register for multi-segment memory management. 
Extended (Format4) Addressing: When e=1, the address is a full 20-bit absolute address, enabling
direct addressing anywhere in the 1MB space. While not a separate addressing “mode” per se (it’s
basically absolute addressing with a longer field), it’s often discussed as an Extended mode. In
assembly, instructions that require the extended format are marked (e.g., writing +JSUB X  forces a
4-byte JSUB). Extended format is needed if neither PC-relative nor base-relative can reach a target
address – for instance, a far subroutine call or a far memory operand beyond the current 4K window.
The trade-off is the instruction is one byte longer and slightly less efficient. 
Additionally, SIC 2 retains the concept of implied addressing for certain instructions. Implied mode means
the instruction implicitly operates on a fixed resource without any operand field. For example, RSUB in SIC
(opcode 0x4C) has no operand; it always returns to L. In SIC 2, instructions like HIO, SIO, TIO (halt I/O, start
I/O, test I/O channel) do not take an operand in the instruction – they implicitly refer to the device or
channel specified in the A register. These would be encoded as Format1 instructions (just the opcode) and
are  documented  as  having  “implied”  addressing  of  A  (or  of  an  internal  channel  register).  The  SIC  2
assembler might forbid writing an operand for these. Another implied instruction example is a hypothetical
clear register instruction, which might just specify the register in the opcode or format2 but no memory
address.

Arithmetic and Logical Instructions: SIC supports basic integer arithmetic using register A. ADD (0x18)
adds a word from memory to A (A ← A + [memory]), SUB (0x1C) subtracts, MUL (0x20) multiplies,

A. Secondly, SIC 2 includes register-to-register arithmetic: ADDR (Add Register) and SUBR, MULR,

first operand register)

and  TIX and  TIXR we discuss under control flow. Logical instructions beyond AND/OR are not
explicitly extended, but with more registers, one can do bit manipulations via register operations as
well.

another) into PC if it were allowed (PC isn’t directly targetable by RMO in standard definitions
though). Another likely addition is a  SVC (supervisor call, sometimes opcode 0xF0 or similar) to
request operating system services – if Beck included it in SIC/XE, it would trap to a monitor. However,
since not explicitly in the reference, we’ll omit speculation. The main improvement in control flow for
SIC 2 is the ability to use base/PC relative addressing for branches (which eliminates the need for full
20-bit addresses for near jumps) and the ability to position code in the 1MB space freely with the
help of base register or extended jumps for far calls.
Input/Output Operations: Input/Output in SIC is handled by three dedicated instructions that perform
device-level I/O one byte at a time
. Each device is assigned a unique 8-bit code (like an
identifier  for  a  specific  I/O  device  or  peripheral)
.  The  CPU  uses  these  instructions  in  a
programmed I/O manner (no DMA, no interrupts in the basic model unless one is simulating them
via SW bits):

byte

the device code is given in memory or as a constant). When executed, TD will check the status of that
device’s control register (simulated). If the device is ready (e.g., input available or output buffer free),
the condition code CC in SW is set to “<” (less) to indicate ready; if the device is not ready (busy), CC is
set to “=” (equal)
. (There is no “greater” for TD; presumably CC = ‘>’ is not used for I/O, or

assignment context. 
SIC 2 significantly enhances the I/O capabilities by introducing the concept of asynchronous I/O channels

I/O channels in mainframes). In SIC 2, three additional machine instructions are defined for channel control

SIO (Start I/O) – This instruction starts an I/O operation on the channel or device specified. The
operand (likely in A or in the instruction) identifies which device or channel to start. On execution,
SIO initiates the data transfer (read or write) on that device and then immediately returns control to
the CPU without waiting for completion. In other words, SIO launches an asynchronous I/O operation.
For example, SIO F1  (if F1 were a device code in A register) might start the tape drive reading data
into a buffer. SIO is analogous to a non-blocking I/O call. In SIC/XE, SIO is often a privileged
instruction (only allowed in supervisor mode, if mode bit is implemented). After an SIO, the CPU can
continue executing subsequent instructions while the device works in parallel. 
TIO (Test I/O) – This instruction tests the status of the I/O operation on a channel/device, similar to
TD but at the channel level. It will set the condition code CC to indicate if the I/O operation has
completed or if the device is still busy

not busy), and CC = ‘=’ means the I/O is still in progress (busy). The program can periodically execute
TIO in a loop to poll for I/O completion, enabling a simple form of concurrency: the CPU does other
tasks (or perhaps just loops) and checks back using TIO. This is effectively software polling; however,
one could also imagine an interrupt mechanism if we use the SW’s interrupt code bits.

Execution Model and Example: Under the hood, the SIC 2 execution model for I/O assumes a separate
device controller (or channel) state that operates asynchronously. In a simulation environment, one might
simulate a device as a concurrent process or thread that, once started by SIO, will after some delay mark
itself ready (so TIO can detect completion). This allows students to experiment with overlapped I/O and CPU
activity, and introduces the need for synchronization (polling or interrupts). Some SIC/XE simulators or
implementations also simulate interrupts: for example, an I/O device could generate an interrupt when
done, which would set some code in SW and potentially transfer control to a fixed interrupt handler
address.  The  SW’s  bits  (interrupt  code,  etc.  as  shown  by  GeeksforGeeks)  could  facilitate  that
.
However, Beck’s text does not delve deeply into writing interrupt-driven SIC programs – the typical usage is
to poll with TIO. Still, the hardware outline is rich enough to let advanced students consider implementing a
simple interrupt system if needed.
Execution Model and CPU Operation: The SIC and SIC 2 CPU follows a classic von Neumann fetch-decode-
execute cycle. The Program Counter (PC) points to the next instruction to fetch
. On each cycle, the CPU
fetches the byte (or bytes) of the instruction at the PC address, decodes the opcode and flags, and fetches
any additional bytes needed (since instructions can be 1, 2, 3, or 4 bytes in SIC 2). It then executes the
instruction’s operation, which may involve reading from memory, writing to memory, performing ALU
operations, or changing the PC or registers. After execution, the PC is normally advanced to point to the
next instruction (PC += instruction_length, which is 3 for format3, 4 for format4, etc., except in case of jumps
where PC is loaded with a new value)
. The machine does not implement pipelining; each instruction
is processed to completion before the next begins. Given the simplicity, one can think of it as a single-cycle-
per-byte or a multi-cycle (but sequential) architecture. In educational simulators, this is often visualized as
stepping through instructions one by one.
During execution of memory-reference instructions, the effective address (EA) is calculated considering the
flags: e.g., if immediate, EA is just (the value from address field); if indirect, the CPU will do an extra memory
fetch. If indexed, adds X. If base relative, adds B, etc. Once the EA or operand value is determined, the CPU
performs the operation (like add, compare, move, etc.). For store instructions, it writes to memory at EA; for
load, it reads from memory at EA. For immediate mode, it might not access memory at that EA at all but use

Execution Model and CPU Features
The execution model of SIC 2, as touched on, is sequential and fairly simple by design. Each instruction is
executed to completion before the next begins (there is no pipelining, out-of-order execution, or parallel
instruction issue). This simplicity makes it easier to reason about in software simulations and educational
settings – the outcome of each step is deterministic and can be stepped through easily in a debugger.
One can describe the CPU operation in terms of phases: Fetch the instruction at PC, Decode it (determine
format and opcode, identify if there is a target address to compute), Fetch Operands (if it’s a memory or
register  operand,  gather  the  data:  possibly  reading  memory,  or  reading  a  register,  or  resolving  an
immediate), Execute (perform the ALU operation or control action), and Write-back (store the result to a
register or memory if needed, and update PC for the next instruction). This is essentially a finite-state
machine cycle that can be microprogrammed.
The Status Word (SW) deserves a bit more elaboration in execution. We know CC is part of SW, and it’s
affected by COMP, TIX, TD/TIO results. In some implementations, SW may also hold a  mask of allowed
interrupts and a bit indicating the CPU’s mode (user or supervisor)
. For example, a student project or an
instructor might augment the SIC/XE specification such that bit 15 of SW is the interrupt mask, and bits 8–
11 encode an interrupt vector when an interrupt occurs
. If an I/O device finishes and interrupts, it could
place an code in SW (ICode bits [16–23] per GeeksforGeeks)
 and switch CPU to supervisor mode (mode
bit set to 1) to handle it. This goes beyond Beck’s initial scope but is a natural extension if one wants to
simulate an OS.
Interrupt Handling (Optional in SIC 2): If implemented, an interrupt would likely work as follows: When a
device completes and if interrupts are enabled, the CPU would automatically trap to a fixed location (say
address 0 or some predefined interrupt handler address), similar to how some simple architectures do. The
current PC and maybe SW could be saved (perhaps in reserved memory or at a memory stack if one is
simulated). The mode bit in SW would switch to supervisor mode, and the interrupt code field would
indicate which device or cause triggered it
. The OS handler would then service the interrupt (e.g.,
read the data or mark I/O complete), and issue an RFI (return from interrupt) which could be perhaps
implemented via an LPS (Load Processor Status) to restore SW including the mode and CC and a jump back.
But since Beck’s main focus was system software, not hardware interrupts, this is typically an advanced
topic possibly left as an exercise. More commonly, SIC programs explicitly check device status with TD/TIO.

Enhancements of SIC 2 over SIC (Summary of Improvements)
SIC 2 (SIC/XE) introduced a number of enhancements to overcome the limitations of the original SIC
architecture. Here we summarize the key improvements and their significance
:
Expanded Memory: The original SIC is limited to 32K bytes of memory, whereas SIC 2 expands the
addressable memory to 1 MB
. This is a jump from 15-bit to 20-bit addressing. The larger memory
enables more complex programs and larger data sets to be loaded, and it necessitated new
instruction formats (formats 3 and 4) to handle 20-bit addresses via base relative and extended
addressing. This makes SIC 2 capable of handling more realistic workloads and scenarios (e.g.,
multiprogramming with each program in a different region of memory). 
Additional Registers: SIC 2 adds four new registers (B, S, T, F) to the original five
. The extra
general-purpose registers (S, T) allow holding more intermediate values or pointers, reducing the
need to use memory for temporary storage. The Base register (B) unlocks new addressing modes
(base-relative) which improve code modularity and relocatability. The Floating-point accumulator (F)
introduces a new computation domain (real numbers). More registers also facilitate compiler
register allocation in hypothetical high-level language implementations targeting SIC 2, thus
bridging a gap toward modern register-rich architectures. 
Floating-Point Arithmetic: Absent in SIC, hardware floating-point operations become available in
SIC 2 via the 48-bit F register and corresponding opcodes (ADDF, SUBF, MULF, DIVF, etc.)
. This
enhancement allows students to experiment with non-integer arithmetic and understand the
complexity of floating-point representation (range and precision, exponent bias, etc.). It also
demonstrates how an architecture can be extended with a new data type in a backward-compatible
way (integer programs still run unchanged, but new code can utilize floats). 
Instruction Formats and Addressing Modes: SIC 2’s introduction of multiple instruction formats
(including shorter 1-byte and 2-byte instructions) and the suite of addressing mode bits (n,i,x,b,p,e) is
arguably the most significant enhancement. It brings in immediate and indirect addressing, PC-relative
and base-relative addressing, and extended format support
. These collectively make the
instruction set far more expressive. Programs can be more efficient – immediate mode avoids extra
memory loads for constants, PC-relative mode makes branching and data access position-
independent and compact, and base-relative mode allows program relocation and larger program
sizes without paying the cost of 4-byte addresses everywhere
. The extended format ensures that
even with these compact modes, the architecture can still reach any address in the larger memory
when necessary. All these modes were chosen to be upward-compatible with SIC’s original scheme:

Extended Instruction Set: In addition to new addressing modes, SIC 2 defines many new opcodes,
expanding the operation repertoire. We have already mentioned the floating-point and register-to-
register arithmetic instructions. Other additions include LPS (load processor status word), STI (store
instruction? possibly not, maybe this is a guess), and certainly the register move (RMO) and clear
and compare registers instructions. The machine control instructions – SIO, TIO, HIO – are new in
SIC 2 and greatly enhance I/O management
. There might also be SHIFT instructions (some
implementations of SIC/XE include SHIFTL/SHIFTR, but Beck’s core description did not emphasize
them; however, given format2’s free space, some assemblers define SHIFT opcodes). If included,
shift/rotate instructions would allow bit manipulation for algorithms like encryption, checksums, etc.,
more easily than relying on repeated additions. In summary, SIC 2’s instruction set is a superset of
SIC’s: everything SIC could do, SIC 2 does as well or better, and it adds dozens of new capabilities
making it comparable to a simple modern CPU. 
Parallel I/O and Device Support: By allowing asynchronous I/O operations (through SIO/TIO/HIO
and possible interrupt handling), SIC 2 moves beyond the purely sequential, CPU-bound I/O of SIC
. This models how real computers deal with slow I/O devices by overlapping computation and I/
O. In educational terms, this allows demonstration of device independence (you can start an I/O and
meanwhile compute something else) and teaches the need for synchronization (polling or interrupt
to know when I/O completes). It also implicitly introduces the concept of privileged operations – the
idea that not all programs should directly manipulate devices – if the mode bit in SW is used to
restrict these instructions to an OS. 
Upward Compatibility and Migration Path: An often overlooked but crucial enhancement is that
SIC 2 was designed to run SIC programs unchanged
. All original SIC instructions are still valid
in SIC 2, and their opcodes produce the same effects. The assembler for SIC 2 can accept SIC source
programs, and the machine will interpret them correctly (since n=0,i=0 for legacy opcodes is handled
as direct addressing mode). This meant instructors and students could start with SIC in early
chapters (covering basic assembler design, etc.), and then progress to SIC 2 (XE) in later chapters for
advanced topics, without discarding prior work. It also reflects how real ISAs evolve (new generations
maintain backward compatibility). Upward compatibility ensured that any educational tools
(assemblers, simulators) built for SIC could be extended to support SIC 2 gradually.
To encapsulate the improvements: SIC 2 transforms the simplistic SIC into a far more practical architecture
suitable for demonstrating real-world system software concepts. It bridges the gap between an overly
simple toy and a full-scale production architecture. Features like indexing, base registers, and PC-relative
addressing connect directly to concepts in assemblers (like addressing modes and program relocation)
.  Floating-point  and  additional  registers  connect  to  compiler/code  generation  topics  (how  to  use
registers efficiently, how floating point is handled). Asynchronous I/O and status flags connect to operating
system  design  (interrupts,  concurrency,  device  drivers).  In  sum,  SIC   2  is  a  deliberately  pedagogical
enhancement to the original SIC, incorporating lessons from real CPU evolutions while keeping the system
straightforward enough to grasp in a classroom setting.

Relevance in Education, Simulation, and Emulator Projects
The SIC and SIC 2 architectures have proven to be invaluable pedagogical tools in computer science and
engineering  education.  Their  design  strikes  an  effective  balance  between  simplicity  and  functionality,
making them ideal for illustrating core concepts of computer architecture and system software:
Computer Architecture & Organization Education: SIC provides a simplified model of a computer,
with clearly defined CPU, memory, and I/O components. By studying SIC (and SIC 2) architecture,
students  learn  about  the  basic  hardware  structure—how  memory  is  addressed,  how  registers
function, and how a CPU executes instructions
. The SIC 2 extension introduces students to more
sophisticated architectural features (like additional registers and addressing modes) without the
overwhelming  complexity  of  a  modern  x86  or  ARM  processor.  Educators  often  use  SIC  as  an
introductory example to explain how instructions are encoded and executed, how an addressing
mode works, or how a simple CPU datapath might be structured. Because the architecture is
completely  documented  and  hypothetical,  students  can  be  tasked  with  thought  exercises  like
modifying the instruction set or extending the architecture themselves, which encourages deep
understanding. 
Assembly Language Programming Practice: The SIC assembly language is straightforward and
small, making it an excellent first assembly language for students to learn. It includes just enough
instructions to perform typical programming tasks (loops, arithmetic, subroutine calls, I/O) so that
students can write meaningful programs (sorting a list, computing a formula, reading input/output)
without getting lost in syntax or peculiarities of a real assembly. The simplicity of SIC’s instructions
(especially in the standard version) helps beginners grasp how high-level constructs map to machine
operations. As they progress, SIC 2’s assembly exposes them to immediate values, base-relative
addressing for data structures, and other patterns common in assembly for real architectures. Many
courses have students implement projects like an  assembler for SIC/SIC 2 or write small assembly
routines to solidify their understanding
. The upward compatibility means student-written SIC
programs can later be run on a SIC 2 simulator to observe differences in performance or to integrate
with new instructions. Overall, SIC’s assembly is praised for being “simple and easy to understand,
making it a useful tool for teaching assembly language programming”
. Students can focus on
concepts like addressing modes and instruction effects rather than arcane assembler directives or
huge opcode sets.
System Software (Assemblers, Linkers) Implementation: Perhaps the most significant use of SIC/
SIC 2 is in system software courses, where students build components like assemblers, linkers, and
loaders. Beck’s textbook itself guides the reader through constructing a two-pass assembler for SIC/
XE as a case study. Because SIC has a well-defined instruction format and a manageable number of
opcodes, writing an assembler for it is feasible as a semester project. Students learn how to translate
assembly code (with labels, directives like BYTE/WORD, maybe even simple literals) into machine
code and how to manage symbol tables, addresses, and relocations. The presence of different
addressing modes and program relocation (using base relative and PC relative) directly ties into how
a relocating loader or linker must work – for example, resolving addresses that can’t be determined
until load time and generating relocation records for format4 addresses or immediate values beyond
range. Many open-source and academic projects have been done to implement SIC/XE assemblers in
various languages
, reflecting how common this educational exercise is. Similarly, writing a
loader that can load SIC object code (with modification records for relocated addresses) is another

Compiler and OS Prototyping: Although less common than assemblers, SIC 2 has been used in
teaching compilers and operating systems at a conceptual level. For compilers, one can use SIC 2 as
a  target  architecture  for  code  generation.  Since  SIC  2  has  features  like  registers  and  indexed
addressing, students can design simple code generation algorithms from a high-level language to
SIC 2 assembly
. It’s enough to illustrate concepts like expression translation, register allocation
(especially given limited registers, which is a realistic scenario), and calling conventions (handling
subroutine calls with L register, etc.). The small scale keeps the focus on the core ideas of compilation
rather than the quirks of a real ISA. For operating systems, SIC 2 can act as a sandbox where
students implement rudimentary OS functionalities such as scheduling, memory management, or I/
O drivers. For instance, because SIC 2 can simulate I/O interrupts and has a mode bit (in some
implementations), it’s possible to demonstrate  process switching: a user program doing I/O might
trigger  an  interrupt, the OS (running in supervisor  mode)  takes over, saves  context (registers,
including PC and SW), and switches to another task – all in a controlled environment. Students can
implement simple round-robin scheduling or memory allocation on a SIC/XE simulator, thus learning
OS concepts without needing an actual hardware platform. GeeksforGeeks notes that “the SIC's
simple architecture can be used as a basis for teaching operating system development” and that
students can learn about process management, memory management, and I/O management by
implementing  them  on  SIC
.  This  is  a  powerful  statement  –  it  means  that  despite  being  a
hypothetical machine, SIC is versatile enough to host a miniature OS, reinforcing concepts with
hands-on experimentation.
Simulation and Emulation Projects: The well-defined simplicity of SIC/SIC 2 also makes it a popular
target for simulation and emulation projects. Writing a SIC virtual machine (VM) or interpreter is a
common  exercise  that  solidifies  understanding  of  instruction  execution.  There  are  numerous
student-created and open-source SIC simulators (in C, C++, Java, Python, etc.) that emulate the SIC/
XE instruction set
. Such a project teaches how to implement fetch-decode-execute, handle
memory and I/O, and possibly how to debug an ISA implementation. It’s manageable in scope – one
can implement all instructions and a simple console I/O device in a few hundred lines – yet it’s
complex  enough  to  be  interesting  (especially  with  SIC   2  features  like  multiple  formats  and
addressing modes which make the decoding logic non-trivial). These simulators allow users to run
SIC assembly programs and observe the CPU state step-by-step, which is excellent for learning.
Additionally, some ambitious projects have involved implementing SIC/XE on hardware description
languages or FPGA boards
. For example, a diploma thesis in 2011 by Kloboves implemented a
SIC/XE processor on a Digilent FPGA, including device controllers and a connecting interface to a PC
. This demonstrates that SIC 2 is simple enough to be realized on an FPGA with limited
resources, and doing so teaches digital design, FSM control, and microarchitecture. The resulting
“SIC chip” on FPGA can execute SIC machine code natively at some clock speed, effectively turning
the hypothetical into a tangible, if simplified, CPU. The educational payoff is seeing how higher-level
language  code  (or  assembly)  translates  down  to  logic  gates  and  flip-flops  that  implement  the
architecture.  One  can  experiment  with  microprogramming  or  hardwired  control  using  the  SIC
design, and try modifications like pipeline it or adding cache as further exercises.

Community and Resources: Over the years, a community of instructors and students has built
around SIC/SIC 2. There are presentations, slides, and online tutorials explaining the architecture, as
well as solved examples and even debugging tools. The consistency of Beck’s textbook through
multiple editions means many people share similar assignments and solutions, creating a wealth of
reference material. For instance, the GeeksforGeeks articles
, slide decks on Slideshare
,
and various GitHub repositories all contribute to accessible learning resources for new generations
of students. This collaborative knowledge base underscores SIC 2’s relevance: it remains a go-to
example in system software curricula internationally
.
Conclusion: The SIC 2 chip (architecture) may be simple and hypothetical, but it encapsulates fundamental
principles of computer architecture in a form that is exceptionally conducive to teaching and exploration. Its
design  includes  just  enough  complexity  to  be  realistic  –  multiple  addressing  modes,  a  variety  of
instructions,  I/O  concurrency  –  while  being  minimal  enough  that  students  can  master  it  and  even
implement it themselves. In advanced courses, SIC 2 serves as a stepping stone: once students understand
how SIC 2 works, they can more easily appreciate the design decisions and challenges in real architectures
(like x86’s complexity or MIPS’s RISC approach), because they have built a mental model from the ground
up. In simulation environments, SIC 2 provides a playground for experimenting with system-level code
safely. And in hardware projects, it offers a blueprint of a small CPU that can be brought to life, demystifying
how machines execute the software we write.
In sum, SIC 2 has endured as a relevant educational architecture because it elegantly bridges theory and
practice. It was born out of a need to simplify and teach, and over time it has evolved (through SIC/XE and
various community extensions) to remain a comprehensive teaching tool. Whether in software simulation
or as an FPGA “chip,” SIC 2 continues to illuminate the workings of computer systems for students,
providing a foundation of knowledge that they carry into understanding real-world computing systems.
