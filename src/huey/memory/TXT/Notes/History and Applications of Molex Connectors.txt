History and Applications of Molex Connectors
Abstract
Molex connectors have played a pivotal role in the electronics industry for over half a century. This paper
presents  a  comprehensive  overview  of  Molex  –  from  the  company’s  origins  in  1938  through  the
development of its connector technologies – and examines the technical specifications and evolution of
these connectors over time. We discuss the widespread applications of Molex connectors across domains
such as robotics, consumer electronics, and the automotive industry, highlighting how their reliability and
standardization contributed to ubiquity in these fields. We also analyze why certain Molex connector types
(notably the four-pin power plug in PCs) have attained  legacy status, having been superseded by newer
standards in some contexts. Diagrams and figures are included to illustrate common Molex connector types
and their internal structures. Through an expert lens, this paper underscores the historical significance of
Molex’s innovations in connectivity, and how its connectors continue to impact modern engineering despite
the emergence of new technologies. All information is cited from reputable sources, and references are
provided in IEEE format to ensure accuracy and traceability.
Introduction
Molex Incorporated is one of the world’s leading manufacturers of electronic connectors, with a product
portfolio  of  over  100,000  interconnect  products  serving  industries  from  data  communications  and
consumer electronics to automotive and medical devices
. Founded in 1938 in Illinois, USA, the
company has grown over 80+ years into a global supplier and is consistently ranked among the top
connector  companies  by  annual  revenue
.  Molex  is  especially  renowned  for  pioneering  the  Molex
connector – a two-piece pin-and-socket plug introduced in the mid-20th century – which achieved universal
adoption in personal computers and other electronics
. Notably, the term “Molex connector” in common
usage often refers specifically to a 4-pin 5.25″ drive power connector used in PCs, though Molex as a
company produces a vast range of connector types
. This paper will clarify this terminology and
explore the breadth of Molex’s connector innovations.
We begin with a historical background of the Molex company and the development of its connector
technology. Next, a technical overview is provided, explaining the specifications of major Molex connector
families and how they evolved over time. We then delve into key application domains – including robotics,
consumer electronics, and automotive – to illustrate how Molex connectors are utilized in each, and how
they became standard solutions in those arenas. In a subsequent section on  Legacy Status, we discuss
certain Molex connectors that have become legacy interfaces in modern designs, examining the reasons
behind their decline or replacement in specific contexts. Finally, we conclude with a summary of Molex’s
enduring impact on connector technology. Throughout, an expert tone is maintained and claims are
supported with citations from datasheets, industry publications, and historical records.

Historical Background
Molex was established in 1938 by Frederick August Krehbiel as the Molex Products Company, originally
not as an electronics firm but as a manufacturer of plastic molding compounds and consumer items
. The company’s very name “Molex” comes from a proprietary plastic material developed by Krehbiel in
the 1930s – a composite of coal tar pitch, limestone, and asbestos fibers – which was found to be an
excellent electrical insulator
. In its early years, Molex used this material to produce household products
like flower pots, salt shakers, and clock cases
. By the 1940s, as World War II drove demand for plastics,
Molex’s insulating material gained attention because it was not subject to wartime rationing (unlike many
standard plastics)
. This positioned the company advantageously to explore new product lines in the
electrical domain.
Post-war, shifting to connectors: After WWII, under the leadership of Frederick’s son John H. Krehbiel,
Molex identified a growing market for electrical connectors. The company shifted focus from just molding
materials to designing interconnection devices, marking a pivotal transition from a materials company to a
connector company
. In 1953, Molex launched its first plug-and-receptacle connector line, targeting
the burgeoning consumer appliance industry
. These early connectors were low-cost terminal blocks and
simple pin-and-socket connectors that found success in home appliances such as refrigerators and washing
machines
. By 1960, Molex had introduced its first connectors made of nylon (rather than the original
Molex  plastic),  signifying  its  full  evolution  into  an  electronics  connector  manufacturer
.  This
development of nylon plug and receptacle lines in 1960 was critical – nylon’s durability and heat resistance
made it ideal for the increasingly complex electrical applications of the era.
Rise of the Molex connector in electronics: Molex’s breakthrough came in the late 1950s and 1960s by
supplying connectors to the fast-growing consumer electronics market. Notably, Molex developed pin-and-
socket connector designs for use in color televisions, which were then an emerging technology
. These
connectors, designed by John Krehbiel, linked electronic components inside TV sets and were adopted by
major TV manufacturers like Zenith, RCA, and Magnavox
.  Figure 1 provides an example of a Molex
standard  pin-and-socket  connector  assembly  from  that  period,  showing  the  male  and  female  plastic
housings alongside the removable crimp contact pins.
Figure 1: Components of a classic Molex pin-and-socket connector (Disk Drive Power Connection System). Shown
here are a 4-pin Molex connector  male housing (plug) and  female housing (receptacle), corresponding crimp

. The pin-and-
socket design, first patented by Molex in the late 1950s, features cylindrical metal pins that insert into spring-
metal sockets, all housed in a nylon shell
. This basic design became a de facto standard for power connectors
in many electronics.
By 1962, the demand for Molex’s connectors had grown the business substantially – the company’s annual
sales exceeded $1 million, driven largely by the widespread use of its connector products in consumer
electronics
.  Molex  earned  a  reputation  for  solving  challenging  design  problems  through  unique
interconnect solutions
. For example, the company developed  simple PCB connectors and stamped
circuitry, as well as the aforementioned nylon plugs and receptacles for color TVs
. In the 1960s and
70s,  Molex  also  introduced  progressively  smaller  connector  form  factors,  essentially  the  “micro”
connectors of their day, to keep pace with miniaturization trends in electronics
. This early emphasis on
innovation laid the groundwork for Molex to become a leader in connector miniaturization – a trend that
continues in its product development to this day.

Global expansion and growth: As its connector business expanded, Molex grew from a small Illinois
enterprise into a global company. It opened its first overseas factory in  Yamato City, Japan in 1970,
allowing it to better serve the rapidly growing Japanese electronics industry
. In 1972 Molex went public,
using the infusion of capital to accelerate growth in international markets
. Through the 1970s and
1980s,  Molex  further  broadened  its  product  lines  (entering  markets  like  automotive  and  computer
peripherals) and aggressively expanded manufacturing globally – by the early 1990s Molex operated more
than 40 plants in 19 countries
. This global footprint enabled close collaboration with diverse customers
and contributed to Molex’s emergence as (by 1993) the second-largest connector company worldwide
(trailing only AMP Incorporated)
. Key acquisitions also enhanced its capabilities; for instance, in 1980
Molex acquired Cardell Corporation, a maker of automotive connectors, strengthening Molex’s position in
the automotive connector market
.
21st century developments: In the 2000s, Molex continued to thrive by focusing on high-performance
connector solutions and niche innovations. The company invested heavily in R&D (routinely over 5% of
annual  revenue)  to  develop  new  connector  families  for  emerging  needs  such  as  high-speed  data
communication and mobile devices
. By 2013, Molex had over 45,000 employees and $3.6 billion in
annual  revenue
.  That  year,  the  Koch  Industries  conglomerate  acquired  Molex,  though  Molex
retained its name and focus on connectors. Today, after more than 80 years, Molex stands as a cornerstone
in the connector industry. It has built “a legacy of innovation” from its humble start with molded plastics to
modern high-density, high-speed connectors
. The historical trajectory of Molex is marked by continuous
adaptation to new technologies and markets, which is reflected in the wide array of connector types
bearing the Molex name.
Technical Overview of Molex Connectors
Molex connectors encompass a vast range of interconnect types, but many share common design principles
and have evolved along a few fundamental families. At their core, most Molex connectors are two-piece,
pin-and-socket systems: one half contains one or more metal pin contacts (often on the male or plug side)
and the mating half contains corresponding socket contacts (on the female or receptacle side). These
contacts are typically housed in molded plastic (originally cellulose-based plastic or nylon) shells that
provide insulation, alignment, and locking features
. This section outlines the technical characteristics
and evolution of Molex connectors, from the classic power plugs to modern miniaturized connectors.
Pin-and-socket  design: The  archetypal  Molex  connector,  as  developed  in  the  late  1950s,  features
cylindrical spring-metal pins that fit into matching spring-metal sockets
. Both pins and sockets are
held in a rectangular insulating housing, and the connectors are usually  polarized/keyed to prevent
incorrect orientation
. Early examples like the Molex  0.093-inch series used relatively large diameter
pins (~2.36 mm) capable of carrying about 8.5 A each
. These became popular for delivering power in
electronics due to their robust current capacity and simple crimp termination. Over time, smaller pin sizes
(such as 0.062″ or 0.084″ diameter) were introduced for lower-current or higher-density needs, with the
smallest in this family handling around 5 A per pin
.  Mechanical latching mechanisms were also
implemented: while some early connectors relied purely on friction fit to hold the halves together, later
designs added latch tabs or locking ramps to ensure a secure connection that would not accidentally
unplug
. For instance, many Molex housings have integrally molded snap latches or require an audible
“click” on mating, confirming proper engagement.

Despite widespread adoption, the classic friction-fit Molex connectors did have known issues. They could be
difficult to disconnect due to tight fit and lack of a release lever, often requiring significant force to unplug
.  Additionally,  if  the  female  socket  contacts  lost  tension  or  the  male  pins  were  misaligned,  the
connection could loosen over time, leading to contact resistance and electrical arcing (visible as browning
or melting of the nylon housing in severe cases)
. These issues spurred continuing refinement of
connector designs by Molex and others, focusing on better locking features and contact stability in later
generations.
Common Molex connector families: As electronics diversified, Molex developed numerous connector
series tailored to different pitches (pin spacings), current ratings, and environments. Some of the most
significant families include:
KK™ series (Modular Connectors): The KK series is a family of  low-power board-to-board and
wire-to-board connectors with a typical pitch of 2.54 mm (0.100″)
. Introduced in the 1970s,
Molex KK connectors are often seen as simple plastic housings that accept crimped tin-plated
terminals; they became a “building block” system that could be configured in various pin counts.
These are widely used for signal connections on PCBs, in everything from home appliances to
hobbyist electronics. The popularity of KK connectors led to many compatible clones – for example,
the ubiquitous 2.54 mm pitch plug and socket pairs found in PC motherboard front-panel headers
and Arduino-style kits are often Molex KK or compatible designs
. The KK family has variants
with friction ramps or latching features to secure the connection
. Its enduring presence (despite
newer alternatives) is due to the simplicity and standard 0.1″ spacing that matches PCB grid layouts.
Mini-Fit  Jr.®  series: This  is  a  family  of  medium-power  connectors with  a  pitch  of  4.20  mm,
featuring fully isolated rectangular pins and positive locking via latch tabs. Molex’s Mini-Fit Jr.,
introduced in the 1990s, is notable for being the connector system used in ATX PC power supply
cables (for example, the 20/24-pin main motherboard power connector is a 24-pin Mini-Fit Jr. plug)
. Each Mini-Fit Jr. contact can carry up to about 9 A with 16 AWG wire
, making it suitable
for delivering substantial power in a relatively compact form factor. The housings are polarized and
have locking clips to prevent accidental disconnection. In addition to PC power connectors, Mini-Fit Jr.
and its higher-power cousin Mini-Fit Sr. find use in industrial and automotive systems that require
rugged power connectors. The widespread adoption of Mini-Fit Jr. in computer hardware (both by
Molex and equivalent connectors from competitors) underscores how a Molex design became an
industry standard for modular power delivery
.
Disk Drive Power connectors (Molex 8981 series): Among Molex’s most famous products is the 4-
pin power plug used historically for computer disk drives and peripherals (often just called “the
Molex connector” by PC enthusiasts). This connector, originating in the late 1970s, has a 5.08 mm
pitch with 0.093″ diameter contacts providing 5 V and 12 V DC power and ground
. Interestingly,
the first versions of this connector used in PCs were based on the  AMP/Tyco Mate-N-Lok series
introduced in 1963
. Molex later produced a compatible version (Series 8981, trademarked as
the  Disk  Drive  Power  Connection  System)  in  1983
.  The  standard  configuration  is  a  4-pin
arrangement in a row, with a friction fit (no latch) – a design that proved very reliable for decades of
computer hardware
.  Figure 2 shows a close-up of this connector type in use, along with the
crimp terminals that are inserted into the housing. While robust, this connector lacks modern
features like hot-swap capability and has largely been phased out of new PC designs (see Legacy
Status section), yet it remains a hallmark of Molex’s influence on PC architecture.

Micro-Fit 3.0™ series: As the need arose for connectors smaller than Mini-Fit but still capable of
moderate power (up to ~5 A per circuit), Molex introduced the Micro-Fit family with a 3.00 mm pitch.
Micro-Fit connectors are high-density, dual-row connectors commonly used in compact PCs, robotics,
and industrial control for both power and signal. They include features like positive locks and keyed
housings to avoid mis-mating. For instance, in robotics applications, teams have used Micro-Fit 3.0
connectors for sensor and actuator wiring because of their balance of size and current capacity
.
However,  as  noted  by  practitioners,  proper  crimping  and  seating  of  the  tiny  terminals  can  be
challenging, prompting exploration of alternatives for reliability
.
Microminiature  and  Specialty  connectors: Molex  has  continually  pushed  toward  smaller
connectors  to  serve  modern  electronics.  Families  like  PicoBlade™ (1.25   mm  pitch)
 and
NanoBlade™/MicroBlade are  used  for  low-current  signal  connections  in  consumer  gadgets,
laptops, and wearables, where space is at a premium. These often employ a friction latch mechanism
and a two-point contact design to ensure reliability even at miniature scale
. In high-vibration or
harsh  environments,  Molex  also  offers  sealed  connectors (e.g.,  ML-XT™,  MX150™)  with  rubber
grommets and robust housings, and  RF/coaxial connectors for high-frequency signals. The  MX150
family cited earlier is a sealed automotive connector system with a 1.50 mm blade-type interface,
supporting up to 22 A and temperatures of 125–150 °C for under-hood applications
. Another
modern example is Molex’s SlimStack® board-to-board connectors, which have fine pitches down to
0.35 mm for connecting printed circuit boards in devices like smartphones; despite their tiny size,
they include alignment features and even audible click feedback for mating
.
Throughout its connector lines, Molex has emphasized compatibility and incremental improvement. Many
of its newer connectors maintain backward-compatible footprints or mating interfaces with older ones
(when possible) to ease adoption. The company also provides extensive application tooling – from hand
crimpers (as in Figure 1) to automated press-fit machines – reflecting the importance of proper termination
in connector performance. By offering both the connectors and the tools to apply them, Molex helped
ensure that its connectors could be reliably integrated by manufacturers at scale
.
In summary, the technical evolution of Molex connectors has been characterized by: (1) a reduction in size
(pitch and form factor) without sacrificing mechanical and electrical integrity, (2) improvements in current
density (more current per pin or per unit area through better materials and designs), (3) enhanced
mechanical  latching  and  keying features  to  prevent  errors  and  accidental  disconnections,  and  (4)
specialized variants for  harsh environments (sealed, high-temperature connectors for automotive and
industrial use). These innovations have enabled Molex to supply interconnect solutions from the largest
power connectors down to the tiniest microminiature links, cementing the company’s role as a one-stop
interconnect provider.

Application Domains of Molex Connectors
Molex connectors are employed across an exceptionally broad range of industries. Here we detail their
usage and significance in three key domains –  robotics,  consumer electronics (including computing),
and  automotive – while noting that they are also prevalent in telecommunications, medical devices,
aerospace,  and  more.  Each  domain  often  utilizes  specific  Molex  connector  series  optimized  for  that
environment’s requirements.
Robotics
In robotics (encompassing industrial automation robots, mobile robots, and even competitive robotics
teams), reliability and robustness of electrical connections are critical. Robots typically have numerous
actuators (motors, servos), sensors, and control boards interconnected by cables – all of which must
withstand motion, vibration, and sometimes harsh conditions. Molex connectors are a popular choice in
robotics for both power and signal wiring due to their proven performance.
For  power  distribution  in  robots,  teams  frequently  use  Molex’s  high-current  connector  families.  For
example, the  Mini-Fit Sr.™ series (the larger cousin of Mini-Fit Jr.) is often chosen for battery and motor
controller connections because it can handle heavy gauge wires (12 AWG or larger) and currents well above
20 A with secure locking
. One FIRST Robotics Competition team noted using Mini-Fit Sr. for all 12 AWG
power runs on their robot
. In the same context, smaller Molex connectors like Micro-Fit 3.0 have been
used for lower-current sensors and devices
. These connectors enable modular wiring harnesses –
sections of the robot can be quickly connected or replaced via plug-in cables rather than soldered joints,
which is advantageous for maintenance and design flexibility.
Molex  also  provides  connector  solutions  specifically  targeting  robotics  and  automation.  The  Molex
Industrial Automation product line includes heavy-duty circular connectors, M12 sensor connectors, and
the Compact Robotic Connectors (CRC) series for slim actuators
. In educational robotics (like FIRST),
Molex has introduced an SL™ series kit – containing standardized SL connectors for PWM signal cables,
CAN bus connections, etc., along with pre-crimped leads for teams without professional crimp tools
.
The SL series is a simple wire-to-wire and wire-to-board connector family with locking housings, well-suited
for signal wires (often 22–30 AWG) on robot control systems. By providing such kits, Molex has essentially
helped  standardize  wiring  practices  in  the  educational  robotics  community,  improving  reliability  (e.g.,
reducing loose or intermittent connections from improper wiring)
.
In industrial robotics, connectors must endure continuous motion (robot arms flexing cables) and often
exposure to oil or dust. Molex addresses these with flexible cable connectors and sealed interfaces. For
instance,  the  Brad®  line (originating  from  Molex’s  acquisition  of  Woodhead)  offers  rugged  circular
connectors  and  quick-disconnect  systems  for  industrial  sensors  and  actuators.  Additionally,  Molex’s
expertise in miniaturization is now aiding  consumer robotics: small home robots or drones benefit from
micro connectors like PicoBlade that save space and weight while delivering reliable connections
. As
robots become more sophisticated, high-speed data connectors (for machine vision cameras, LiDAR, etc.)
are also needed; Molex supplies solutions here too (e.g., automotive-grade ethernet connectors used in
autonomous robots). In sum, Molex connectors in robotics range from tiny board-level connectors on
controllers  to  high-power  battery  plugs  –  all  designed  to  maintain  connectivity  under  the  dynamic
conditions unique to robotics.

Consumer Electronics and Computing
Molex connectors have long been ubiquitous in consumer electronics – a domain that includes personal
computing  devices,  home  entertainment  equipment,  appliances,  mobile  devices,  and  more.  Their
widespread use in this area is due to a combination of standardization, cost-effectiveness, and the vast
array of form factors Molex offers for different needs.
In the realm of personal computers, Molex connectors essentially set the standard for internal power and
board connections for decades. Early microcomputers and PCs in the 1970s–90s employed Molex pin-and-
socket connectors for power supply interfaces: for example, IBM PCs used two 6-pin Molex connectors (the
P8/P9 connectors) to link the PSU to the motherboard in the 1980s
. By the mid-1990s, the ATX standard
introduced the 20-pin (later 24-pin) main power connector, which – as mentioned – is a Molex Mini-Fit Jr.
series plug
. Molex’s connectors were also used for peripheral devices: the 4-pin Molex peripheral
power connector (Figure 2) became a  de facto standard for hard drives, optical drives, and other
peripherals across all PC manufacturers
. Additionally, smaller Berg connectors (another brand, but
often supplied by Molex) were used for floppy drives’ power. Inside a typical desktop PC from around 2000,
one would find a dozen Molex-made or Molex-compatible connectors: motherboard headers for USB, front
panel LEDs (often KK family), fan power connectors, drive power connectors, and so on.
Molex  connectors  gained  this  universal  adoption  in  PCs  in  part  because  Molex  actively  worked  with
standards committees (like the Intel ATX specification) to define connector interfaces. For instance, Intel’s
ATX specification explicitly references Molex part numbers for the main power connector and peripheral
power connectors as approved reference designs
. The consistency and reliability of these connectors
helped ensure interoperability – any ATX power supply could plug into any ATX motherboard because they
all used the same Molex Mini-Fit Jr. 20/24-pin interface. This standardization was crucial for the growth of
the PC industry, enabling mix-and-match of components from different vendors.
Beyond  computers,  in  consumer  electronics  and  appliances,  Molex  connectors  appear  virtually
everywhere behind the scenes. Televisions and audio equipment often use wire-to-board KK or MicroBlade
connectors to link power boards, speakers, and control boards. Household appliances (washing machines,
HVAC systems, kitchen appliances) use a variety of Molex quick-connect harnesses – e.g.,  Molex .093″
series or MLX series connectors for internal wiring harnesses that carry power to motors and sensors.
These connectors offer secure latching and the ability to carry higher currents for things like compressor
motors. The Molex Mini-Fit Jr. series, while famous in PCs, is also found in appliances that need compact
connectors for moderate power – for example, some refrigerators and air conditioners use 2- to 4-circuit
Mini-Fit connectors to hook up control boards and fans, taking advantage of their 6–9 A per pin capability
and reliable locking
. In high-volume consumer products, cost is a major factor; Molex often provides
value-engineered connector versions (using cheaper materials or simpler designs) to meet price targets
while maintaining adequate performance.
An area of increasing importance is  mobile and handheld devices. As gadgets like smartphones and
tablets became thinner, traditional wire harness connectors were too bulky, which spurred development of
micro coaxial connectors, board-to-board mezzanine connectors, and flex cable connectors. Molex has been
at the forefront here, creating families like SlimStack board-to-board connectors used to connect display
panels  or  modules  inside  phones
,  and  Micro  USB/USB-C  connectors (Molex  is  a  supplier  of
standardized  USB  connectors  for  charging  and  data).  For  wearables  and  IoT  devices,  Molex  micro
connectors (like micro coax RF connectors and tiny wire-to-board connectors) enable sensors and batteries to

Automotive
The automotive industry presents one of the most demanding environments for connectors: extreme
temperatures, moisture, vibration, and safety-critical reliability requirements. Automobiles contain dozens
of electronic control units (ECUs) and hundreds of sensors/actuators, all linked by wiring harnesses. Molex
has been deeply involved in automotive connectors for many decades, both through its own developments
and strategic acquisitions (such as Cardell in 1980, and more recently partnerships in autonomous vehicle
connectors).
Traditionally, automotive wiring harnesses used a variety of specialized connector systems (often following
standards like USCAR). Molex’s early involvement in automotive included supplying connectors for basic
electrical systems and later for the first generations of electronic engine control and infotainment. A
hallmark of Molex’s automotive connectors is the focus on sealed connectors to prevent ingress of water,
dirt, and chemicals. For example, Molex MX150™ is a family of sealed and unsealed connectors widely used
in car engine compartments and lighting systems
. The MX150 series conforms to USCAR specifications
and uses a mat-seal design (integral seals) to achieve environmental sealing. It can operate up to 150 °C
and carry up to 22 A per circuit, making it suitable for applications like fuel injectors or exterior lighting
.
Despite being a mature product, MX150 remains a go-to solution in automotive due to its proven field
performance and standardized interface (it provides roughly 37% space savings over older 1.50 mm pitch
connectors without sacrificing current handling)
.
As vehicles demanded more electronic features, the number of connections skyrocketed, leading to a push
for  smaller  automotive  connectors to  save  space  and  weight.  Molex  answered  this  with  the  Mini50™
connector system – the industry’s first automotive-grade connector with a 0.50 mm terminal interface
(roughly 50% smaller footprint than the traditional 0.64 mm connectors used in many car harnesses)
.
Mini50  connectors  are  designed  for  low-current  signals  in  space-constrained  locations,  such  as  in
infotainment  systems,  advanced  driver-assistance  systems  (ADAS)  sensors,  and  body  electronics.  They  are
available in both sealed and unsealed versions and meet USCAR requirements, illustrating how Molex
brought miniaturization to the automotive world without compromising on ruggedness
. By reducing
size, car manufacturers can route harnesses through tighter spaces and potentially reduce the weight of
bundles by including more circuits in a smaller connector housing.
Additionally, Molex has developed high-speed data connectors for automotive applications. Modern cars
use Ethernet-based networks, high-speed camera links, etc. Molex’s connectors like  HSAutoLink™ and
coaxial RF connectors help implement USB, LVDS camera links, and Ethernet in cars with automotive-grade

connector designs for long periods. Molex’s legacy connectors (like the older MLX series or CGrid and KK
series for interior electronics) still appear in new vehicles for this reason. Manufacturers appreciate that a
mature connector series will have stable supply and known failure rates
. Moreover, reusing an
existing connector design means technicians are already trained to assemble and repair them, and OEMs
have already invested in the crimp tooling and assembly equipment
. Molex, recognizing this, continues
to produce and support many of its legacy automotive connectors even as it introduces new ones –
balancing innovation with backward compatibility.
In summary, Molex’s role in automotive connectors is characterized by a dual focus: continuous innovation
(smaller, smarter connectors for next-gen vehicles) and sustaining reliable legacy systems (ensuring that
existing connector styles remain available and improved incrementally). This strategy helps automakers
integrate new features without reinventing the wheel for every connector interface, contributing to vehicle
reliability and manufacturability.
Legacy Status of Molex Connectors
Certain Molex connectors, despite their historical prevalence, have become regarded as “legacy” in modern
contexts – meaning they are largely phased out or used only for backwards compatibility with older
equipment. This section explores why this occurs, using the example of the 4-pin Molex peripheral power
connector in PCs as a primary case study, and touches on other scenarios where older Molex connector
styles are superseded by newer technology.
The 4-pin Molex power plug in PCs: For decades, the white 4-pin Molex plug (Figure 2) was the standard
way to supply disk drives and fans with 5 V and 12 V from a PC power supply. However, with the advent of
the Serial ATA (SATA) interface in the mid-2000s, a new power connector was introduced for hard drives and
optical drives. The SATA power connector is a 15-pin wafer-style connector that provides additional voltages
(including 3.3 V) and supports hot-swapping drives
. This new design, which Molex and other companies
helped develop, offers a more streamlined form factor and features like staggered pins for hot-plugging. As
SATA drives became ubiquitous, the older 4-pin Molex plug saw diminishing use. New drives stopped
including Molex power sockets, favoring the SATA power port exclusively. Consequently, in a modern PC, the
4-pin Molex connectors from the power supply might only be used for peripherals like case fans, water
cooling pumps, or LED lighting strips – and even many of those have transitioned to newer connectors or
direct motherboard connections.
Today, the 4-pin Molex is widely considered a  legacy connector in the PC world
. Enthusiast forums
commonly describe it as “really old” and note that “barely anything still uses Molex” in a contemporary
computer build
. Its design limitations (no latch, stiff to unplug, bulky shape) and the lack of a 3.3 V
supply made it less suitable for newer devices. The SATA power connector’s slim, keyed design is easier to
insert and cannot be accidentally reversed, improving user experience and safety
. Moreover, the SATA

Other legacy contexts: Beyond the PC power plug, there are other Molex connectors that have been
supplanted by new solutions in various fields:
In electronics hobbyist and small devices, the older 2.54 mm KK-series connectors or larger pitch
headers are sometimes viewed as legacy as the industry shifts to smaller JST-brand connectors (e.g.,
JST-PH  2.0   mm  or  JST-SM  series)  for  compactness.  While  Molex  KK  connectors  are  still  used

connectors internally, using micro-coax, flat flex cables, or low-profile micro connectors instead
.
Thus KK connectors could be considered legacy in the context of modern mobile device design,
though they thrive in legacy-compatible realms like PC hobby projects or industrial control panels.
In  telecommunications  and  networking  equipment,  older  backplane  connector  systems
(including  some  made  by  Molex)  have  been  replaced  by  high-speed  modular  connectors.  For
example, legacy telecom systems might have used Molex VMEbus or CompactPCI connectors; today
these are often replaced by advanced high-speed backplane connectors (some made by Molex, some
by others like TE Connectivity). The legacy connectors persist only in existing infrastructure, whereas
new designs adopt connectors that can handle multi-gigabit signals.
In automotive, as discussed, the presence of legacy connectors is actually intentional for longevity.
But even there, some formerly standard connectors become legacy when a new standard is agreed
upon. A case in point is the OBD-II diagnostic connector – not made by Molex specifically, but as a
standard,  it  succeeded  various  proprietary  diagnostic  connectors  (some  of  which  Molex  did
manufacture for certain car makers). Once OBD-II became mandated, the older ones became legacy
and disappeared from new vehicles. Similarly, as vehicles move to  higher-voltage EV systems,
certain 12V connector styles might be considered legacy for high-power connections, being replaced
by newly designed high-voltage interlock connectors.
Why connectors become legacy: In general, a connector becomes “legacy” when  either its technical
limitations impede new requirements or a superior standardized alternative emerges. In the case of
the Molex 4-pin: it could not meet the evolving requirements for SATA drives (hot-swap, additional voltage
rail)
, and a new standard (SATA) took over with broad industry support. Once widely adopted, the new
connector rapidly displaced the old in its primary application. Another reason is ergonomics and safety –
newer connectors often aim to reduce user errors and improve serviceability. The 4-pin Molex’s tendency to

ensuring that even as specific products become legacy, the company’s innovations continue to shape the

Conclusion
From its origin as a maker of plastic molding compounds in the 1930s to its status today as a global leader
in electronic interconnects, Molex’s journey is a remarkable case of technological adaptation and influence.
The development of Molex’s connector technologies closely parallels the evolution of electronics over the
past seventy years. In the post-war era, Molex’s introduction of affordable pin-and-socket connectors
helped enable the expansion of consumer appliances and the nascent electronics industry
. By the
late 20th century, Molex connectors had become virtually universal in personal computing, powering the
world’s PCs and peripherals
. The technical overview in this paper highlighted how Molex’s connector
designs evolved – becoming smaller, more capable, and more diverse – to meet the changing needs of
applications ranging from tiny wearables to heavy-duty automotive systems. Molex’s portfolio today ranges
from legacy 0.093″ power plugs to cutting-edge 0.35 mm pitch board-to-board connectors, reflecting an
ongoing commitment to innovation in connectivity.
The applications surveyed – robotics, consumer electronics, and automotive – demonstrate the breadth of
scenarios where Molex connectors are employed. In each domain, certain Molex products became standard
solutions (e.g., Mini-Fit Jr. in PC power supplies, sealed MX150 connectors in cars, modular KK connectors in
appliances), underscoring how the company’s focus on reliability and ease-of-use resonated with engineers.
These connectors often form the unseen backbone of electronic systems, quietly ensuring power and
signals flow correctly. When they perform flawlessly, end-users rarely notice them – which is arguably the
highest compliment for a connector. At the same time, we have seen that even successful connectors
eventually face obsolescence as new requirements arise. The 4-pin Molex PC connector’s transition to
legacy status in the SATA era is a prime example: it highlights how advancements (hot-swap, higher pin
counts) drive new interface standards, and older ones gracefully retire from the spotlight
. Molex’s
strategy of balancing new product development with support for established connectors has allowed for
