## Executive Summary

Solid-State Drives (SSDs) have revolutionized storage by eliminating moving parts and leveraging high-speed flash memory. This paper presents an overview of SSD architecture and examines the performance benefits of SSDs over traditional Hard Disk Drives (HDDs) and across different SSD interface types (SATA, NVMe, PCIe, M.2, U.2, EDSFF). We detail the internal architecture of SSDs – including flash memory arrays, controllers, and caches – and explain how design features (parallelism, advanced controllers, and optimized interfaces like NVMe) yield dramatic improvements in input/output operations per second (IOPS), throughput, and latency. Comparisons show that SSDs deliver orders-of-magnitude higher IOPS and much lower access latencies than HDDs. Furthermore, modern NVMe SSDs connected via PCI Express outperform SATA-based SSDs by substantial margins due to a streamlined protocol and greater bandwidth.

## 1. Introduction

In recent years, SSDs have become the primary storage medium for many applications, replacing or augmenting HDDs. Unlike HDDs, which store data on spinning magnetic platters and require mechanical head movements, an SSD stores data in semiconductor memory (typically NAND flash) and has no moving parts. This fundamental difference in architecture leads to faster data access speeds and lower latency.

**Current Performance Landscape (2026):**

| Storage Type | Sequential Read | Random IOPS | Latency |
|--------------|----------------|-------------|---------|
| **HDD (7200 RPM)** | 100–160 MB/s | 80–120 | 8–12 ms |
| **SATA SSD** | 500–560 MB/s | 90K–100K | 0.1–0.2 ms |
| **NVMe Gen3** | 3,000–3,500 MB/s | 400K–500K | 0.02–0.05 ms |
| **NVMe Gen4** | 5,000–7,000 MB/s | 600K–1M | 0.01–0.02 ms |
| **NVMe Gen5** | 14,000–15,000 MB/s | 1.5M–2M+ | <0.01 ms |

**Verification:** Kimsufi 18-month benchmarks (January 2025 – June 2026) across 30 servers show NVMe delivers 4–6× faster query times than HDD .

Beyond speed, SSDs offer additional benefits: they draw less power, run cooler, and are shock-resistant due to the lack of mechanical parts. These advantages come at a higher cost per gigabyte and the intrinsic limitation that flash memory can endure only a finite number of write/erase cycles. Nevertheless, for performance-critical applications and everyday computing alike, the performance gains of SSDs overwhelmingly justify their adoption.

## 2. SSD Architecture Overview

### 2.1 Core Components

At a high level, an SSD consists of three primary components:

| Component | Function |
|-----------|----------|
| **NAND Flash Memory** | Data storage (SLC, MLC, TLC, QLC, PLC) |
| **SSD Controller** | Embedded processor managing data placement, retrieval, and flash management |
| **DRAM Cache / Buffer** | Staging data for writes/reads; caching FTL mapping table |

**Verification:** Modern SSD architecture comprises NAND flash memory, SSD controller (FTL, garbage collection, wear leveling), and host interface protocols .

### 2.2 Flash Memory Organization

NAND flash is organized into arrays of memory cells, pages, and blocks:

| Unit | Typical Size | Operation |
|------|-------------|-----------|
| **Page** | 4 KB or 16 KB | Read/write unit |
| **Block** | Contains many pages | Erase unit |

Because flash cells must be erased before being rewritten, the controller uses techniques like out-of-place updates and garbage collection. As an SSD fills up, write operations may incur additional overhead (read–erase–modify cycles), causing performance to degrade from peak "fresh-out-of-the-box" state to a steady-state level.

**Verification:** In steady state, an SSD's IOPS can be less than 50% (or even as low as 5%) of its initial peak value, depending on workload and controller firmware .

### 2.3 Internal Parallelism

A key architectural advantage of SSDs is internal parallelism. To overcome the relatively slow program/erase speed of individual flash cells, SSD controllers access multiple NAND chips concurrently via independent channels.

**Verification:** A high-end SSD may have 8 or more flash channels, each with several chips (dies) that can operate in parallel. This allows the device to service multiple I/O requests simultaneously and aggregate the throughput of many chips .

**2026 Controller Developments:**

| Controller | Manufacturer | Key Features |
|------------|-------------|--------------|
| **SM2524XT** | Silicon Motion | 6nm DRAMless, 14 GB/s, AI PC optimization  |
| **E28** | Phison | 8-channel, DRAM cache, NVMe 2.0, Gen5  |
| **Bravera SC6** | Marvell | PCIe 6.0, 16 NAND channels, 15 cores  |

### 2.4 DRAM Cache and Buffer Memory

Most SSDs include a DRAM buffer or cache integrated with the controller. This DDR memory is used to stage data for writes and reads, coalesce small random writes into larger sequential bursts, and cache the mapping table (FTL) for faster translation of addresses.

**Verification:** Some lower-end consumer SSDs forego a DRAM cache to save cost, instead relying on the host memory (via NVMe Host Memory Buffer) or using a small on-controller SRAM . High-end enterprise SSDs have large caches and power loss protection capacitors.

## 3. SSD Interfaces and Form Factors

### 3.1 Interface Comparison

| Interface | Protocol | Max Theoretical | Typical Sequential | Queue Depth |
|-----------|----------|-----------------|-------------------|-------------|
| **SATA III** | AHCI | 6 Gbps (600 MB/s) | 500–560 MB/s | 1 queue, 32 commands |
| **PCIe 3.0 x4** | NVMe | ~32 Gbps (3.94 GB/s) | 3,000–3,500 MB/s | 64K queues |
| **PCIe 4.0 x4** | NVMe | ~64 Gbps (7.88 GB/s) | 5,000–7,000 MB/s | 64K queues |
| **PCIe 5.0 x4** | NVMe | ~128 Gbps (15.75 GB/s) | 14,000–15,000 MB/s | 64K queues |

**Verification:** NVMe supports up to 64,000 queues, each with up to 64,000 commands – a massive increase in concurrency compared to AHCI's single queue of 32 .

### 3.2 Form Factor Overview

| Form Factor | Connector | Typical Use | Hot-Swap |
|-------------|-----------|-------------|----------|
| **2.5" SATA** | SATA | Legacy replacement | Yes |
| **M.2** | M.2 (PCIe/SATA) | Laptops, desktops | No |
| **U.2** | SFF-8639 | Enterprise servers | Yes |
| **EDSFF (E1.S, E3.S)** | SFF-TA-1002 | Hyperscale, edge | Yes |

**Verification:** U.2 (formerly SFF-8639) is a 2.5-inch form factor that typically carries four PCIe lanes and additional power lines .

**EDSFF (Enterprise and Datacenter Standard Form Factor):** Introduced by the Open Compute Project (OCP) to meet the evolving demands of hyperscale data centers. Unlike incremental updates to existing U.2 or M.2 drives, EDSFF represents a complete structural redesign.

**Verification:** E1.S provides higher power support (20–25W+), front-access hot-swap capability, optimized airflow channels, and higher density per rack unit .

**PCIe 6.0 Form Factor:** PCI SIG has decided that future PCIe 6.0 SSDs will only retain EDSFF interfaces (E3, E1, etc.), though U.2 and M.2 may persist temporarily .

## 4. Performance Metrics and Comparison

### 4.1 Latency and IOPS

The elimination of mechanical delays gives SSDs an enormous advantage in latency:

| Storage Type | Random 4K IOPS | Latency | Relative Speedup |
|--------------|----------------|---------|------------------|
| **HDD (7200 RPM)** | 80–120 | 8–12 ms | 1× |
| **SATA SSD** | 90K–100K | 0.1–0.2 ms | ~1,000× |
| **NVMe Gen3** | 400K–500K | 0.02–0.05 ms | ~5,000× |
| **NVMe Gen4** | 600K–1M | 0.01–0.02 ms | ~8,000× |

**Verification:** A benchmark comparing a fast consumer HDD to a mainstream SSD found the HDD achieved ~208 IOPS in a 4K random write test, versus ~30,000 IOPS for the SSD – approximately a 144× speedup .

**Verification:** "SSDs handle these about 1,000 times faster than HDDs (roughly 100×)" .

### 4.2 Throughput (Bandwidth)

| Storage Type | Sequential Read | Sequential Write |
|--------------|-----------------|------------------|
| **HDD (7200 RPM)** | 100–160 MB/s | 100–160 MB/s |
| **SATA SSD** | 500–560 MB/s | 500–530 MB/s |
| **NVMe Gen3** | 3,000–3,500 MB/s | 2,500–3,000 MB/s |
| **NVMe Gen4** | 5,000–7,000 MB/s | 4,500–6,900 MB/s |
| **NVMe Gen5** | 14,000–15,000 MB/s | 12,000–13,700 MB/s |

**Verification:** "PCIe 5.0 SSDs have reached the point where the fastest of them read at nearly 15,000 MB/s" .

**Verification:** "Samsung 9100 PRO — reaching up to 14,800 MB/s read and 13,400 MB/s write, this drive sets a new benchmark for consumer sequential performance" .

### 4.3 Multi-Queue and Concurrency

NVMe's design with many queues means it can take advantage of parallel I/O from multi-core systems without locking – multiple CPU cores can submit I/O to an NVMe drive simultaneously on different queues, greatly reducing contention and context switches.

**Verification:** At high queue depths, NVMe IOPS continue to climb, whereas SATA drives often plateau. An NVMe SSD can sustain hundreds of thousands of IOPS at high queue depth (QD32 or QD64), whereas a SATA SSD might reach its max ~90K–100K IOPS by QD32 .

### 4.4 Performance vs. Capacity and Endurance

SSD performance depends on NAND type and drive capacity:

| NAND Type | Bits/Cell | Relative Speed | Endurance | Cost |
|-----------|-----------|----------------|-----------|------|
| **SLC** | 1 | Fastest | Highest | Highest |
| **MLC** | 2 | Fast | High | High |
| **TLC** | 3 | Moderate | Moderate | Moderate |
| **QLC** | 4 | Slowest | Lower | Lowest |

**Verification (QLC Performance Cliff):** When QLC caches are exhausted, direct-QLC writes average around 79.4 MB/s, with minimum results dropping to 25.6 MB/s; at 60% storage usage, direct-QLC writes averaged around 45 MB/s, with minimum dropping to 1.14 MB/s .

**Verification (TLC vs QLC):** In mixed read/write testing, the 512 GB TLC version scored 11,285 points versus 8,168 for QLC – approximately 38% lower .

## 7. Conclusion

SSDs achieve their superior performance through a combination of architectural features and optimized interfaces. By replacing mechanical motion with electronic operations, SSDs eliminate the seek and rotation delays inherent to HDDs, cutting access latency from milliseconds to microseconds. Internally, SSD controllers use parallelism (multiple flash channels and dies) and caching to overcome NAND flash's physical program/erase limitations, thereby delivering high throughput and IOPS to the host.

The evolution of host interfaces from SATA/AHCI to PCIe/NVMe has further unlocked SSD performance: NVMe drives leverage wider bandwidth and deep queueing to fully utilize the internal speeds of flash memory, outpacing SATA SSDs by large margins. Empirical data and industry benchmarks consistently show drastic performance benefits of SSDs – often 100× or more in random I/O – over even the fastest HDDs.

Looking ahead, SSD architecture continues to advance. Emerging technologies such as PCIe 6.0, EDSFF form factors, and advanced controllers from Silicon Motion, Phison, and Marvell will continue to push performance boundaries. The fundamental architectural theme remains: minimizing latency and maximizing parallel throughput to storage media.
