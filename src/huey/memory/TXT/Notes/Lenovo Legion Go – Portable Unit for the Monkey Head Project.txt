Lenovo Legion Go – Portable Unit for the Monkey
Head Project
The Lenovo Legion Go is a handheld gaming PC that Lenovo introduced as a direct competitor to devices
like the Steam Deck and Asus ROG Ally. It’s essentially a full Windows 11 computer in a portable console
form factor, and in the context of the Monkey Head Project, it will serve as a flexible, modular "Huey" unit
– i.e. a portable assistant on the go. What makes the Legion Go particularly notable (besides its powerful
internals) is its unique detachable controller design, which adds versatility in how it can be used within
the project. Below, we break down the Legion Go’s key specifications, features, and how it can be integrated
and optimized for performance in the project’s use-case.
Key Hardware Specifications and Features
Processor & Graphics: AMD Ryzen Z1 Extreme 8-core/16-thread APU (up to ~5.1 GHz boost) with 12
RDNA 3 graphics compute units (integrated GPU up to 2.7 GHz)
. This is the same high-end chip
used in the ROG Ally, offering comparable performance to a mid-range gaming PC in handheld form
. (The integrated GPU shares system memory and is roughly equivalent to a dedicated graphics
card in the ~3GB VRAM class, suitable for 720p to 1080p gaming). 
Memory: 16 GB of LPDDR5X-7500 RAM (soldered)
, providing ample memory bandwidth for both
the CPU and integrated GPU. 
Storage: 1 TB PCIe Gen4 NVMe SSD (M.2 2242 form factor)
 for fast storage. This is a generous
amount of onboard storage for the operating system, project files, and even games. Additionally, the
Legion Go has a microSD card slot for expansion – it supports cards up to 2 TB, which can more
than double the total storage capacity for data or media
. 
Display: 8.8-inch WQXGA touchscreen (2560×1600 resolution, 16:10 aspect ratio) at up to 144 Hz
refresh rate
. This IPS panel is bright (500 nits) and protected by Gorilla Glass 5
. The high
resolution and refresh rate are standout features, though running at lower resolutions (like 1200p or
800p) is often preferable for better performance and battery life. 
Detachable Controllers: Two gamepad controllers attach to the sides of the screen (similar to
Nintendo Switch Joy-Cons). These controllers can be released via hidden latches and used
wirelessly when detached
. They include dual analog sticks (hall-effect sensors to prevent drift), D-
pad, ABXY buttons, analog triggers, bumpers, a small trackpad on the right controller, a scroll
wheel, and six assignable grip buttons on the back
. (More on the controllers in the next
section.)
Battery: 49.2 Wh rechargeable battery
. This capacity is slightly larger than Steam Deck/ROG
Ally’s ~40 Wh batteries, but the powerful hardware means battery life is still limited – in heavy use
(gaming at high settings) it can drop under 1 hour, while more moderate use or power-saving
settings can yield around 2–3 hours on a charge
. (In best-case scenarios with tweaks, testers
have managed roughly 3 hours, which is better than the Ally but shorter than the Steam Deck
under similar conditions
.) 
Ports & Connectivity: Uncommonly for a handheld, the Legion Go offers two USB-C 4.0 ports (one
on top, one on the bottom)
. Both support USB4/Thunderbolt-like speeds and DisplayPort 1.4

Overall, the Legion Go’s hardware is top-tier for a handheld device. It essentially packs ultrabook-level
internals into a portable form. Next, we’ll look at the Legion Go’s signature feature – its  detachable
controllers – and why that matters for modular use.
Unique Detachable Controllers and Modular Design
One defining feature of the Lenovo Legion Go is its modular controller system. The two controllers can
slide on and off the sides of the main unit, very much like a Nintendo Switch’s Joy-Cons, but Lenovo’s
mechanism is more robust and well-engineered with minimal wobble when attached
. You simply
press a release switch and slide them off, and they’ll automatically connect via Bluetooth to the main unit
when detached
. The device also has a wide, sturdy kickstand on the back, spanning almost the entire
width of the unit, so you can prop the screen up on a table when using the controllers off-device
. 
Each controller is feature-packed: the  right-hand controller includes a tiny circular trackpad (useful for
mouse cursor movement in Windows or strategy games) and even a scroll wheel, which is uncommon on
game controllers
. Notably, the bottom of the right controller has an optical sensor and a switch for
“FPS mode”
. In FPS mode, you can attach a small included cover to the controller that lets you use it like
a vertical mouse – the optical sensor tracks movement when you slide the controller on a surface, and the
triggers act as mouse buttons
. This effectively turns the Legion Go into a mini tabletop PC: one
controller becomes a mouse and the other can be used like a joystick or gamepad in the other hand. This
mode is ideal for precision aiming in shooters or navigating Windows interface when docked on a table. 
Furthermore, both controllers have haptic feedback and six-axis gyros for motion controls
, and Lenovo
used hall-effect joysticks to avoid drift issues over time
. There are also extra grip buttons (M1, M2,
M3, Y1, Y2, Y3) on the backs of the controllers that can be custom-mapped for additional functions
,
which could be useful for custom commands in the project’s applications. 
From a modularity perspective, this detachable design means the Legion Go can serve multiple roles: it’s a
handheld console when you need all-in-one portability, but it can also act like a small desktop or tablet
when you set it down and remove the controllers. For the Monkey Head Project, this flexibility is a big plus –
you could, for example, mount the main tablet portion onto a robotic frame or a stand (as a display or
control unit) while an operator uses the wireless controllers to interact from a short distance. The kickstand
supporting  various  angles  makes  it  practical  to  deploy  the  device  on  a  desk  or  lab  bench  and  still
comfortably use it. In short, the Legion Go’s hardware isn’t just powerful; it’s reconfigurable, which aligns
well with the project’s need for a adaptable, modular assistant unit.

AI Operating System
. In the full lab setup, Huey is a large, stationary unit with high-end hardware (like

Despite its much smaller size, the Legion Go’s specs (discussed above) are quite formidable. The 8-core Z1

Having Windows 11 on the Legion Go is not an issue, as the GenCore AI and its subsystems are designed to

, which confirms that the team is actively using it to test and run the AI system. As a portable Huey,
the Legion Go could be used by a lab assistant or engineer on the move – for instance, carrying it to

(through a connected headset or external microphone, since the device itself doesn’t have built-in mics/
cameras like a phone), view data on its screen, and even connect AR/VR goggles or other peripherals if
needed. In fact, Lenovo has an accessory called Legion Glasses (AR glasses) that can pair with the Legion Go
for heads-up display, pointing to future AR integration possibilities
.
Thanks to the dual USB4 ports, integration into larger setups is straightforward. For example, in a lab one
could plug the Legion Go into a power source and connect it to external devices: monitors, keyboards/
mice, or even an external GPU if extreme graphics performance were needed. The USB-C ports support
DisplayPort-out and power delivery simultaneously, so the Legion Go can be docked and act almost like a
small desktop when the situation calls for it
. This could be useful if the AI needs to display a lot of
information on a big screen during a demonstration, or if it needs to interface with specialized hardware
(via USB adapters) while still being easily removable and portable.
In summary, using the Lenovo Legion Go as a portable Huey unit provides the Monkey Head Project with a
highly versatile, mobile platform for their AI. It bridges the gap between the fixed, powerful lab hardware
and a truly mobile assistant. While it doesn’t match the raw power of the full-sized Huey rig (with server-
grade components), it offers sufficient performance to run the AI in the field, and its modular form allows it
to adapt to different scenarios (handheld use, table-top use with detached controllers, or docked use in a
larger setup).
Performance Profile and Battery Life
Given its powerful AMD APU, the Legion Go’s performance is impressive for a handheld. In practical terms,
it can run modern 3D games and heavy applications – an important consideration if the Monkey Head
Project’s AI involves real-time processing or even some on-device machine learning inference. The Ryzen Z1
Extreme is essentially on par with an AMD Ryzen 7 7840U (8-core Zen4 architecture), and the integrated
RDNA3  GPU  (similar  to  Radeon  780M  graphics)  can  handle  many  games  at  respectable  settings.
Benchmarks in reviews have shown that at 20W TDP (Performance mode) – which is the default max on
battery – the Legion Go achieves, for example, around  64 FPS in Doom Eternal (Medium settings, at
~1200p) and around  80 FPS in Star Wars: Squadrons (Low settings)
. This indicates the device can
comfortably handle 3D environments or GUI-intensive tasks the project might throw at it, especially if the

One thing to note is that the Legion Go’s high native screen resolution (2560×1600) can be very demanding.
For optimal performance (and to conserve energy), the  project team will likely run applications at a

battery life without much downside.
On the topic of battery life, as mentioned, the Legion Go can be quite power-hungry. The 49 Wh battery is
physically larger than many competitors’, yet under heavy load (gaming, stress tests) users have reported
draining the battery in under an hour
. For less intense usage (web browsing, coding, running simpler
AI tasks), you might get a few hours. In direct comparisons, the Legion Go tends to last a bit longer than
the Asus ROG Ally (which has a smaller battery) but still significantly less than a Steam Deck running
Linux, which is heavily optimized for efficiency
. One review noted that after Valve’s optimizations, a
Steam Deck could reach 5 hours of light use, whereas the Legion Go “gets 3 at best with the same
tweaks”
. 
However, for the Monkey Head Project’s use as a portable assistant, there are ways to manage this: running
in “Balanced” or “Quiet” power modes for routine tasks can cap the APU to a lower wattage (e.g., 8-15W),
dramatically extending runtime (at the cost of some performance)
. The device has a physical mode
switch (with an LED indicator: blue for Quiet, white for Balanced, red for Performance) to quickly toggle
these modes
. This means a user can dynamically choose to conserve battery when doing light tasks (like
note-taking or idle listening) and then kick into Performance mode (20W TDP) when heavy computation is
needed. Additionally, because the Legion Go is a full Windows PC, traditional power management (Windows
battery saver, dimming screen, etc.) applies as well. And of course, whenever possible, using the device
while plugged in (or carrying a USB-C power bank) can alleviate runtime concerns.
Thermal performance of the Legion Go is quite good for its class. Thanks to a robust cooling solution (with
ample ventilation and a large fan), it tends to  stay relatively cool to the touch under load
 – an
important consideration if someone will hold it or have it close to them while it’s working. Reviewers
observed internal temperatures in the 60°C range on Performance mode, and surface temperatures that
were warm but not uncomfortable
. The fan can get audible (running up to ~34 dB(A) in Performance
mode)
, but in many cases, especially if running lighter workloads or when the fan is in Quiet mode,
noise should not be a major distraction. For an AI assistant usage, this means the device likely won’t
overheat even during sustained computations, and any fan noise is a reasonable trade-off for keeping the
device cool.
In summary, out-of-the-box performance of the Legion Go should be more than sufficient for a portable
Monkey Head Project unit. The key is balancing that performance with battery life and heat – using the right
performance profile for the task at hand. In the next section, we’ll discuss potential  modifications and
tweaks to further improve performance, efficiency, and overall suitability of the Legion Go for the project.

Potential Modifications and Performance Improvements
To get the most out of the Lenovo Legion Go as a project platform, there are several modifications and
optimizations  that  can  be  considered.  These  range  from  simple  software  settings  to  more  involved
hardware tweaks:
TDP and Power Settings: The Legion Go comes with built-in power profiles (accessed via Lenovo’s
Legion Space software or a quick sidebar) that let you adjust the APU’s TDP on the fly. For instance,
Quiet mode limits wattage (for cooler, quieter operation), while Performance mode targets ~20W on
battery
. There’s also a Manual mode where users can attempt higher TDP (25W or 30W) when
plugged in. However, testing shows going beyond ~20W on battery yields diminishing returns – in
fact, one review noted that switching from 20W to 30W while on battery sometimes  worsened
performance due to power or thermal constraints
. The takeaway: using the built-in modes is
usually effective, and  tuning TDP to the task (lower for efficiency, higher for heavy computation
when plugged in) can optimize performance per watt. The quick-toggle button makes this easy to
manage on the go. 
Undervolting the APU: If squeezing out more efficiency is desired, undervolting is an advanced
tweak. By slightly lowering the voltage to the CPU/GPU while maintaining clock speeds, you reduce
heat and power draw, which can either prolong battery life or allow higher clocks within the same
power budget. Officially, Lenovo doesn’t advertise undervolting, but some users have reported that
the Legion Go’s BIOS has options for it, and tools like the Universal X86 Tuning Utility can be used
in Windows to undervolt the Z1 Extreme
. This isn’t a necessity for most users – as one Legion Go
owner noted, you might not  need to undervolt since you can just use the TDP slider to manage
performance/power
. But for enthusiasts in the project, a stable undervolt could be beneficial
(e.g., it might shave off a few degrees of heat and extend battery life during intensive computations).
Caution is advised: every chip is different, so any undervolt should be tested thoroughly for stability.
Thermal Paste Upgrade: Improving the cooling interface is another tweak that’s been tried. The
stock thermal paste on the APU can be replaced with a higher-performance compound. Community
reports indicate that using a quality paste like  Arctic MX-4 can noticeably lower temperatures,
whereas even more exotic solutions like Honeywell’s PTM7950 phase-change pad gave only modest
gains in this particular device
. Re-pasting the device requires opening it up (which is a delicate
process), so it’s an optional mod for those comfortable with hardware. If done properly, better
cooling can help the Legion Go sustain high performance longer (less throttling) and possibly make
the fan quieter under load. Given that the Legion Go already stays fairly cool externally, this mod is
more about maximizing internal performance and longevity.
Screen and Refresh Rate Tuning: As mentioned earlier, running the display at full 2560×1600 and
144 Hz is overkill for many use cases and drains power. For practical use, especially in the project
context, one can reduce the resolution or use Windows scaling to effectively render at a lower
resolution (like 1920×1200 or 1280×800) when the ultra-fine detail isn’t needed. This can boost frame
rates significantly (important if the AI interface has any 3D visualization or if the device is used for
demos/gaming)
. Similarly, setting the refresh rate to 60 Hz except when 144 Hz is specifically
needed will help both battery and thermals. These are simple software settings that can be switched
as needed.

Storage and Memory Upgrades: The 1TB NVMe SSD is quite fast (Gen4 speeds) and more than
sufficient for most uses. But since it’s a standard M.2 2242 drive, it is technically replaceable if a
larger capacity or a faster model is desired in the future
. For example, one could install a 2TB
2242 SSD to have more onboard storage for AI datasets or media. The microSD slot, as mentioned,
offers up to an additional 2TB of removable storage
 – perfect for keeping large, less frequently
used files or backups. The RAM, however, is soldered and not upgradable; 16GB is the fixed amount.
If  the  project  workflows  ever  require  more  than  16GB  RAM  (for  running  multiple  AI  models
simultaneously, etc.), swapping to a device with more memory might be the only solution – but 16GB
is a generous amount for a mobile unit and aligns with the project’s current design (the MacBook
Pro 2019 dev machine likely has a similar RAM size, for reference).
External  GPU  (eGPU)  and  Docking: Thanks  to  the  dual  USB4  ports,  the  Legion  Go  has  the
uncommon ability (for a handheld) to connect to external PCIe devices. This means in a stationary
setting, one could attach an eGPU enclosure via USB4/Thunderbolt, allowing a desktop-class GPU to
drive graphics or GPU-accelerated computations. This is more of a hypothetical enhancement – it
hasn’t been widely reported in reviews – but USB4 theoretically supports Thunderbolt 3/4 devices, so
an eGPU might work (bandwidth would be limited compared to internal GPU slots, but still a huge
boost for tasks like 3D rendering or machine learning that can use a powerful GPU). Even without an
eGPU,  the  ports  allow  connecting  to  multiple  external  displays  and  peripherals  easily.  Lenovo
specifically positioned one USB-C on the top and one on the bottom so that docking and charging
scenarios are very flexible
. For example, you can prop the Legion Go on its kickstand, plug in
power to the bottom USB-C, and plug the top USB-C into a monitor or hub – turning it into a
makeshift desktop. For the project, this means during development or presentations, the Legion Go
can act as a computer powering large screens or specialized equipment, and then be quickly
undocked and taken on the go.
Software Updates and OS Optimization: Since the unit is running Windows 11 Insider Preview,
it’s likely receiving the latest fixes and features for handheld devices. Microsoft has been improving
Windows support for devices like these (e.g. better on-screen keyboard, UI scaling, game mode
improvements for handhelds). Keeping the OS updated will ensure any  early bugs (and Lenovo’s
own software bugs) get patched – early reviews noted that the Legion Go’s software (the Legion
Space app, etc.) still had some issues at launch
. It’s wise to update Lenovo’s drivers/firmware as
well, as they have already pushed a few updates to refine fan curves and controller behavior.
Additionally, for project-specific optimization, one can  trim unnecessary background apps on
Windows, use power profiles, and ensure that the AI software services start on boot and are
configured for a touchscreen interface, etc., to make the device as user-friendly and responsive as
possible in the field.
In summary, the Legion Go is quite capable out of the box, but the above tweaks can tailor its performance
and functionality to better suit the Monkey Head Project’s needs. Adjusting power settings gives control
over  performance  vs.  battery  life,  undervolting  and  improved  cooling  can  eke  out  more  sustained
performance, and the hardware’s inherent expandability (ports/storage) provides avenues to integrate with
larger setups or handle more data. All these modifications and improvements ensure that the Legion Go
can reliably function as a portable AI hub – delivering high performance when required and conserving
energy when portability is paramount.
