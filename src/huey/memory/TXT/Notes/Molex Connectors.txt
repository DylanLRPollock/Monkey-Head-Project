## 1. Introduction

Molex Incorporated is one of the world's leading manufacturers of electronic connectors, with a product portfolio of over 100,000 interconnect products serving industries from data communications and consumer electronics to automotive and medical devices. Founded in 1938 in Illinois, USA, the company has grown over 80+ years into a global supplier and is consistently ranked among the top connector companies by annual revenue. Molex is especially renowned for pioneering the Molex connector – a two-piece pin-and-socket plug introduced in the mid-20th century – which achieved universal adoption in personal computers and other electronics.

**Note on Terminology:** The term "Molex connector" in common usage often refers specifically to a 4-pin 5.25″ drive power connector used in PCs, though Molex as a company produces a vast range of connector types. This document will clarify this terminology and explore the breadth of Molex's connector innovations.

## 2. Historical Background

### 2.1 Founding and Early Years (1938–1950s)

Molex was established in **1938** by **Frederick August Krehbiel** as the Molex Products Company, originally not as an electronics firm but as a manufacturer of plastic molding compounds and consumer items. The company's very name "Molex" comes from a proprietary plastic material developed by Krehbiel in the 1930s – a composite of coal tar pitch, limestone, and asbestos fibers – which was found to be an excellent electrical insulator.

In its early years, Molex used this material to produce household products like flower pots, salt shakers, and clock cases. By the 1940s, as World War II drove demand for plastics, Molex's insulating material gained attention because it was not subject to wartime rationing (unlike many standard plastics). This positioned the company advantageously to explore new product lines in the electrical domain.

### 2.2 Post-War Shift to Connectors (1950s–1960s)

After WWII, under the leadership of Frederick's son **John H. Krehbiel**, Molex identified a growing market for electrical connectors. The company shifted focus from just molding materials to designing interconnection devices, marking a pivotal transition from a materials company to a connector company.

| Year | Milestone |
|------|-----------|
| **1953** | Molex launched its first plug-and-receptacle connector line, targeting the consumer appliance industry |
| **1960** | Molex introduced its first connectors made of nylon, signifying full evolution into an electronics connector manufacturer |
| **1962** | Annual sales exceeded $1 million, driven largely by connector products in consumer electronics |

Molex's breakthrough came in the late 1950s and 1960s by supplying connectors to the fast-growing consumer electronics market. Notably, Molex developed pin-and-socket connector designs for use in **color televisions**, which were then an emerging technology. These connectors, designed by John Krehbiel, linked electronic components inside TV sets and were adopted by major TV manufacturers like Zenith, RCA, and Magnavox.

### 2.3 Global Expansion and Growth (1970s–1990s)

| Year | Development |
|------|-------------|
| **1970** | First overseas factory opened in Yamato City, Japan |
| **1972** | Molex went public |
| **1980** | Acquired Cardell Corporation (automotive connectors) |
| **1993** | Second-largest connector company worldwide (trailing only AMP Incorporated) |
| **1990s** | Operated more than 40 plants in 19 countries |

### 2.4 21st Century Developments

In the 2000s, Molex continued to thrive by focusing on high-performance connector solutions and niche innovations. The company invested heavily in R&D (routinely over 5% of annual revenue) to develop new connector families for emerging needs such as high-speed data communication and mobile devices.

**Verification:** By 2013, Molex had over **45,000 employees** and **$3.6 billion in annual revenue**. That year, **Koch Industries** acquired Molex for **$7.2 billion**, though Molex retained its name and focus on connectors.

**Current Status (2025):** Molex has approximately **42,000 employees** and **74 plants** located in **19 countries**, supporting a portfolio of more than **100,000 products**.

## 3. Technical Overview of Molex Connectors

### 3.1 Pin-and-Socket Design

The archetypal Molex connector, as developed in the late 1950s, features cylindrical spring-metal pins that fit into matching spring-metal sockets. Both pins and sockets are held in a rectangular insulating housing, and the connectors are usually polarized/keyed to prevent incorrect orientation.

| Series | Pin Diameter | Current Rating | Pitch |
|--------|-------------|----------------|-------|
| **0.093" series** | ~2.36 mm | ~8.5 A | 5.08 mm |
| **0.062" series** | ~1.57 mm | ~5 A | — |
| **KK series** | — | 5 A | 2.54 mm |
| **Mini-Fit Jr.** | — | 9–13 A | 4.20 mm |
| **Micro-Fit 3.0** | — | 5 A | 3.00 mm |
| **PicoBlade** | — | 1 A | 1.25 mm |

**Known Issues:** The classic friction-fit Molex connectors could be difficult to disconnect due to tight fit and lack of a release lever, often requiring significant force to unplug. Additionally, if the female socket contacts lost tension or the male pins were misaligned, the connection could loosen over time, leading to contact resistance and electrical arcing (visible as browning or melting of the nylon housing in severe cases).

### 3.2 Common Molex Connector Families

#### KK™ Series (Modular Connectors)

The KK series is a family of **low-power board-to-board and wire-to-board connectors** with a typical pitch of **2.54 mm (0.100")**. Introduced in the 1950s, Molex KK connectors became a "building block" system that could be configured in various pin counts. These are widely used for signal connections on PCBs, in everything from home appliances to hobbyist electronics.

**Specifications:** KK 254: 2.54 mm pitch, 250V, 5A.

#### Mini-Fit Jr.® Series

This is a family of **medium-power connectors** with a pitch of **4.20 mm**, featuring fully isolated rectangular pins and positive locking via latch tabs.

**Verification:** Mini-Fit Jr. was **first developed in the 1980s**, with current capability of up to **12 amperes per line**. It is the connector system used in ATX PC power supply cables – the 20/24-pin main motherboard power connector is a 24-pin Mini-Fit Jr. plug.

**ATX Specification:** Intel's ATX spec explicitly references Molex part numbers:
- **Housing:** 24 Pin Molex Mini-Fit Jr. PN# 39-01-2240 or equivalent
- **Motherboard connector:** Molex 44206-0007 or equivalent
- **Wire gauge:** 18 AWG suggested for all wires except +3.3V and sense return (22 AWG)

#### Disk Drive Power Connectors (Molex 8981 Series)

Among Molex's most famous products is the **4-pin power plug** used historically for computer disk drives and peripherals (often just called "the Molex connector" by PC enthusiasts). This connector, originating in the late 1970s, has a **5.08 mm pitch** with **0.093″ diameter contacts** providing 5V and 12V DC power and ground.

**Historical Note:** The first versions of this connector used in PCs were based on the AMP/Tyco Mate-N-Lok series introduced in 1963. Molex later produced a compatible version (Series 8981, trademarked as the **Disk Drive Power Connection System**) in 1983.

**Standard Configuration:**
- 4-pin arrangement in a row
- Friction fit (no latch)
- +5V (red wire), +12V (yellow wire), two ground wires (black)

#### Micro-Fit 3.0™ Series

As the need arose for connectors smaller than Mini-Fit but still capable of moderate power (up to ~5A per circuit), Molex introduced the **Micro-Fit family with a 3.00 mm pitch**. Micro-Fit connectors are high-density, dual-row connectors commonly used in compact PCs, robotics, and industrial control for both power and signal.

**Robotics Application:** Teams have used Micro-Fit 3.0 connectors for sensor and actuator wiring because of their balance of size and current capacity.

#### Microminiature and Specialty Connectors

| Family | Pitch | Application |
|--------|-------|-------------|
| **PicoBlade™** | 1.25 mm | Low-current signal connections in consumer gadgets, laptops, wearables |
| **SlimStack®** | 0.35 mm | Board-to-board connectors in smartphones |
| **MX150™** | 1.50 mm | Sealed automotive connectors; up to 22A, 125–150°C |
| **Mini50™** | 0.50 mm | Industry's first automotive-grade 0.50mm terminal interface; 50% smaller than 0.64mm connectors |

**Verification (MX150):** The MX150 series conforms to USCAR specifications and uses a mat-seal design (integral seals) to achieve environmental sealing. It can operate up to **150°C** and carry up to **22A per circuit**.

**Verification (Mini50):** Mini50 connectors achieve **50% space savings** over traditional USCAR 0.64mm connectors with sealed or unsealed single- and dual-row receptacles.

## 4. Application Domains

### 4.1 Robotics

In robotics, reliability and robustness of electrical connections are critical. Robots typically have numerous actuators (motors, servos), sensors, and control boards interconnected by cables – all of which must withstand motion, vibration, and sometimes harsh conditions.

| Application | Molex Connector | Specifications |
|-------------|-----------------|----------------|
| **High-current power** | Mini-Fit Sr.™ | 12 AWG, >20A, secure locking |
| **Lower-current sensors** | Micro-Fit 3.0 | 5A per circuit |
| **Signal wiring** | SL™ series | 22–30 AWG, locking housings |
| **Industrial sensors** | Brad® line | Rugged circular connectors |
| **Compact devices** | PicoBlade™ | 1.25 mm pitch |

**Verification (FIRST Robotics):** FIRST Robotics Competition teams use Molex SL for CAN and low-power DC connections, and Mini-Fit Jr. for power runs. Molex has introduced an SL series kit containing standardized connectors for PWM signal cables, CAN bus connections, and pre-crimped leads for teams without professional crimp tools.

### 4.2 Consumer Electronics and Computing

Molex connectors have long been ubiquitous in consumer electronics – personal computing devices, home entertainment equipment, appliances, and mobile devices.

| Era | Connector | Application |
|-----|-----------|-------------|
| **1980s** | 6-pin Molex (P8/P9) | IBM PC PSU-to-motherboard |
| **1990s–present** | Mini-Fit Jr. 20/24-pin | ATX main power connector |
| **1980s–2000s** | 4-pin Molex (8981) | Disk drives, peripherals |
| **2000s–present** | KK series | Motherboard headers, fans |
| **Modern** | SlimStack, PicoBlade | Smartphones, wearables |

**Standardization:** Intel's ATX specification explicitly references Molex part numbers for the main power connector and peripheral power connectors as approved reference designs. The consistency and reliability of these connectors helped ensure interoperability – any ATX power supply could plug into any ATX motherboard because they all used the same Molex Mini-Fit Jr. interface.

### 4.3 Automotive

The automotive industry presents one of the most demanding environments for connectors: extreme temperatures, moisture, vibration, and safety-critical reliability requirements.

| Connector | Key Specifications | Application |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------|
| **MX150™** | Sealed, 22A, 150°C, USCAR-compliant | Engine compartments, lighting |
| **Mini50™** | 0.50 mm terminal, 50% smaller | Infotainment, ADAS sensors |
| **HSAutoLink™** | High-speed data | USB, LVDS camera links, Ethernet |
| **stAK50h** | Sealed, high-density | LED headlight systems |

**Verification:** Molex's Mini50 connector system is the **industry's first automotive-grade connector with a 0.50 mm terminal interface**, achieving roughly 50% smaller footprint than traditional 0.64 mm connectors. The MX150 series provides roughly **37% space savings** over older 1.50 mm pitch connectors without sacrificing current handling.

## 5. Legacy Status of Molex Connectors

### 5.1 The 4-Pin Molex Power Plug in PCs

For decades, the white 4-pin Molex plug was the standard way to supply disk drives and fans with 5V and 12V from a PC power supply. However, with the advent of the **Serial ATA (SATA) interface** in the mid-2000s, a new power connector was introduced.

| Feature | 4-Pin Molex | SATA Power |
|---------|-------------|------------|
| **Pins** | 4 | 15 |
| **Voltages** | 5V, 12V | 3.3V, 5V, 12V |
| **Hot-swap** | No | Yes (staggered pins) |
| **Latch** | None (friction fit) | Keyed, slim design |
| **Size** | Bulky | Slim wafer-style |

**Verification:** The Serial ATA standard provides for the adoption of a new 15-pin drive power connector, replacing the ubiquitous 4-pin Molex connector. The Molex connector provides only 5 and 12 VDC power, while some SATA drives require the additional 3.3 VDC power provided for in the SATA specification.

Today, the 4-pin Molex is widely considered a **legacy connector** in the PC world. Its design limitations (no latch, stiff to unplug, bulky shape) and the lack of a 3.3V supply made it less suitable for newer devices. However, PC power supply manufacturers continue to include a few Molex outputs on most units, primarily for compatibility with older accessories. Adapters are readily available (e.g., Molex-to-SATA power adapters) to bridge old and new.

### 5.2 Other Legacy Contexts

| Context | Legacy Connector | Replacement |
|---------|-----------------|-------------|
| **Mobile devices** | 2.54 mm KK | JST-PH, micro-coax, flat flex |
| **Telecom backplane** | VMEbus, CompactPCI | High-speed modular connectors |
| **Automotive diagnostics** | Proprietary OBD connectors | OBD-II standard |

### 5.3 Why Connectors Become Legacy

A connector becomes "legacy" when:
1. **Technical limitations** impede new requirements (e.g., no hot-swap, missing voltage rail)
2. **A superior standardized alternative** emerges with broad industry support
3. **Ergonomics and safety** improvements make newer connectors more foolproof

Yet, "legacy" doesn't mean "vanished." Molex and other manufacturers continue to produce legacy connectors for the aftermarket and replacement parts. Mature connector products offer advantages like assured long-term availability and widespread distributor support.

## 6. Conclusion

From its origin as a maker of plastic molding compounds in the 1930s to its status today as a global leader in electronic interconnects, Molex's journey is a remarkable case of technological adaptation and influence. The development of Molex's connector technologies closely parallels the evolution of electronics over the past seventy years.

The applications surveyed – robotics, consumer electronics, and automotive – demonstrate the breadth of scenarios where Molex connectors are employed. In each domain, certain Molex products became standard solutions, underscoring how the company's focus on reliability and ease-of-use resonated with engineers.

The legacy of Molex connectors is twofold: **technological** (embodied in the connectors themselves, many of which became industry benchmarks) and **cultural** (the very word "Molex" entered the lexicon as a synonym for a certain type of connector). Even as specific Molex connectors become legacy, the company's influence persists in the connectors that replace them.
