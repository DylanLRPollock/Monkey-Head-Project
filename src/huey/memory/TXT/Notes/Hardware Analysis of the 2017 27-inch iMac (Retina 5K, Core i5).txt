Hardware Analysis of the 2017 27-inch iMac
(Retina 5K, Core i5)
Introduction: This report provides a detailed analysis of the hardware configuration and performance of a
2017 27-inch Apple iMac with Retina 5K display, equipped with a 7th-generation Intel Core i5 CPU, 48 GB of
DDR4 RAM, a 1 TB Fusion Drive, and a discrete AMD Radeon Pro GPU with 4 GB VRAM. We examine the
technical specifications of each major component and discuss their implications for software performance
under high loads and a modern operating system (macOS Ventura). We also compare this iMac’s hardware
to contemporaries and consider upgradeability, thermal design, and other engineering trade-offs in Apple’s
all-in-one design. 
1. CPU Specifications (Intel Core i5, 7th-Gen “Kaby Lake”)
The 27″ iMac (Mid-2017) introduced Intel’s 7th-generation Kaby Lake processors. In this configuration, the
Core i5 is a quad-core desktop-class CPU (LGA 1151 socket) running at around 3.4–3.5 GHz base frequency
with Turbo Boost up to ~4.0 GHz (exact clocks vary by the specific i5 model)
. All 27″ models use Kaby
Lake chips built on Intel’s refined 14 nm+ process, which delivered higher clock speeds and efficiency over
the previous Skylake generation
. Architecturally, Kaby Lake’s CPU core is essentially identical to Skylake’s
(same microarchitecture and pipeline)
, so per-clock performance is similar. The main improvements
came from the process tweak (allowing higher turbo frequencies) and an upgraded media engine. Notably,
Kaby Lake added fixed-function hardware acceleration for modern video codecs – it can fully decode and
encode 10-bit 4K HEVC (H.265) and VP9 video in hardware, whereas Skylake relied on a hybrid CPU/GPU
approach for those tasks
. This means the i5-7XXX in the iMac can handle 4K HDR video playback and
encoding more efficiently, which benefits video editing, streaming, and other media-heavy workloads on
macOS Ventura (for example, smooth HEVC video playback and faster exports in apps that use Quick Sync
video). 
In terms of raw performance, the quad-core i5 (which lacks Intel’s Hyper-Threading) is a solid mid-range
processor for its time. It has 4 physical cores/4 threads and was rated with a Thermal Design Power of 65 W
(for the i5-7500/7600 models)
. Under sustained heavy loads, this 65 W TDP fits within the iMac’s cooling
capabilities better than the optional Core i7-7700K upgrade (a 4-core/8-thread chip with a 91 W TDP). In
practice, the Core i5 can run up to ~3.8–4.0 GHz on one core (during bursty single-thread tasks) and around
3.5–3.6 GHz across all cores under multi-threaded workloads
, as long as temperatures are kept in check.
This yields strong single-core performance and decent multi-core throughput, though it will be outpaced in
highly parallel tasks by newer CPUs with more cores. 
Performance Metrics: In contemporary benchmarks, the 3.4 GHz Core i5-7500 iMac scores on the order of
4,000 in Geekbench 4 single-core and ~13,000–14,000 multi-core. (In Geekbench 5, which uses a different
scale, the same i5 scores around 960 single-core and 3,100 multi-core
.) For comparison, a high-end 2017
iMac configured with the Core i7-7700K scored about 19,600 in Geekbench 4 multi-core, significantly
outperforming other all-in-one PCs of that era like Microsoft’s Surface Studio (13,197) or Dell’s XPS 27
(14,586)
. Even in single-threaded tasks, the Kaby Lake i5/i7 chips held a strong advantage over older pro

Thermal Design and Impact: The Core i5’s 65 W TDP means it dissipates substantially less heat than the

full  load

throttling, so Ventura can utilize the processor’s Turbo Boost frequencies more consistently for bursty tasks.

are somewhat more subdued compared to the hotter i7 configuration. Overall, the 7th-gen Core i5, while no
longer cutting-edge, remains a capable workhorse for many computing tasks on Ventura, benefiting from
its strong single-thread performance and sufficient multi-threaded power for everyday pro applications. Its
main limitation in 2025 is simply core count – with only 4 cores, it can become the bottleneck in heavily
parallel workflows or when running many virtual machines/containers. In such scenarios, users may notice
the CPU saturating at 100% usage, and the system will rely on macOS’s scheduling and perhaps App Nap
features to maintain responsiveness. But for most uses that an all-in-one like this targets (productivity, light
content creation, gaming, etc.), the Core i5 holds up decently. Modern software is increasingly optimized for
multi-core, yet also for heterogeneous computing (offloading work to the GPU, etc.), which helps extend the
useful life of a quad-core CPU like this in running the latest macOS and apps. 
2. Memory (48 GB DDR4 RAM Configuration)
This iMac is configured with 48 GB of RAM, which is a large amount of memory for a system of this era (and
well above Apple’s factory default). All 27-inch Mid-2017 iMacs use 2400 MHz DDR4 SDRAM and feature four
SO-DIMM slots that are user-accessible via a small rear hatch
. Apple shipped these iMacs with 8 GB by
default (2×4 GB modules) and officially supported configurations up to 32 GB or 64 GB depending on the
model tier
. Notably, the base 3.4 GHz Core i5 model was only configurable to 32 GB on Apple’s online
store, whereas the higher models advertised up to 64 GB – but in reality all 27″ 2017 iMacs can accept 64 GB
(4×16 GB) if the user installs compatible modules
. Retailer tests (e.g. by OWC) confirmed 64 GB works
flawlessly
. In this case, 48 GB likely consists of two 16 GB sticks added to the two original 8 GB sticks (for
16+8 GB in each memory channel). This configuration operates in dual-channel mode and at the full
2400 MT/s speed, as the iMac’s memory controller can run mismatched-capacity DIMMs in a symmetric
fashion (equal total size per channel). There is no significant performance penalty for using 16+8 vs.
identical pairs, aside from the general caveat that all DIMMs should be of the same speed rating (which they
are, in this scenario). 
Type and Upgradeability: The iMac uses notebook-style SO-DIMM DDR4 modules, running at 2400 MHz
with likely CL17 timing (as provided by Apple). Being user-replaceable is a huge advantage for longevity:

Speed and Real-World Impact: The 2400 MT/s DDR4 memory provides about 19 GB/s of theoretical
bandwidth per channel (the iMac has two memory channels). This is an upgrade from the previous-gen
iMac (which used 1867 MT/s DDR3) and helps feed the quad-core CPU and discrete GPU with data more
effectively. In real-world performance, the jump to DDR4 and higher capacity primarily affects heavy
multitasking and professional workflows: with 48 GB, the system can cache large portions of active data in
RAM. macOS Ventura will take advantage of abundant RAM by caching filesystem data (via the Unified
Buffer Cache) and keeping applications resident, resulting in snappier reopening of apps and files. For tasks
like compiling large codebases, processing high-resolution images or video, or running analysis on big
datasets, having tens of gigabytes of RAM means the iMac seldom needs to write to its slow HDD-based
swap. This dramatically improves the user experience under high load – even if CPU or disk are busy, at
least memory is not a limiting factor. In fact, memory pressure in Activity Monitor will likely stay green for all
but the most extreme use cases on this 48 GB system. 
For example, a user doing professional photo editing can load hundreds of RAW images into memory or
open very large multi-layer Photoshop documents without hitting RAM limits. Video editors can cache more
4K footage for real-time scrubbing. Developers can run multiple Docker containers or a large IDE with
indexing, all in RAM. The iMac’s memory throughput is sufficient to handle such scenarios, though the CPU
or disk might become bottlenecks first. It’s also worth noting that the 2017 iMac’s RAM is non-ECC (as it’s a
consumer Core i5 platform), which is typical and generally fine for most users; only the iMac Pro/Mac Pro
lines use ECC for error-resistant memory. The absence of ECC isn’t an issue for software performance per
se, but in extremely critical applications (scientific computing, etc.) it’s a distinction from workstation-class
hardware. 
Memory and Ventura: macOS Ventura itself has some increased memory footprint compared to older
macOS versions, thanks to more background processes and features (for instance, the Window Manager’s
Stage Manager feature, more iCloud syncing processes, etc.). With 48 GB, however, the OS and background
services will consume only a small fraction, leaving the vast majority free for user applications and caching.
This means Ventura can fully leverage the available RAM to offset the slower Fusion Drive by caching
frequently accessed files and even entire applications in memory. The result is that everyday operations feel
smooth: application switching is instant and beachballs are minimized, so long as tasks fit in RAM. In
summary, the 48 GB memory configuration is a significant boon for this iMac’s performance and longevity –
it ensures that memory will rarely be the limiting factor, even as modern software continues to demand
more RAM. Upgrading the RAM is also the easiest and most effective user improvement one can make on
these iMacs, and in this system it has already been maxed out to a high capacity.

3. Fusion Drive Storage (1 TB Hybrid SSD/HDD)
The 2017 iMac under review uses Apple’s  1 TB Fusion Drive, a hybrid storage system that combines a
traditional spinning hard disk drive with a small solid-state drive acting as a cache. Specifically, the 1 TB
Fusion Drive in this model consists of a 1 TB 7200 RPM HDD paired with a 32 GB NVMe SSD on a custom
Apple PCIe blade
. (Apple’s documentation at the time noted that “The 1TB Fusion Drive pairs a 1TB hard
drive with 32GB of fast SSD”, whereas the larger 2 TB and 3 TB Fusion variants included a 128 GB flash
portion
.) The idea behind Fusion Drive is that the system presents a single logical volume to macOS,
and the macOS storage management software (CoreStorage in older macOS, and later an APFS volume
group) automatically migrates frequently used data to the SSD portion while shuffling less-used data to the
HDD. This tiered storage is meant to combine the speed benefits of flash with the capacity of a platter drive,
achieving a balance of performance and cost per GB. 
Performance Characteristics: In optimal conditions, the Fusion Drive behaves like a fast SSD for the most
common reads and writes. The 32 GB SSD holds the operating system files, application binaries, and
recently used documents, which means booting, app launch, and waking from sleep are typically very quick
. Indeed, Apple claimed this hybrid setup allows  “fast startup, near instant wake from sleep, and quick
application launching, with room left over for your most frequently used files and apps.”
. Small writes are
first  cached  to  the  SSD,  which  can  absorb  them  at  solid-state  speeds  and  later  flush  to  the  HDD
asynchronously. The SSD in this 2017 iMac, while only 32 GB, is a high-performance NVMe drive on PCIe 3.0
– in pure SSD configurations, these Apple drives can exceed 700–1000 MB/s throughput. In a Fusion Drive,
those speeds are realized for data that resides on the SSD portion. For example, an initial file transfer of,
say, a 4–5 GB file might complete at hundreds of MB/s (since it fits in the SSD cache). In fact, iMac models
equipped with pure SSDs were measured duplicating a 5 GB file in ~7 seconds (~710 MB/s)
, and a Fusion
Drive would show similar speed  until the SSD cache is saturated. The challenging scenario is when the
workload exceeds the SSD cache or involves non-cached, random access to data on the hard disk. In those
cases, the performance drops to that of the 1 TB 3.5″ HDD, which has an average transfer rate on the order
of 150 MB/s (with perhaps ~200 MB/s on the outer tracks sequentially, down to ~100 MB/s on inner tracks),
and millisecond-level seek latency that is orders of magnitude slower than an SSD. 
Under macOS Ventura and APFS, the Fusion Drive is managed such that the SSD is always filled first with
system files and recent data. But if, for instance, the user has hundreds of gigabytes of applications and
files, the 32 GB SSD can only cache a small fraction. Large files or infrequently accessed data sitting on the
HDD will incur noticeable delays when accessed. For example, loading a huge video project that doesn’t fit
entirely on the SSD may result in a few seconds of spinning beachball as the HDD seeks. Likewise, any burst
of writes larger than 32 GB will eventually spill to the HDD and slow down. Real-world benchmarks and user
experiences reflect this dichotomy: the Fusion Drive makes everyday tasks feel SSD-like, but heavy disk I/O
(such as importing a very large photo library, or working with ultra-large datasets) can hit HDD bottlenecks.
In a professional context, 32 GB of flash is quite limited – many power users eventually feel the HDD’s drag
when working with media files that go beyond that cache. In fact, by 2020, Apple themselves phased out
Fusion Drives in favor of pure SSDs in new iMacs, acknowledging that modern workflows benefit from all-
flash storage.
Potential Bottlenecks: The Fusion Drive is often cited as the weakest link in this iMac’s performance. A
number of 2017 iMac owners observed that macOS runs  notably slower on Fusion Drives versus an SSD,
especially as the OS has evolved. One reason is that macOS Ventura (and recent macOS versions) make
heavy use of disk for things like maintaining snapshots, indexing with Spotlight, and swapping memory

operations and even impact the responsiveness of the OS (since macOS uses unified memory and disk
caching, it might wait on disk I/O). Ventura’s APFS file system is optimized for SSDs and issues a lot of small,
frequent writes (for things like snapshots, etc.), which spinning disks handle poorly. The result can be
increased drive thrashing on the HDD. The ample 48 GB of RAM in this iMac does mitigate some of this –
macOS will cache as much data in RAM as possible, reducing how often it has to hit the disk. In many cases,
the system will feel okay as long as active working sets fit in memory or in the SSD tier. But a prolonged
heavy task (say, rendering a video that produces tens of GBs of temp files, or copying a huge file set) will
eventually show the HDD’s slower performance. This is where one might see export jobs take longer or app
launches slow down if their assets aren’t on the SSD portion. 
Overall Assessment: The Fusion Drive was a clever solution in 2012–2017 to offer users large storage
without the then-prohibitive cost of big SSDs. In 2025, however, a 1 TB HDD is a clear performance
bottleneck by modern standards. For intensive professional use, many recommend upgrading to a pure
SSD. Indeed, some iMac owners have either replaced the internal HDD with a SATA SSD (using a thermal
sensor cable kit) or more commonly, boot macOS from an external Thunderbolt 3 SSD to bypass the Fusion
Drive entirely
. Doing so yields a dramatic improvement: the same iMac that felt sluggish on Fusion Drive
can feel “awesome” and very responsive when running on an all-flash disk
. Still, if one works mostly with
a fixed set of apps and files that the 32 GB SSD can accommodate, the Fusion Drive will deliver a reasonably
fast experience – it’s those edge cases of large or unpredictable I/O where it falters. In summary, the Fusion
Drive provides a functional but compromised storage environment: it offers far better performance than a
lone  1   TB  HDD  for  everyday  use  (thanks  to  caching)
,  but  it  cannot  match  the  consistent  high
performance of a full SSD, and it can become a choke point under heavy I/O load on macOS Ventura. Users
must be mindful of this, perhaps moving their most I/O-intensive workflows to external SSDs or upgrading
the internal storage for sustained high performance. 
4. GPU Analysis (AMD Radeon Pro 570/575 with 4 GB GDDR5)

575 (4 GB) by default

GCN), specifically using the “Ellesmere” GPU die also found in desktop Radeon RX 470/480 series. The
Pro 580 (which came with the high-end i7 iMac) is the fully enabled chip with 36 compute units and 8 GB

Graphics Architecture and Capabilities: Being Polaris-based, the Radeon Pro supports modern graphics

and effects to the Radeon Pro. The 4 GB VRAM, while not huge, is sufficient for driving the built-in 5K display

simultaneously  powering  the  internal  5120×2880  display  at  60   Hz  (with  10-bit  color)  and up  to  one
additional 5K external display (5120×2880 @60 Hz) or two 4K UHD displays (3840×2160 @60 Hz) via its
Thunderbolt 3 ports
. This was an impressive output capability at the time, enabled by DisplayPort 1.4
HBR3  bandwidth  over  USB-C/Thunderbolt   3

professional setups without issue, provided the GPU’s performance is up to the task of driving those pixels. 
In  terms  of  real-world  graphics  performance,  the  Radeon  Pro   570/575  is  a  mid-range  GPU  by  2017
standards. It excels at tasks like UI rendering (the 5K UI is smooth with Metal), video playback, and
moderately  intensive  compute  shaders.  Apple  touted  that  this  iMac  generation  “delivers  accelerated
performance with creative apps like Final Cut Pro, Adobe Premiere Pro, After Effects, and Photoshop”, even
enabling some VR content creation on Mac for the first time
. Indeed, with ~4 TFLOPs of performance,
these GPUs finally crossed the threshold for VR headset support, which Apple highlighted at WWDC 2017
(they demoed an HTC Vive running on an iMac). Metal performance benchmarks (like Geekbench 5 Metal
test) for the 4 GB Polaris Pro tend to score around 25,000–30,000 points, which is roughly in line with an
NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1650 Max-Q or GTX 1060 Mobile in compute ability. 
Professional Use and Ventura Compatibility: The Radeon Pro 570/575 fully supports macOS Ventura’s
graphics features. Ventura introduced Metal 3 with new API capabilities and upscaling technologies (e.g.
MetalFX upscaling). While Apple’s latest GPUs (in Apple Silicon) reap the most benefit, the Polaris GPU can
still leverage these to a degree. For instance, any UI effects, Mission Control animations, or GPU-accelerated
rendering tasks in Ventura are handled by this GPU. There is no compatibility issue – Apple continues to
include driver support for Polaris in macOS 13 (though it’s worth noting macOS 14 dropped support for
2017 iMacs, meaning no official drivers beyond Ventura). Day-to-day, the GPU handles the 5K desktop
flawlessly, including features like translucent materials and heavy compositing in macOS. Content creation
apps can use the GPU for timeline scrubbing and effects: Final Cut Pro X, for example, can utilize the
Radeon Pro for rendering multiple streams of 4K video with color grading in real time (4 GB VRAM is usually
enough for a couple streams of 4K, but might be limiting for 8K or very complex scenes). Similarly,
Lightroom and Photoshop use Metal to accelerate image adjustments and filters, which this GPU can do
smoothly for typical image sizes. Developers can also use the GPU via Metal or OpenCL for compute tasks
(the GPU’s 1792–2048 cores are useful for parallel computations).

firmware will ramp the fan to maintain safe temperatures and may slightly throttle the GPU clock if needed.

when pushed (like extended gaming or computing), will produce a lot of heat that the slim iMac must expel.
Expandability – eGPU: One noteworthy capability thanks to Thunderbolt 3 is the option of external GPUs.
While not a feature of the iMac’s internal hardware per se, macOS (since High Sierra 10.13.4) supports
external GPU enclosures over TB3. This means if more graphics power is needed, a user could connect an
eGPU chassis with a newer AMD card (Apple officially supports various AMD Radeon cards) to greatly boost
graphics performance for specialized tasks or gaming. The iMac would use the external GPU for workloads
while still driving the internal 5K display (with some bandwidth overhead). This extendability somewhat
future-proofs the graphics – although an eGPU cannot fix the internal GPU’s limitations for every scenario
(and  TB3’s  40   Gbps  bandwidth  bottlenecks  very  high-end  GPUs),  it  is  a  path  to  upgrade  graphical
horsepower without replacing the entire iMac. Under Ventura, eGPU support is mature, and a modern card
like a Radeon 5700 XT or 6600 XT in an eGPU box would vastly outperform the built-in Radeon Pro 570/575.
Of course, this is an external addition and not an internal upgrade, but it demonstrates the flexibility
offered by the iMac’s Thunderbolt 3 connectivity in the graphics department. 
In summary, the iMac’s Radeon Pro 4 GB GPU is a capable mid-tier graphics solution for the time: it handles
the 5K display and content creation tasks well and is fully utilized by macOS Ventura’s frameworks. It offers
entry-level discrete GPU performance by today’s measures – fine for light to moderate GPU tasks and older
games, but outpaced by modern GPUs especially for high-resolution or VR applications. Apple’s decision to
include discrete graphics in all 27″ iMacs of this generation (even the base model) means even this Core i5
iMac benefits from having a dedicated GPU as opposed to relying on Intel’s integrated graphics, which
would have been inadequate for 5K. The Radeon Pro thus significantly contributes to the machine’s ability
to handle pro workloads (like driving multiple high-res displays, accelerating pro apps, etc.). It strikes a

5. Thermal Management and Acoustics
Cooling a quad-core CPU and a discrete GPU inside the slim enclosure of the 27″ iMac is a non-trivial
engineering challenge. Apple’s thermal design for the 2017 Retina 5K iMac consists of a single large blower
fan, heat sinks with heatpipes attached to the CPU and GPU, and carefully directed airflow within the
chassis. Cool air is drawn in through the vents along the bottom edge of the iMac, flows over the logic
board components (CPU, GPU, voltage regulators, etc.), and warm air is exhausted out of a wide vent slit
along the top rear of the case. The blower fan is centrally mounted and connects to ducts – one leading
across the CPU’s heat sink and one across the GPU’s heat sink – so that a single fan can cool both major
chips
. The 27″ iMac’s fan is larger and more effective than the dual-fan setup used in the later iMac
Pro (the iMac Pro had two smaller fans), but the Pro’s overall cooling capacity was greater due to larger
heatsinks and lower target acoustics. The iMac’s internal layout is very compact, with the components
tightly integrated behind the display. There isn’t much empty space – the two speakers flank the logic board
at the sides, the HDD (if present) sits below the logic board, and the fan and power supply occupy the
center-bottom area.

Efficiency Under Load: The thermal management in this iMac favors quiet operation until temperatures
really climb. That means for short bursts or mild usage, the fan might not ramp up much at all, letting the
CPU/GPU run hot briefly (in turbo frequencies) and then cool off. For sustained loads, the control curve will
increase fan RPM to prevent throttling. The Core i5 CPU, as mentioned, is easier to cool than the i7 – it
produces less heat, so the iMac can often keep the i5 at high clock speeds without maxing the fan
immediately. By contrast, users noted that an i7-7700K iMac triggers higher fan speeds with relatively lower
CPU usage; one report mentioned the i7’s fans starting to audibly ramp at ~35% load and hitting maximum
by ~50% load

to remove ~100 W of heat from the GPU; it will get loud, but the CPU stays cooler so it might not throttle the
CPU at all. The combined worst case (both CPU and GPU at 100% for long periods) is relatively rare in typical
use, but in that scenario the iMac will definitely be running its fan at full and may still let component temps
hover in the 80–90 °C range. The system firmware will throttle clocks only if necessary to avoid exceeding
safe thermal envelopes. There is anecdotal evidence that the 2017 iMac’s GPU can throttle slightly after
extended gaming sessions (reducing core frequency a bit to stay below ~85–90 °C), but in professional
applications like video exports, usually the limiting factor is either the fan noise one can tolerate or the time
of computation, rather than abrupt performance drop-offs. 
Noise Profile: Under typical office loads (browsing, coding, watching videos), the iMac is effectively silent.
The fan noise becomes evident only under heavier continuous loads. When it does ramp up, the noise is
described as a steady “air rush” sound – there are no high-pitched whines or rattles if the fan is clean and in
good condition. Apple’s use of a large single fan means the noise is a lower-frequency whoosh rather than
multiple smaller fans which might produce a buzz. At maximum speed, it’s certainly audible in a quiet room
(~40–45 dB). Some content creators found this bothersome if, for instance, doing audio work (the iMac Pro
was later introduced with a quieter cooling for exactly that reason – “…the iMac Pro is noticeably quieter”
under load, making it ideal for audio production

intrusive when doing tasks like batch exporting photos or running games, so one might avoid placing the
iMac’s back close to a wall that reflects sound, etc., to mitigate perceived loudness. It’s worth noting that as
dust accumulates in the cooling fins over years, fans may ramp up more aggressively – maintenance like
cleaning out dust (which unfortunately requires opening the machine) can restore some cooling efficiency.
Some  power-users  also  use  macOS  fan  control  utilities  to  set  a  more  aggressive  fan  curve  to  keep
temperatures lower (at the expense of more noise upfront), or conversely to keep fans quieter and allow
higher temps (not generally recommended, but possible).

6. Display Specifications and Quality (Retina 5K Panel)
One of the crown jewels of the 27-inch iMac is its Retina 5K display. The 2017 model features a 27-inch
(diagonal) IPS panel with an ultra-high resolution of  5120 × 2880 pixels – that’s over 14.7 million pixels,
yielding a pixel density around 218 ppi on a 27″ screen
. At typical viewing distances (~50 cm), pixels are
essentially indiscernible, making text and graphics extremely sharp. Apple was the first to market with a 5K
display  in  2014,  and  by  2017  this  panel  was  refined  further.  Notably,  Apple  increased  the  display’s
brightness to 500 nits (candelas per square meter) on this generation
, which is exceptionally bright for
a  desktop  monitor  (many  standard  monitors  are  ~300  nits).  This  allows  for  better  visibility  in  bright
environments and provides a foundation for handling high dynamic range content (though the iMac’s panel
is not a true HDR monitor by modern standards, due to limited zone dimming and contrast). The display
also supports wide color gamut (DCI-P3), meaning it can reproduce a much broader range of colors than
the sRGB gamut
. Apple switched to P3 panels in the 2015 iMac, and in practice this wide gamut covers
about 99% of the DCI-P3 space and ~25% more colors than sRGB – especially richer reds and greens. This is
a significant benefit for professional visual work, as it aligns with the color space used in digital cinema and
high-end photography. 
Importantly, the iMac (2017) supports 10-bit color depth (1.07 billion colors) for the internal display. Apple
enabled support for 10-bit per channel color in macOS around this time for compatible GPUs. The tech
specs note support for “one billion colors” on the internal display
, indicating that the GPU is driving the
panel with a 10-bit signal (likely using 8-bit + FRC dithering on the panel itself, as the LG-made panel is an 8-
bit+FRC IPS). For professional imaging, this means smoother gradients and the ability to accurately display
content with very fine color distinctions (no banding in 10-bit video, for example).

Use  in  Professional  Visual  Work: This  display  was  and  remains  highly  regarded  among  creative

applied since 2015 – while the screen is glossy, the coating cuts most mirror-like reflections and diffuse
reflection levels are low. In a normal studio environment, reflections or glare are minimal, but in very bright
direct light you’ll still see some reflectivity. The high brightness helps combat any ambient light washing out
the image.

might still keep it for tasks or consider workarounds (some enthusiasts have physically modded the panel
into an external monitor housing, but that’s beyond normal use).

7. I/O and Expansion Ports
The 2017 27″ iMac offers a robust set of  I/O ports and connectivity options, befitting a professional
desktop. All the ports are situated on the lower rear right side of the iMac (when viewed from the front). The
selection includes: a 3.5 mm headphone jack, a full-size SDXC card slot (SD Card reader), four USB 3.0
Type-A ports, two Thunderbolt 3/USB-C ports, and a Gigabit Ethernet (RJ-45) jack
. There’s also a
small Kensington lock slot nearby for physical security
. This port layout was pretty advanced for 2017,
essentially the same as what Apple later carried into the 2020 iMac. The inclusion of Thunderbolt 3 (using
the USB-C connector) is particularly significant: each of the two TB3 ports provides up to 40 Gbps of
bandwidth for data and can natively support DisplayPort 1.2/1.4 for monitors, as well as USB 3.1 Gen 2
(10 Gbps) for USB-C devices
. In practical terms, the Thunderbolt 3 ports are extremely versatile –
one can connect ultra-fast external SSDs/RAID arrays, external GPUs, additional 4K/5K monitors, or daisy-
chain multiple Thunderbolt devices. Thunderbolt 3 also carries power (up to 15 W to bus-powered devices)
and can be adapted to legacy interfaces like HDMI, DVI, or Thunderbolt 2 with appropriate adapters
. 
The four USB 3.0 Type-A ports operate up to 5 Gbps each (USB 3.1 Gen 1). These are backward-compatible
with USB 2.0/1.1 for peripherals like keyboards, mice, or older devices. This is handy for connecting existing
USB-A devices (thumb drives, printers, audio interfaces, etc.) without needing dongles. The SDXC card slot
supports UHS-II speeds (in 2017, Apple quietly upgraded the 27″ iMac’s card reader to UHS-II, which means
it can take advantage of faster SD cards at up to ~300 MB/s). This is great for photographers/videographers
to quickly ingest media from cameras. The Gigabit Ethernet port supports 10/100/1000 BASE-T and auto-
negotiation; Apple did not offer a 10 Gigabit upgrade on the standard iMac (that feature was reserved for
the iMac Pro’s Nbase-T port)
. Nonetheless, 1 GbE is fine for general use and even many pro workflows
(streaming HD media off a NAS, etc.), though some might wish for faster networking for large file transfers
– an external Thunderbolt NIC could be used if needed for 10 Gb. Additionally, inside the Ethernet port on
these iMacs, Apple integrated support for Power-over-Ethernet passthrough for their accessories (though
not widely used, it can sense if an adapter is drawing power). 
The  iMac’s  wireless  connectivity  was  state-of-the-art  for  its  time:  it  has  802.11ac  Wi-Fi (3×3  MIMO,
supporting up to 1300 Mbps link rates) and Bluetooth 4.2 built-in
. This allows fast wireless networking
and peripheral connectivity (e.g., BT keyboards, mice, headphones). In 2025 terms, Wi-Fi 5 (ac) is not the
newest (Wi-Fi 6/6E are now common), but it’s still quite capable, and the iMac can easily hit a few hundred
Mbps on a good ac router. For most internet and wireless usage, this is sufficient, though those with gigabit
fiber might not fully saturate over Wi-Fi 5. Bluetooth 4.2 supports all typical peripherals; the only limitation
versus newer BT5.0+ might be range and bandwidth for certain devices, but it’s minor. The wireless card in

General OS Performance: Ventura’s UI and core experience runs well on this iMac. The system’s

the Fusion Drive, which would significantly hurt performance. Indeed, some complaints of Ventura

multi vs 19,500 on the iMac i5
, and the Vega 56 GPU is roughly 2–3× the performance of the
Radeon Pro 580. The iMac Pro also added a better cooling system (significantly quieter under load)
, 10 Gb Ethernet, and other pro features. However, it started at \$5000 and targeted a different
market (high-end video production, 3D, etc.). In terms of design, the iMac and iMac Pro looked
identical aside from color. Both had the gorgeous 5K display (exact same panel)
. So a key
differentiator was that the regular iMac, at a much lower price, was delivering the same visual
experience. The trade-off is performance headroom and noise: as mentioned, the iMac Pro could
sustain heavy loads with less noise
 and had more expansion (4 TB3 ports, etc.
), but none of
these made the standard iMac obsolete – it remained a great value for those who didn’t absolutely

teams rather than a few iMac Pros, depending on needs. 
Versus Windows All-in-Ones: The prime competitors in 2016–2017 were the  Microsoft Surface
Studio and the  Dell XPS 27 AIO (7760). The Surface Studio (1st gen, released late 2016) was a
beautifully designed 28″ all-in-one with a unique hinge (could lay down as a drafting table) and touch
screen with pen support, aimed at artists/designers. However, its internals were comparatively weak:
a mobile Core i7-6820HQ (4-core but laptop-grade, and older Skylake gen) and an NVIDIA GTX 965M
or 980M (essentially laptop GPUs from 2015). It also had only an HDD or hybrid drive, and 16 GB
RAM in base (up to 32 GB max). In benchmarks, the iMac blew the Surface Studio away – as one
review noted, the iMac i7 scored 19,588 in Geekbench 4 vs Surface Studio’s ~13,197
, and in GPU
tasks the Radeon Pro 580 was roughly on par or better than the laptop 980M (the 980M is closest to
a GTX 1060 Max-Q, which is similar to the Pro 575). So performance-wise, the iMac is significantly
faster. The Surface Studio’s strengths were its touch/pen and gorgeous 3:2 aspect ratio screen
(4500×3000). But that screen, while very nice, wasn’t 5K resolution or wide color in the first gen. It
targeted sRGB (some later update allowed a DCI-P3 mode) and had lower pixel density. So for
photographers or video editors, the iMac’s higher resolution and P3 gamut were more attractive. For
illustrators, the Surface Studio offered an interactive canvas that iMac lacks. So the choice came
down to workflow: If you needed pen input integrated, Microsoft’s offering was compelling, but you
sacrificed raw power and even some display fidelity. Microsoft updated the Studio in 2018 with
better internals (Surface Studio 2 got a GTX 1070 etc.), but by then Apple had iMac Pro or later iMac
updates. In price, the Surface Studio was very expensive (it started at \$3000 with specs much lower
than a \$3000 iMac config). So value-wise, the iMac offered more performance per dollar. 
The Dell XPS 27 (7760) was another interesting AIO competitor, released around the same time (early 2017).
The XPS 27 had a 27″ 4K touch screen (3840×2160) and was notable for a built-in 10-speaker soundbar array
(exceptional audio for an AIO, but that also made it heavy—over 38 lbs vs iMac’s ~21 lbs
). Performance-
wise, some configs of the XPS 27 had an Intel Core i7-7700 and an AMD RX 570 graphics card (desktop class
with 8 GB VRAM in one review)
. This means a top-spec XPS 27 could actually match or slightly exceed a
base iMac in CPU (i7 vs i5) and have a roughly similar GPU (Radeon RX 570 8GB vs iMac’s Pro 575 4GB). In
tasks, that XPS would do well (the i7-7700 is ~10% faster multi-core than i5-7500). However, its display was
only 4K and standard gamut, so not as sharp or color-rich as iMac’s 5K P3
. If someone prioritized touch
or needed an AIO to double as a multimedia center (the Dell’s audio was class-leading), the XPS had appeal.
But for content creation visual fidelity, iMac was better. Also, the XPS 27 was a chunkier design (the base
with speakers was large, and it lacked the slim elegance of iMac). From a cost perspective, a fully loaded XPS
27 was also in the \$2500–\$3000 range, similar to a high-end iMac. Tom’s Guide actually reviewed the iMac

2013 Mac Pro due to its last-generation high clock cores and updated I/O.

GPU, drives, etc. So at purchase time, the iMac buyer had to more or less decide their specs (with RAM
being the one thing easily changed later). This is a consideration for longevity: e.g., the soldered GPU
means one cannot upgrade graphics later as you might in a tower PC. In 2019, when newer GPUs came out
or when 8-core CPUs became mainstream, a tower PC user could drop those in, whereas an iMac user
would have to buy a new machine. Apple partially addressed that by offering eGPU support via Thunderbolt
(so one could keep the iMac and attach a new GPU externally). This actually became a popular route for
some 2017 iMac owners who wanted better gaming or 3D performance – hooking up an external Radeon
5700XT or similar can give a big boost and extend the life of the system. It’s not as optimal as an internal
GPU (some overhead on TB3), but it works. Windows AIOs generally did not have such an option (some had
Thunderbolt 3, but few promoted eGPU usage).

Value and Lifespan: The 2017 iMac’s combination of a high-end display and strong internal hardware made
it a great value among all-in-ones at the time (and arguably still now if one acquired one used). For
professionals requiring color accuracy and resolution, the iMac basically had zero competition except very
costly alternatives (like buying a Mac Pro plus a 5K monitor, which was far more expensive, or a PC plus that
rare 5K monitor). Over the subsequent years, Microsoft and others didn’t push beyond 4K in AIO displays –
even latest Surface Studio 2+ still uses the same 4500×3000 panel. Only in 2020 did Apple introduce the next
jump: the Pro Display XDR (6K resolution, very high HDR capability) but that’s a standalone \$5000 monitor.
So the 5K iMac screen remains somewhat singular in the market even many years later, aside from Apple’s
own Studio Display (5K, essentially the same panel lineage). This fact underlines that if visual quality is
paramount, the iMac was a standout versus any all-in-one or typical desktop monitor of its era. 
In head-to-head comparisons done in 2017 reviews, the iMac pretty much won categories like display
quality, overall performance, and design, while perhaps losing in niche categories like “touch input” (which
it doesn’t have) or audio (the XPS 27’s many speakers gave it an edge in sound). For example, one review
might say: iMac has best screen and speed; Surface Studio best for artists with pen; Dell XPS great sound
but lesser screen; HP Envy cheaper but much weaker, etc. So depending on a user’s priorities, they might
choose differently, but for a balanced professional need, the iMac was the top choice. 
Comparison with an Era-Similar Desktop Workstation: Let’s consider someone in 2017 looking for a
system for media production: Option A – iMac 27 (2017) around \$2500 for a nicely equipped model; Option
B – a Windows workstation with similar price. They could get maybe a dual-monitor setup with one 4K color-
accurate monitor and a second standard monitor, a Core i7 or even i9 (in 2017, i9 wasn’t out until late 2017
on X299 platform), 32 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA 1070 or Quadro. That workstation might outperform the
iMac in some tasks (NVIDIA GPUs had CUDA, beneficial for certain apps like some Adobe functions or 3D).
But it would lack the Retina pixel density unified on one screen, and likely be physically larger and noisier.
Many independent video/photo pros actually liked the iMac for being an all-in-one solution that looks sleek
in a studio or office and is quiet when not under extreme load. In contrast, some tower workstations can be
quite  loud.  The  iMac,  under  average  use,  is  nearly  silent  –  that’s  a  factor  often  overlooked  in  spec
comparisons. Only under heavy rendering did it get loud, whereas a high-power PC might have constant
fan noise. 
In summary, the 2017 iMac compared extremely well to its contemporaries. It set a high bar for all-in-one
devices  with  its  combination  of  performance  and  display,  which  competitors  struggled  to  match
simultaneously. It effectively replaced the need for a separate workstation + monitor for a large segment of
content creators, unless they needed absolute top-tier power (in which case by end of 2017 Apple offered
iMac Pro). Over time, of course, technology moved on – by 2019, Apple refreshed the 27″ iMac with 9th-gen
6-core and 8-core CPUs and slightly updated GPUs (Radeon Pro 500X series). Those offered ~50–100%
higher multi-core and maybe ~20% GPU bump. And then Apple Silicon in 2021–2022 is another leap. But
comparing within its era, the mid-2017 iMac holds up as arguably the best balanced AIO of that period.
Even  today  it  can  be  favorably  compared  to  any  integrated  solution  from  that  time.  The  only  real
disadvantage is lack of user upgrades (common to AIOs) and the typical Apple premium (though given the
display, the cost was justified). 
11. Repairability, Upgradeability, and Design Trade-offs
The 27-inch iMac’s beautiful, slim design comes with the downside of difficult serviceability and limited user
upgrades (outside of RAM). In iFixit’s teardown, the 2017 iMac earned a Repairability Score of 5 out of 10
25

------------------------------------------------------------------------
Page 26
------------------------------------------------------------------------

. This is a moderate score – not the worst (some laptops and smaller iMacs are lower) – thanks mainly to
the RAM door and modular components, but far from easily repairable. The key points: 
User-Accessible RAM: As discussed, the 27″ iMac uniquely features a rear hatch that allows quick
access to the SO-DIMM slots
. This is a major plus for upgradeability. One can install new RAM
without any tools (just need to push a latch and pop the door open). This design choice is a nod
toward professional users who often need to expand memory. It’s a trade-off Apple made – the 21.5″
iMac doesn’t have this door and has RAM buried inside, but the 27″ being larger could accommodate
it. So in terms of memory, the iMac is quite upgrade-friendly (and users can save money by buying
third-party RAM rather than Apple’s pricey upgrades). 
Modular Internals: Inside, many components are modular in the sense that they can be individually
replaced if you’ve opened the machine. The CPU is on a socket (LGA1151) and not soldered
, so
technically  one  could  upgrade  the  Core  i5  to  a  Core  i7  by  swapping  the  chip  (indeed,  some
enthusiasts have done CPU upgrades on these iMacs)
. The GPU is not in a standard slot (it’s a
custom card or soldered, depending on exact model—on 2017 iMac 27″, the GPU is on a separate
card using an MXM connector, which theoretically could be replaced with a compatible GPU board,
but such swaps are unsupported and limited to what Apple provided in that generation). The HDD
and SSD are separate modules (3.5″ SATA drive and an M.2-style Apple PCIe SSD) which can be
replaced/upgraded as well
. The power supply, fan, speakers, etc., are all discrete parts that can
be changed if one obtains the spare part. So from a theoretical repair standpoint, most major parts
are not permanently affixed to the logic board (except the GPU in some configurations might be
soldered; in 2017 27″ it’s an MXM board). iFixit noted  “components are modular and fairly easy to
remove” once you’re inside
. This is good for longevity – if, say, the HDD fails, it is possible to put in
a new drive (with the aforementioned thermal sensor cable) rather than junk the whole machine. If
the PSU fails, it can be swapped, etc. The challenge lies in getting inside. 
Adhesive-Sealed Display: Starting with the 2012 iMac redesign, Apple stopped using magnets and
instead glues the display assembly to the chassis. The glass and LCD are fused together as one
unit (which is fine – no dust can get between, and it improves image quality slightly)
, but that
entire display is then attached with strong double-sided adhesive tape. To open the iMac, one must
use a special cutting wheel or thin wire to slice through the adhesive all around the perimeter. This is
a delicate operation – you have to be careful not to cut display cables or scratch the panel. Once
opened, the old adhesive must be cleaned off and new custom-cut adhesive strips applied to reseal
the iMac during reassembly
. This process is fiddly and leaves room for error (e.g., if not sealed
perfectly, the display could come loose or dust ingress could occur). Because of this, iFixit and others
consider the iMac difficult to service. Compared to older (pre-2012) iMacs where the front glass was
held by magnets and could be popped off easily, this is much more involved. Any repair that requires
accessing the internals – hard drive replacement, logic board repair, GPU replacement – will require
going through this adhesive ordeal. For a novice, this is not advisable; most users would resort to
Apple or authorized service providers for such repairs, which can be costly. 
Risk Factors: Opening the iMac not only risks cracking the display glass if done improperly, but also
small mistakes like forgetting to reattach cables or getting dust on the camera or display edges
during reassembly can happen. Technicians have to be trained for it. Additionally, after several years,
that adhesive might become extra tough or the display more fragile – making repairs on aging units

Upgradability: Aside from RAM, and to a lesser extent storage, there is limited scope for upgrades.
CPU upgrade is unofficial but possible (one could put in an i7-7700K; that said, cooling that 91W chip
is on the edge, but Apple themselves offered it as BTO so it works). GPU upgrade is essentially not
feasible beyond what Apple offered in that model line – the BIOS/EFI won’t recognize arbitrary MXM
GPUs typically, and power/cooling constraints exist. So you cannot, say, put a modern GPU inside the
2017  iMac  –  you’re  stuck  with  Polaris  generation  internally.  For  expansion  of  functionality,
Thunderbolt 3 is your friend (e.g., need more storage? use TB3 RAID; need new ports? TB3 dock;
need better GPU? TB3 eGPU, etc.). Apple’s design trade-off was clear: internal flexibility was sacrificed
for integrated design, but high-bandwidth external I/O partially compensates for some needs. 
Repair Costs: If something like the GPU or logic board fails out of warranty, repairing a 2017 iMac
could be expensive – often several hundreds of dollars for parts plus labor. For instance, the Radeon
Pro cards, if bought as spare parts, are pricey. The display itself is one of the most expensive
components – if it cracks, replacement panels cost a lot (close to \$600–\$700 or more, just for part).
That’s one reason people get a bit nervous about opening the iMac: one slip and you might damage
that beautiful (and costly) screen. Apple’s own policies for repair might in some cases opt for a whole
unit replacement if multiple things fail. However, for a professional environment, these iMacs have
been serviceable enough to keep in operation with upgrades (like replacing Fusion Drive with full
SSD to improve reliability and performance, or re-pasting CPU/GPU for better thermals after years of
use). 
Design Trade-offs: Apple’s pursuit of a thin, minimalistic aesthetic yields an AIO that looks stunning
and has a small footprint – it’s essentially a high-end monitor that also contains a full computer. The
trade-off is  primarily  in  serviceability  and  heat  dissipation.  We’ve  discussed  how  thermals  are
managed – it works but with little headroom, and the machine will throttle or get loud rather than
compromise the slim design. Another trade-off is upgradability vs obsolescence: with a traditional
tower, one could upgrade the GPU to get another few years of life out of the system. With the iMac,
once the GPU is insufficient (say for latest software or OS features), you can’t easily upgrade it
internally. Apple somewhat addressed this generationally by ensuring that each iMac generation is
fairly advanced at release – e.g., the 2017 iMac’s GPU was enough for Metal and all current macOS
features for at least 5+ years. But eventually, it’s stuck. For example, macOS Sonoma (2023) dropped
support for Polaris GPUs (like in this iMac), hence the iMac is stuck on Ventura – partly a decision
based on GPU feature set. A Mac Pro tower user from 2013 could theoretically swap GPUs to get
extended OS support (and indeed some did to run Mojave etc.). iMac users don’t have that luxury,
aside from unsupported hacks. This is the inherent compromise of an all-in-one design. 
Longevity  Considerations: The  iMac’s  tightly  integrated  design  means  if  one  component  fails
significantly (e.g., logic board goes bad), the entire machine is out of commission until fixed – you
can’t just use the display with another computer easily (no Target Display Mode for 5K) and you can’t
as easily replace the board as a PC motherboard (due to custom form factor). So there’s a bit of eggs-
in-one-basket risk. However, historically iMacs are reasonably reliable; common failures are the hard
drive (solvable via external boot or replacement) or the GPU in some models (the 2011 iMacs had
GPU failures – the 2017 with Polaris has not had widespread known GPU issues). The PSU can fail
too; replacing it is possible with effort. Apple typically supports spare parts for some years, but by

Expandability: We’ve  covered  how  Thunderbolt   3  mitigates  some  of  the  need  for  internal
expandability. Indeed, the mindset with iMac is to use external peripherals for additions. Need more

modular desktop where you might add an internal SATA drive or a PCIe card for more ports. So the

setup can become a mess of attached devices and cables if one needs a lot of expansion, whereas a

desk aesthetic out-of-the-box, expecting that many users won’t need more than what’s provided
built-in. For those who do, the extra dongles/docks is the compromise.
Service  and  Upgrades  by  Apple: If  we  consider  official  routes,  Apple  did  allow  configurable
upgrades at purchase (like choose bigger SSD, better CPU/GPU). Post-purchase, Apple’s stance is
usually not to upgrade internals (except RAM in 27″ which they even describe in user manual). If you
go to an Apple Store to, say, upgrade the Fusion to pure SSD, they would likely decline or charge a lot
for essentially doing a repair-level operation. Many independent shops, however, have made a
business of iMac upgrades (e.g., replacing Fusion drives with SSDs to breathe new performance into
older units – a highly recommended upgrade for a 2017 iMac as it ages, since SSDs are more reliable
and much faster than the HDD portion). 
Apple’s Design Philosophy and Trade-offs: The iMac’s design epitomizes Apple’s philosophy of integrating
hardware and software tightly in an elegant form. The benefit is a device that is greater than the sum of its
parts for the end-user experience – the stunning display, powerful components, and macOS all tuned to
work together seamlessly and look good doing it. The cost of this approach is flexibility: users give up some
freedom to tinker or extend the hardware. Apple bets that most users would prefer a cohesive, albeit fixed,
system rather than a clunky but modular one. For professionals, this is sometimes a point of contention –
historically, pro users liked upgradable Macs (like older Mac Pros). The iMac sort of straddles that line; it’s
sold to pros (designers, photographers, etc.) yet it’s not fully modular. Apple tried to address that with the
iMac Pro and later Mac Pro for those who truly need modularity. But the success of the iMac shows that a
lot of pro users were okay with (or even embraced) the all-in-one concept, given the value it offered in other
areas (like that display or lower price compared to separate components). 
From a repair perspective, iFixit’s score of 5/10 means “not great, not terrible.” They praised the RAM door
and modular internals
, but dinged the glued display and the need to painstakingly re-tape it on closure
. They also note that “you’ll have to masterfully peel off old tape and apply new tape to reseal the iMac
to original condition”
 – which is a skill that average consumers won’t have. So effectively, Apple’s design
choice here ensures most users will never crack open their iMac (thus preserving the sleekness and
structural integrity), but it also locks them into either paying Apple for upgrades at purchase or using
external solutions. It aligns with Apple’s general approach to keep the internals controlled and encourage
replacing the machine every so often rather than upgrading bits of it over time.

limited. The design trade-offs Apple made – adhesive assembly, custom parts, slim form – prioritize
