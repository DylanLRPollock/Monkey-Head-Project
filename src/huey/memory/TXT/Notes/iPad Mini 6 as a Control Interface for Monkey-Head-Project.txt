iPad Mini 6 as a Control Interface for Monkey-
Head-Project
The Monkey-Head-Project is a robotics initiative that requires a robust and user-friendly control interface.
This report evaluates the  Apple iPad Mini 6 (6th generation) as the potential control interface for the
project, analyzing its performance, features, and suitability in a robotics control context. Where relevant,
comparisons are made with the  iPad 10th Generation (2022) to highlight differences in capability. Key
aspects reviewed include the iPad Mini 6’s touch responsiveness, display quality, processing performance
(CPU/GPU/RAM),  hardware  specifications  (chipset,  screen,  ports),  software  compatibility,  battery  life,
connectivity (Wi-Fi/Bluetooth), and overall ergonomics for integration into a robotics setup.
Overview and Form Factor
The iPad Mini 6 (released late 2021) is Apple’s smallest modern iPad, featuring an “all-screen” design with an
8.3-inch display and no home button. It adopts the flat-edge design and Touch ID-in-power-button style of
the iPad Air/Pro series
. Physically, the Mini 6 measures about 195.4 x 134.8 x 6.3 mm and weighs
only 293–297 g (≈0.65 lbs)
. This compact footprint – roughly 7.7" x 5.3" – makes it much smaller and
lighter than a standard 10-inch tablet. In comparison, the  iPad 10th Gen has a  10.9-inch screen and
measures 248.6 x 179.5 x 7.0 mm, weighing about 477 g (1.05 lbs)
. The Mini’s lighter weight “makes
it incredibly easy to use with just one hand”, whereas the 10th-gen iPad typically demands two-handed use or
a mount for comfortable operation
.
From an ergonomics standpoint, the iPad Mini 6’s smaller form factor is advantageous for a  handheld
control interface. An operator can hold it for extended periods with less fatigue, and its narrower width
allows thumb reach across the screen for touch inputs. The aluminum unibody construction is high quality
and sturdy
, which is important in a workshop or field environment. The Mini 6’s compact size also
eases integration into a robotics setup – it can be more easily mounted onto a control rig, embedded into
a console, or even attached to the robot itself if needed. (In fact, reports suggest Apple has prototyped a
robot arm with an iPad as the control panel, underlining the concept of using an iPad as “the brain, allowing
users to interact with the robot”
.) The iPad 10, with its larger size, offers more screen real estate (useful
for complex dashboards or video feeds) but is less wieldy to mount on a small robot or to carry around. The
choice may hinge on whether  portability or  display area is the priority for the Monkey-Head-Project’s
control interface.

As shown, the iPad Mini 6 is smaller and lighter, with a newer A15 chip and higher pixel density display,
whereas the iPad 10 offers a larger screen and identical RAM, but with a slightly older A14 chip and a slower
USB port. The following sections delve deeper into each of these aspects and their implications for use as a
control interface.
Display Quality and Touch Responsiveness
Display and Touch are critical since the iPad will serve as the primary user interface for controlling the
robot. The iPad Mini 6 features an 8.3-inch Liquid Retina IPS display with a sharp 2266×1488 resolution,
yielding about 326 pixels per inch (ppi)
. In practice, this means crisp text and detailed graphics – finer
than the 264 ppi of the 10.9-inch iPad 10 (2360×1640)
. The Mini’s screen is noted as  “one of the best
screens for a tablet of its size,” with rich colors (103% sRGB coverage, similar to larger iPads) and high
brightness around 500+ nits
. In fact, it measured up to ~520 nits, outshining the iPad Air 4 and base
iPad 9 in brightness
. This is beneficial for visibility – even  outdoors or in bright environments,
content remains readable, though reflections on the glossy screen can be an issue in direct sunlight
.
The iPad 10’s display is comparable in brightness (~500 nits), so both devices offer good but not exceptional
brightness (for instance, not as high as pro tablets). True Tone technology on both models adjusts color
temperature to ambient light, which helps with clarity and comfort.
Touch responsiveness on both iPads is excellent. They feature a 60 Hz refresh rate (no ProMotion 120 Hz
here), which is standard for iPads in this class
. While 60 Hz means input and scrolling are not as ultra-
smooth as on 120 Hz iPad Pro models, in practice the touch latency is low and the interface feels responsive
for typical use. Apple’s touch sampling and iPadOS optimizations ensure that gestures, taps, and swipes

capabilities of the iPad Mini 6 are well-suited for a control interface, delivering the needed clarity and
responsiveness.
Processing Performance and Benchmarks
At the heart of the iPad Mini 6 is the Apple A15 Bionic chip – a very powerful 5nm SoC that also powers the
iPhone 13 series. This 6-core CPU (2 high-performance + 4 efficiency cores) and 5-core GPU provide the Mini
with  flagship-level  performance for  a  tablet.  In  comparison,  the  iPad  10th  Gen  uses  the  previous-
generation A14 Bionic (from the iPhone 12/iPad Air 4), with a similar 6-core CPU but a 4-core GPU. Both
devices have  4 GB of RAM, ensuring smooth multitasking (the Mini 6 got a bump from 3 GB in its
predecessor to 4 GB, which  “means [it] will be better suited for multitasking” going forward
). For
running a robotics control app and related tasks, both A14 and A15 are more than capable, but the A15 in
the Mini 6 does offer a performance edge.

(single-core) and 4540 (multi-core)
. The iPad 10 (A14) scores roughly 1580 single and 4027 multi-
core in the same tests
. That means single-threaded speed is essentially on par (within a couple
percent), while the Mini 6’s multi-core performance is about 12–15% higher than the iPad 10

, so the iPad could be connected to an

better support high-resolution output or certain USB peripherals. For example, if the project ever requires
plugging in a USB accessory (like a camera, sensor interface, or even a USB-to-serial adapter to directly
interface with the robot’s hardware), the Mini’s port would handle it with less bottleneck. The iPad 10’s USB

Battery Life and Power Considerations
For field use or extended sessions, battery life is a crucial factor. Apple rates both the iPad Mini 6 and iPad
10 for around 10 hours of typical use on Wi-Fi (web surfing, etc.), and testing confirms they meet or slightly
exceed this in practice. In Tom’s Guide’s test, the iPad Mini 6 achieved 10 hours 56 minutes of continuous
web browsing over Wi-Fi at 150 nits brightness
. NotebookCheck’s Wi-Fi web surfing test for the iPad 10
(also at ~150 nits) recorded about  10 hours 47 minutes
. In other words, both devices can last
roughly 11 hours on a charge with moderate use, slightly above Apple’s conservative 10-hour claim. 
Under  heavier loads, such as continuous high-brightness use, video streaming, or gaming, the battery
drains faster. For example, video playback (1080p local video) lasted about 12+ hours on iPad 10
, and a
heavy CPU+GPU load can exhaust the battery in just over 3 hours
. A robotics control interface likely falls
in between these extremes. If the app is mostly sending commands and displaying sensor data (which is
not very CPU-intensive), battery life will be closer to the upper range. If it involves streaming live camera
feed from the robot and perhaps performing some processing, the drain will increase. Still, it’s reasonable
to expect a full work day of intermittent use on the iPad Mini 6. At ~19.3 Wh battery capacity
, the Mini’s
endurance is impressive for its size – Apple’s efficiency and the A15’s optimizations help it nearly match the
larger iPad 10 with its ~28.9 Wh battery
. NotebookCheck did note the iPad 10’s battery life, while good,
was the shortest among some peers (largely because competitors had even larger batteries)
. They
mentioned “the battery life of [the 2021 iPad 9] is even better” than the iPad 10, due to a bigger battery and
lower power chip in the older model
. By extension, the iPad Mini 6’s longevity is quite solid given its
small battery, thanks to the efficient A15.
For the Monkey-Head-Project team, this means the iPad Mini 6 can likely handle  long robot testing
sessions or demos without needing a recharge mid-way. If the robot is used in the field, an operator
could use the Mini throughout the day, perhaps charging during breaks. Both iPads support fast charging

Ergonomics and Integration into Robotics Setup
The ergonomics of using the iPad Mini 6 in a robotics context are highly favorable. Its lightweight, small
design allows for versatile handling: an operator can hold it in one hand and interact with the other, or even
attach  it  to  a  forearm  mount  or  handheld  bracket  if  desired.  In  scenarios  where  the  robot  is  being
teleoperated,  the  operator  may  need  to  move  around  –  the  Mini’s  portability  means  it  won’t  be
cumbersome while on the move. In contrast, a larger tablet like the iPad 10 may cause more arm fatigue if
held for long and could be awkward in tight spaces.
If the Monkey-Head-Project envisions the control interface being mounted to a fixed station or onto the
robot itself, the iPad Mini 6 again shines. Its compact 8.3″ form can fit into smaller panels. There are many
off-the-shelf mounting solutions for iPad Minis (from drone controller mounts to tripod clamps) thanks to
its popularity in portable scenarios. The device could be attached to a custom bracket on the robot “Huey”
to act as a local UI or face. Its wide viewing angles and bright screen would allow onlookers to read the
display from various positions
. The iPad 10, being larger, provides a bigger interface if multiple people
need to see or if very large touch targets are required for safety (e.g., emergency stop buttons could be
bigger). However, the larger size also means a larger footprint and more weight on any mounting system
(nearly double the mass of the Mini, which might matter if the robot has to support it).
Ease of use: The Mini 6’s smaller screen does mean UI elements will also be smaller unless specifically
designed for the iPad Mini resolution. The project team should ensure the control app’s buttons and text are
scaled appropriately – the pixel density is higher, so graphics are very sharp, but one must avoid making tap
targets too tiny. Tom’s Guide pointed out that on the Mini, using split-screen multitasking can result in
“incredibly small touch targets” and tiny text
. The implication is that the Mini is best used for one app
at a time (which is likely the case here) and that the UI should be optimized for its display. Fortunately,
iPadOS provides auto-scaling and interface guidelines for different screen sizes, so a well-designed app will
present controls at a comfortable size on the Mini.

robot can be activated and the iPad is ready at a moment’s notice to control it.

As one review put it, those who choose iPad 10 “will get a fast tablet with an accurate display, good battery life,
and optional 5G and will probably be very satisfied with it”, even though the tech upgrade was modest
.
This statement applies even more so to the Mini 6, which has slightly better tech (A15, etc.) in an even

Conclusion and Comparison

, and a  compact, lightweight design that enhances mobility and mounting flexibility
. It

. The Mini 6’s hardware features – from Wi-Fi 6 and sensors to the versatile

wirelessly or tethering for diagnostics

Comparatively, the iPad 10th Gen provides a larger screen (10.9″ vs 8.3″) which can be beneficial for UIs
that require more on-screen information or bigger control elements. It too has solid performance and
battery life, but its A14 chip is a step behind – in multi-core and graphics tasks the Mini 6 holds an edge
. The iPad 10’s form factor is less convenient to handle on the go or to mount on a compact robot, and it

.
Additionally, the iPad 10’s limitations like the USB 2.0 port and needing an adapter for Pencil may introduce
minor inconveniences
. Unless the larger display is absolutely critical for the control application, the
iPad Mini 6 tends to be a more efficient and ergonomic solution.
In a scenario where the team needs to, for example, monitor a video feed from the robot’s camera while
also controlling it, the bigger screen of iPad 10 might allow a clearer view. But the Mini 6’s high pixel density
and ability to be held closer (since it’s lighter) can offset that. And the Mini’s extra GPU muscle can ensure
the video runs at high frame rate without stutter. 
The table below summarizes when to prefer one over the other:
iPad Mini 6 – Best For: Highly mobile control, one-handed use, mounting in tight spaces, scenarios
needing top-notch performance (CPU/GPU), and users who value portability over screen size. It’s
ideal if the control interface is streamlined or if the operator needs to walk around with the
controller. Also preferable if using modern accessories (Pencil 2, faster data I/O).
iPad 10th Gen – Best For: Situations where a larger display greatly enhances usability (complex
dashboards, split-screen multitasking), or when slightly lower cost is a factor. Its performance is still
very good, and it would serve well if mounted on a console or used by a stationary operator who
benefits from a 10.9″ touch area.
Overall, the iPad Mini 6 excels as a control interface due to its balance of power and portability. It has the
“more than enough power for every use case” expected in this context
, and its form factor aligns with
the practical needs of a robotics project (ease of handling, longer usage without strain, versatile placement).

that can fluidly run their control software, stay connected with minimal latency, and remain convenient for
developers and operators alike. In comparison to the iPad 10, the Mini 6’s  newer A15 chip, higher
mobility, and better accessory support give it the edge as the more suitable control interface device for
this project’s needs
.
