An In-Depth Exploration of macOS Ventura
(Version 13)
Overview and Historical Context
macOS Ventura (version 13) is the nineteenth major release of Apple’s Mac operating system, succeeding
macOS 12 Monterey
. Announced at Apple’s Worldwide Developers Conference (WWDC) on June 6, 2022,
Ventura was previewed as  “the latest version of the world’s most advanced desktop operating system” and
touted to “take the Mac experience to a whole new level”
. It became publicly available as a free update on
October 24, 2022
. The Ventura codename continues Apple’s tradition of naming macOS after Californian
places, reflecting the product’s heritage and environment (in this case, Ventura County). This release arrived
amid  Apple’s  ongoing  transition  to  Apple  Silicon  chips,  and  many  of  its  enhancements  leverage  the
performance and capabilities of the M1 and M2 processors introduced in recent years
. Notably, Ventura
drops support for several older Intel-based Macs; only 2017-and-later iMacs and MacBook Pros, 2018-and-
later MacBook Airs and Mac minis, the 2019 Mac Pro, the 2017 MacBook (12″), and the iMac Pro are officially
supported
. In other words, models from 2015-2016 or earlier that ran Monterey are no longer eligible
for Ventura
, marking a significant cutoff as Apple optimizes macOS for newer hardware.
Historically, macOS Ventura is an evolutionary update building on foundations laid by Big Sur (macOS 11)
and  Monterey.  While  Monterey  introduced  high-profile  features  like  Shortcuts  and  Universal  Control,
Ventura’s focus is on productivity and tighter integration with the broader Apple ecosystem. Many headline
features in Ventura originated on iOS/iPadOS (for example, the new Stage Manager windowing system and
the Continuity Camera)
. This reflects Apple’s strategy of unifying experiences across devices, making the
Mac more welcoming to users who also use iPhones and iPads. At the same time, Ventura brings Mac-
specific improvements under the hood – from performance optimizations to developer technologies –
ensuring that pro users and developers have new tools to leverage. In the sections below, we examine all
major aspects of macOS Ventura in detail, from its marquee features to system refinements, app updates,
security enhancements, developer APIs, performance profile, and the community’s critical reception.
Major New Features in macOS Ventura
Ventura introduces several major features aimed at enhancing multitasking, continuity between devices,
and  collaboration.  Three  of  the  most  notable  additions  are  Stage  Manager,  Continuity  Camera  (with
FaceTime  Handoff),  and  the  Freeform  app.  These  features  exemplify  Ventura’s  blend  of  improving
traditional desktop workflows while borrowing ideas from iPadOS and iOS to create a more seamless cross-
device experience.

Stage Manager is a new window management interface that helps users organize open applications and stay
focused on the current task. When enabled from Control Center, Stage Manager automatically arranges
open app windows into a strip on one side of the screen (by default, on the left) while keeping the active

. Stage

Continuity Camera and FaceTime Handoff
Continuity Camera is a Continuity feature that lets Mac users harness their iPhone’s camera as a high-quality
wireless webcam for the Mac
. Without any complex setup – the Mac automatically detects a nearby
iPhone and can use it as the camera – users gain access to capabilities far beyond what typical laptop
webcams offer. Continuity Camera supports advanced features powered by the iPhone’s imaging system:
Center Stage (which keeps you centered in frame as you move), Portrait mode (for background blur), and
Studio Light (which illuminates your face while dimming the background) are all available on the Mac
through  the  iPhone’s  cameras
.  A  particularly  innovative  feature  is  Desk  View,  which  leverages  the
iPhone’s Ultra Wide camera to simultaneously show the user’s face and an overhead view of their desk or
hands – ideal for demonstrations or creative tutorials
. Apple notes that some of these experiences were
“never possible before on a webcam”
. Continuity Camera works wired or wirelessly, and Apple released
accessories to physically mount an iPhone on a MacBook’s lid for convenience
.
Ventura also extends Continuity to FaceTime calls with Handoff. Now, you can start a FaceTime call on one
device (say, your iPhone) and seamlessly transfer it to your Mac when you sit down at your desk (or vice
versa) with a single click
. The call handoff is smooth and automatic, provided the devices are in
proximity and the same user is logged in. Together, Continuity Camera and FaceTime Handoff exemplify
Ventura’s emphasis on cross-device workflows: the Mac, iPhone, and iPad work in concert, allowing users to
leverage the best device for the task at any moment. These features require iOS 16 on the iPhone and
macOS  13  on  the  Mac,  and  use  local  wireless  protocols  (Bluetooth  and  Wi-Fi)  for  detection  and
communication, maintaining Apple’s focus on privacy and ease-of-use.
Freeform: Collaborative Whiteboard App
Introduced in macOS 13.1 (a few months after Ventura’s initial release), Freeform is a new app designed for
creative brainstorming and real-time collaboration. Freeform provides an infinite blank canvas where users
can sketch or paste almost any content – drawings, text sticky notes, shapes, photos, PDFs, links, and more
– and work together with others in the same space
. Multiple users can contribute to a Freeform
board  simultaneously,  seeing  each  other’s  changes  live,  which  makes  it  a  powerful  tool  for  remote
meetings,  project  planning,  or  design  sessions.  The  app  syncs  across  Mac,  iPad,  and  iPhone,  and
collaboration  can  be  initiated  easily  via  FaceTime  or  Messages.  Apple  calls  Freeform  “a  powerful  new
collaboration application with a flexible canvas” that allows people to  “see, share, and collaborate all in one
place”
. Essentially, it’s a digital whiteboard that integrates with Apple’s communication tools. While
Freeform arrived a bit later than Ventura’s launch, it is now a key part of the macOS 13 feature set and

System and UI Updates
Beyond the headline features, macOS Ventura brings a variety of system-wide changes and interface
updates. Some are subtle quality-of-life improvements, while others represent significant redesigns or new
additions to the Mac experience.
System Settings Redesign – One of the most noticeable changes in Ventura is the replacement of
System  Preferences with  the  new  System  Settings app,  featuring  a  completely  redesigned
interface. Instead of the iconic icon grid layout that Mac users had known for decades, System
Settings adopts a sidebar list design similar to the Settings app on iPhone/iPad
. The intent was
to create a more consistent experience across platforms and modernize an aging UI. In practice, the
new System Settings has drawn criticism for its ergonomics and polish. While it organizes settings
into an iOS-like hierarchy (with categories listed on the left and detail panes on the right), reviewers
noted that it  “has too many scrolling lists” and nested sub-menus, sometimes making navigation
confusing
.  The  app  was  rebuilt  using  SwiftUI  (Apple’s  newer  UI  framework),  and  early
versions suffered from glitches and layout issues
. Apple has since issued fixes, but power users
have complained that the new design is less efficient for quickly finding settings compared to the old
layout. In summary, Ventura’s System Settings represents a bold UI departure – aligning Mac more
with iOS design language – but one that has been controversial. Apple appears aware of the
feedback; reports suggest further refinements may come in future macOS versions to address these
usability concerns
.
New Native Apps (Weather and Clock) – For the first time, macOS gains the Weather app and the
Clock app as native applications. These are essentially ports of the iPhone/iPad versions, brought to
Mac (likely via Apple’s Mac Catalyst technology). The Weather app on Ventura offers the same rich,
animated weather data and maps that iOS users are familiar with, allowing Mac users to check local
forecasts and receive severe weather alerts with a full-featured interface
. The Clock app
similarly brings timers, alarms, world clocks, and stopwatch functionality to the Mac, integrated with
Siri and widgets. While these apps don’t introduce new functionality beyond what existed on iOS,
their addition rounds out the Mac’s app suite and reflects Apple’s ecosystem approach – ensuring
core apps are available and consistent on all devices. For example, a user can set an alarm on their
Mac or ask Siri about weather on the Mac without relying on web equivalents. Under the hood, these
additions demonstrate the ease of code-sharing between iOS and macOS in Apple’s developer
frameworks.
Spotlight Improvements – Ventura includes an overhauled  Spotlight search experience with an
updated design and new capabilities. The Spotlight window is more in line with the iOS search UI,
featuring  richer  search  results  for  many  query  types
.  Users  can  now  find  images  through
Spotlight (searching their Photos library, files, and even the web for images), and they can leverage
Live Text recognition to search text inside images
. For example, typing a location or phrase can
surface photos containing that text on signs or in screenshots. Spotlight results for contacts, actors,
musicians, sports, businesses, etc., are more detailed, presenting information cards rather than just

Miscellaneous UI Changes – Ventura brings numerous small interface tweaks and refinements. For

updates system sounds, and in some cases, animations (such as Mission Control’s grouping) for a

how window resizing and tiling behave, making it smoother to tile windows side-by-side by dragging
them to screen edges

remains  familiar,  Ventura’s  UI  changes  continue  Apple’s  gradual  convergence  of  design  across
platforms and introduce new behaviors aimed at increasing productivity and consistency.
Enhancements to Built-in Apps
Nearly all of Apple’s core apps see improvements in macOS Ventura, ranging from major overhauls to small
useful additions. Here we detail enhancements in several key apps and features: Mail, Messages, Safari,
Photos & iCloud, and more.
Mail: Improved Search and Modern Features
The Mail app in Ventura receives one of its biggest updates in years, adding many features expected of
modern email clients. First, Mail now supports email scheduling and undo send: users can choose to send
later (at a specific time) and can cancel the delivery of a just-sent message within a short window
. This
brings Mail.app in line with services like Gmail which have had “undo send” and scheduling capabilities. Mail
in Ventura also gains the ability to set follow-up reminders for emails – you can tell Mail to resurface a
message at a later time so you don’t forget to respond – as well as automatic suggestions to follow up if an
email you sent hasn’t received a response
. Another handy addition is that Mail will now detect if you
mention an attachment or recipient in the body of an email but forget to actually attach the file or include
that recipient, and it will prompt you before sending (helping avoid common email mistakes)
.
Most significantly, Apple completely reengineered Mail’s search using “state-of-the-art search technologies”.
As soon as you click into the search field, Mail now shows recent emails, contacts, documents, photos, and
more – even before you start typing
. The search engine is more intelligent and faster, delivering more
relevant results by correcting typos and using synonyms. Apple touted this as “the biggest overhaul to search
in years” for Mail
. In practice, users have found Mail’s search far more useful in Ventura, as it can find
emails by content and context reliably. For IT departments, these enhancements mean the default Mail app
is a more viable option for power users, reducing the need for third-party email clients. Overall, Ventura’s

Messages: New Messaging Features and Collaboration
The Messages app on Mac gains parity with the new features introduced in iOS 16, making conversations
more flexible and forgiving. Users can now edit an iMessage after sending it (within a short window) or
undo send to delete a sent message entirely – with visible indicators in the chat if a message was edited or
removed
. Additionally, recently deleted messages can be recovered, and you can mark any thread as
unread to remind yourself to revisit it later
. These features are especially welcome for professionals
who use iMessage for quick communications – for example, catching a typo or retracting a mistaken
message is now possible, reducing miscommunication.
Ventura’s Messages also integrates more deeply with collaboration features. When you share a document
or  file  via  Messages,  you’ll  get  an  option  to  share  it  in  a  collaborative  way:  everyone  in  the  thread
automatically gets access, and any edits to the shared document will show an activity update in the
message thread
. This ties in with Apple’s iWork and Files collaboration – a bit like Slack or Microsoft
Teams, the thread becomes a mini collaboration hub. Furthermore, if someone shares content like a Safari
Tab Group or an Xcode project via Messages, the thread will update as people make changes. Apple has
also connected Messages with the new Freeform app and with SharePlay: Ventura allows you to initiate a
SharePlay  session  (e.g.,  watching  a  video  or  listening  to  music  together)  directly  from  a  Messages
conversation,  without  requiring  a  FaceTime  call
.  This  means  colleagues  or  friends  can  enjoy
synchronized content while chatting in text. For enterprise or group productivity, these additions make
Messages more than just a chat app – it’s now part of Apple’s collaboration toolkit.
Safari and Passwords: Passkeys and Shared Tab Groups
Apple’s Safari browser in Ventura focuses on collaboration and security. Safari introduces  Shared Tab
Groups, which let users create sets of tabs that can be shared with others. For example, a team planning a
project or a family planning a trip can share a Tab Group; everyone can see the same set of websites and
even see, in real time, which tab others are currently viewing
. Participants can add tabs for the
group to see, and the group can even have a shared Start Page with common bookmarks. This feature turns
Safari into a lightweight collaborative research tool. It’s integrated with Messages and FaceTime as well –
you can jump into a group chat or call about the tabs directly from Safari
. Shared Tab Groups are saved
in iCloud, and like regular Tab Groups (introduced in Monterey), they sync across your devices.
On  the  security  front,  Safari  in  Ventura  is  ushering  in  the  era  of  Passkeys –  a  new  passwordless
authentication method based on public-key cryptography. Passkeys are designed to replace traditional
passwords  with  biometrics  (Touch  ID/Face  ID)  and  device-local  keys.  When  creating  an  account  on  a
website, you can generate a Passkey, which the system stores securely in your iCloud Keychain. The Passkey
never leaves your device; what gets stored on servers is only a public key, so it cannot be phished or leaked
in plaintext
. Logging in becomes as simple as authenticating with Touch ID or Face ID, and behind
the scenes a cryptographic challenge is answered by your device. Passkeys work across apps and the web,
and even on non-Apple devices via QR codes (using your iPhone to authenticate)
. In Ventura’s Safari,
Passkey support is built-in and Apple is working with standards (FIDO/WebAuthn) so that this is an industry-
wide transition. In short, “Safari ushers in a passwordless future with passkeys”, which are more secure and
easier to use than passwords
. For IT security, this is a significant development – reducing reliance on

Photos and iCloud Shared Photo Library
The  Photos app itself in Ventura sees some editing improvements and added intelligence (such as the
ability to detect duplicates and merge photo libraries), but the standout new feature is the iCloud Shared
Photo Library. This is a separate iCloud photo library that up to six family members (or any set of people
you invite) can contribute to and share
. It’s Apple’s answer to the problem of families and friends
wanting a common pool of photos (for instance, all the pictures of a vacation taken by multiple people). In
Ventura (and iOS 16), users can choose to move or share existing photos into the shared library, either
manually or based on smart criteria (like all photos after a certain date, or all photos containing specific
people)
. Once set up, the Photos app provides a toggle to easily switch between viewing your personal
library, the shared library, or both combined. You can also have the Camera app on iPhone automatically
send new captures directly to the shared library (when certain people are detected in the photo, for
example).
In the shared library, all participants have equal permissions to add, edit, or delete items. Edits and captions
sync for everyone, and favorites or keywords applied by any user are visible to all. Each user’s Memories
and  widgets  will  include  photos  from  the  Shared  Library  as  well
.  This  feature  greatly  simplifies
collaborative  photo  management  –  no  more  sending  pictures  to  each  other  and  having  duplicates;
everyone in the group truly shares the photos (stored once in iCloud, counting toward one person’s storage
or prorated). From a technical perspective, Apple had to implement robust conflict resolution and sync for
edits across multiple users, which demonstrates the cloud advances under the hood. Privacy-wise, you only
share what you choose to share; your personal library remains separate. iCloud Shared Photo Library was
slightly delayed but became available in late 2022 updates to Ventura and iOS 16. It has been well received
by families and teams alike, though it requires careful initial setup. For professionals, this could even be
used in small teams to quickly build a shared media repository (bearing in mind Apple’s iCloud storage
limitations and that it’s not a full DAM solution).

Security and Privacy Advancements
Security and user privacy remain a cornerstone of macOS Ventura, which introduces several high-profile
features and under-the-hood changes to make the Mac more secure against threats. Notably, Ventura
pushes  the  envelope  with  passwordless  authentication,  new  extreme  protection  modes,  safer  system
updates, and expanded encryption for iCloud data, among other enhancements.
Passkeys for Passwordless Authentication – As mentioned earlier, Ventura (with Safari 16 and
accompanying frameworks) implements passkeys, a next-generation credential system based on the
WebAuthn standard. Passkeys are unique digital keys created for each account, stored securely in
the iCloud Keychain, and protected by biometrics. Unlike passwords, passkeys  cannot be shared,
phished, or leaked from a server because the private key never leaves your device
. Logging in
with a passkey typically requires Touch ID on Mac (or Face ID on an iPhone) to authorize the use of
the credential. For example, a website that supports passkeys will prompt you to create a passkey;
later, to log in, you simply authenticate with Touch ID, and the Mac cryptographically proves your
identity
.  This  is  both  simpler  and  far  more  secure  than  memorized  passwords.  Apple’s
implementation syncs passkeys across your devices with end-to-end encryption via iCloud Keychain
, so you have them on Mac, iPhone, and iPad. You can even use your iPhone to log into a site on
a non-Apple device by scanning a QR code that invokes a WebAuthn handshake. Passkeys represent
a  major  industry  shift,  and  Ventura  is  among  the  first  OS  releases  to  roll  it  out  broadly.  For
organizations, this could dramatically reduce the risk of credential theft if adopted – though it

Lockdown Mode – Apple introduced  Lockdown Mode as an extreme, opt-in protection level for
users who face grave, targeted threats (such as journalists, activists, or high-profile individuals
targeted by state-sponsored spyware). Debuting in iOS 16 and macOS Ventura, Lockdown Mode
hardens the system by disabling or restricting features that could be avenues for zero-click attacks.
When  enabled,  it  “automatically  block[s]  most  message  attachment  types,  disables  complex  web
technologies like just-in-time JavaScript compilation, stops wired connections to a computer when the
iPhone  or  Mac  is  locked,  and  prevents  configuration  profiles  from  being  installed,” among  other
restrictions
. In short, Lockdown Mode dramatically reduces the attack surface: many features
of Messages, FaceTime, and web browsing are limited to eliminate vectors used by spyware like NSO
Group’s Pegasus
. Apple has acknowledged that this mode is overkill for the vast majority of
users,  describing  it  as  “extreme  and  optional” protection  for  the  very  few  who  need  it
.  It
exemplifies Apple’s stance on security: willing to constrain functionality to preserve safety for at-risk
users. Apple also launched a $10 million grant for research into mercenary spyware and increased
bug bounties up to $2 million for Lockdown Mode exploits
. For IT admins, Lockdown Mode is
likely too restrictive for everyday enterprise use (it can break many workflows), but it’s a valuable
option for those in sensitive roles. It can be enabled via System Settings > Privacy & Security on
Ventura, and requires a reboot to activate.
Rapid Security Response – To address the increasing pace of security vulnerabilities, Ventura
introduces a mechanism called Rapid Security Response updates. These are security patches that
can be delivered in between regular macOS updates and without requiring a reboot in many cases
. Essentially, Apple can now push small fixes to macOS (and iOS) for things like WebKit or other
critical components, and users can install them like an app update – a much quicker process than
the full OS update which historically required a system restart. This change is significant for reducing
exposure to zero-day exploits: organizations can have users apply a Rapid Security Response as soon
as Apple releases it, without the downtime of a full update. Ventura’s software update mechanism
supports uninstalling these rapid patches as well, in case they cause issues, but generally they are
intended to be seamless. By making security patches easier and faster to deploy, Apple is aiming to
keep devices protected even between major updates. This approach also allows Apple to be more
agile in responding to active threats.
Expanded iCloud Encryption (Advanced Data Protection) – In a late 2022 update (macOS 13.1),
Apple introduced Advanced Data Protection for iCloud, an optional feature that extends end-to-
end encryption to nearly all iCloud data categories. Prior to this, sensitive data like iCloud Keychain,
Health data, and passwords were end-to-end encrypted, but device backups, Photos, Notes, etc.,
were  not  –  those  used  standard  encryption  where  Apple  held  the  keys.  With  Advanced  Data
Protection enabled, iCloud Backup, Photos, Notes, Reminders, Voice Memos, Safari bookmarks, and
more (23 categories in total) are end-to-end encrypted, meaning only the user’s trusted devices have
the keys
. Not even Apple can access that data, which would protect it in case Apple’s servers
were ever breached. To use this, users must opt in and meet certain criteria (like having all devices
updated and account recovery set up, because Apple can’t assist in data recovery if you lose your
credentials when this is on). For IT departments, this feature can vastly improve cloud data security,
though it also means lost data if credentials are lost. It’s part of Apple’s continued emphasis on
privacy – giving users the choice to eliminate even Apple’s access to their data. macOS Ventura fully

Anti-Malware  and  System  Security –  Ventura  further  strengthens  macOS’s  built-in  security
architecture. The system volume remains cryptographically sealed (as in Big Sur/Monterey), and
Ventura likely updates the seal when Rapid Security Responses are applied. Kernel extensions (kexts)
are largely phased out; developers and IT admins are expected to use System Extensions and
DriverKit for extending macOS, which run in user-space, reducing kernel attack surface. Ventura also
improves the Gatekeeper mechanism: it now checks notarization on first launch even for apps that
were already on disk when you upgraded to Ventura (closing a previous loophole). Additionally,
Apple noted that Ventura’s Mail app “now automatically blocks messages with malicious content”, and
Safari has enhanced tracking protection and fingerprinting defenses. Another interesting security-
related addition is support for passkey login on the web and for third-party FIDO2 security keys
for account authentication on Mac – giving users the option to use physical security keys for Apple ID
or other accounts.
Privacy Features – On the privacy front, Ventura inherits a lot of the new iOS 16 privacy features. For
instance, there is an option in System Settings > Privacy & Security for Safety Check (for users who
need to quickly revoke sharing of their data with others, such as in domestic abuse situations),
similar to on iPhone. Mail’s privacy protection (which hides your IP and blocks tracking pixels)

prevent  silent  clipboard  reading).  Also,  Photos has  a  new  Locked  Hidden  album feature:  the

Touch ID or login to view, preventing casual access to sensitive photos. Ventura also requires explicit
user permission for apps to access the Mac’s camera and microphone (as before), but it expands this
to certain other areas like Calendar, Reminders, and even to USB and network volumes in some
cases (apps need permission to access removable drives or network volumes, adding another layer
of security for data access).
In aggregate, macOS Ventura’s security and privacy advancements make the system much more robust
against both mass-scale attacks and targeted exploits. Features like Lockdown Mode and passkeys are at
the cutting edge of consumer operating system security. IT professionals will particularly appreciate Rapid
Security Response for quicker patch management, and may look to encourage the use of passkeys and
iCloud Advanced Data Protection to protect corporate data. As always, there’s a balance: some security
options (Lockdown Mode, full encryption) can impede usability or recovery, so they should be adopted
thoughtfully. But Ventura clearly raises the bar on Mac security at a time of increasing threats.

macOS Ventura requires relatively recent Mac hardware, cutting off support for several models that were
supported by Monterey. According to Apple’s official compatibility list, Ventura is supported on the following
Macs
:
iMac – 2017 models and later. (Monterey had supported iMac late 2015 and 2016, but Ventura drops
those
.)
iMac Pro – the 2017 iMac Pro (the only model year it had).
MacBook Air – 2018 and later (dropping early 2015 and 2017 Air models).
MacBook Pro – 2017 and later (dropping all 2015-2016 Pros)
.
Mac Pro – 2019 and later (dropping the 2013 “trash can” Mac Pro)
.
Mac mini – 2018 and later (dropping 2014 minis)
.
MacBook (12-inch) – 2017 model (the last 12″ MacBook made; earlier 2015-2016 models are not
listed as supported).
In practical terms, Ventura requires a Mac with at least a 7th-generation Intel Core processor or any Apple
Silicon chip. All Apple M1 and M2 based Macs support Ventura, of course. The cutoff of 2017 for Intel
corresponds to dropping Intel 5th and 6th gen CPUs (and even 7th-gen U-series in 2017 MacBook Air wasn’t
supported because no Air 2017 retina existed). The move is partly to ensure acceptable performance and
support for features like Metal 3, which might not run on older GPUs. Some features of Ventura explicitly
require Apple Silicon – for example, Live Captions and some advanced dictation or AI features need the
Neural Engine present in M-series chips.
In terms of system requirements, Ventura formally needs a minimum of 4 GB of RAM and around 12 GB
of available disk space to install, but realistically Apple recommends at least 8 GB RAM for a smooth
experience
. In fact, Apple increased the memory floor: Monterey could run on 4 GB systems,
whereas Ventura’s memory usage is higher and it’s best run on 8 GB or more
. Virtually all supported
Macs meet that (since most post-2017 Macs started with 8 GB by default). The installation download is
around 11-12 GB from the Mac App Store. A broadband internet connection is required to download and
install Ventura, as with other recent macOS releases.
It’s also important to note that certain Ventura features have their own hardware requirements. For
example, Continuity Camera requires an iPhone XR or later (running iOS 16) and a Mac that supports
Bluetooth/Wi-Fi Continuity. Desk View in Continuity Camera requires an iPhone with an Ultra Wide camera
(iPhone 11 or later). Stage Manager works on all Ventura-capable Macs, but on iPad it was limited to M1
iPads – not an issue on Mac. The new high-quality background blur in FaceTime videos may work best on
Apple  Silicon.  And  of  course,  anything  using  Neural  Engine  (Live  Text  in  video,  Visual  Look  Up  for
recognizing animals/objects
, etc.) will perform better on M1/M2 than on Intel Macs which have to use
CPU/GPU for those tasks.

Performance and Benchmarks
macOS Ventura generally delivers solid performance, often comparable to or slightly better than macOS
Monterey,  thanks  to  optimizations  for  Apple  Silicon  and  refinements  in  system  efficiency.  Apple’s
engineering focus for Ventura was not on drastic performance overhauls but on ensuring that new features
did not introduce lag or bloat. Here we assess Ventura’s performance profile in terms of speed, resource
usage, and comparisons with its predecessor:
General System Speed: In everyday use on supported Macs, Ventura feels as fast as Monterey, if not
a bit snappier in certain tasks. Apple has optimized UI animations and memory management – for
instance, Mission Control and window resizing operations have been tuned. Some benchmarks and
user reports indicated small improvements: one analysis found Ventura scoring about 10-15% higher
in CPU multi-core benchmarks and GPU tests compared to Monterey on the same hardware
. This
could be attributed to compiler improvements in Xcode 14 (which might benefit code in the OS) and
Metal 3’s more efficient GPU resource handling. App launch times and disk access in Ventura are on
par with Monterey; the switch to APFS in earlier macOS means file indexing and copying remain
quick. There were concerns that the addition of Stage Manager or more background features could
slow things, but when Stage Manager is off, it has no performance impact, and when on, it’s fairly
lightweight. One area of noted improvement is Safari: Safari 16 is highly optimized and, in Apple’s
Speedometer 2.0 browser benchmark, significantly outperforms not only Safari on Monterey but
also other browsers
. This contributes to faster web app and JavaScript performance on
Ventura by default.
Battery Life: For MacBook users, moving to Ventura does not degrade battery life; in fact, some
users observed slight improvements. Apple has continued to refine power management in macOS,
especially  for  Apple  Silicon.  Ventura  introduces  an  “Intelligent  Power  Management” feature  that
adjusts background activity more granularly based on usage patterns
. In practice, if you’re on a
portable Mac, Ventura should yield battery longevity equal to or slightly above Monterey’s under the
same workload
. For example, one might stream video or code for hours with similar battery
drain, but Ventura may handle things like Spotlight indexing or iCloud sync more efficiently in the
background to save power. Of course, enabling new features like Continuity Camera (which engages
Wi-Fi and the iPhone camera) will use additional power, but that’s workload-dependent. Apple’s
claimed efficiency gains are largely thanks to the hardware (M1/M2 chips’ excellent performance-
per-watt), but the OS plays a role in scheduling tasks at optimal times.

critiques that emerged around Ventura:
System Settings App – The most universally criticized aspect of Ventura is the new System Settings
app (formerly System Preferences). As detailed earlier, many users (especially longtime Mac fans)
find the new design problematic. Common complaints include: confusing navigation (too many

was meant to be more user-friendly for the average person (resembling the iPad), but this change

. For now, users have to adapt to it, use the search function liberally, and

optional, and indeed it’s off by default – which some noted as Apple itself acknowledging it may not
fit everyone’s workflow
. Early adopters who enjoy it praise the quick grouping and single-click
desktop access, but others disabled it after a trial. The consensus is that Stage Manager on macOS,
while more successful than on iPadOS (where it was buggier at launch), is a niche feature: great for
certain organization styles, irrelevant for others. It’s a v1.0 and might evolve in future updates.

integration and features, even if it left some wishing for a bit more focus on the classic Mac experience. For
anyone  upgrading,  the  advice  is:  be  prepared  for  the  new  Settings  interface,  don’t  expect  radical
performance changes, but do enjoy the practical enhancements (and maybe give Stage Manager a try to
see if it fits your style). The Mac remains, as Apple would say, “the world’s most advanced desktop operating
system”, and Ventura both reinforces that title and illustrates the challenges in moving such a mature
platform forward
.
