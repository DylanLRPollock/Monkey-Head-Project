Real-Time Audio Understanding and Generation in
AI: A Comparative Study of OpenAI, Google
Gemini, and DeepSeek
Abstract
Artificial intelligence has made significant strides in audio processing, enabling machines to understand
and  generate audio with unprecedented accuracy and realism. This paper presents a comprehensive
overview of state-of-the-art AI models for audio, focusing on real-time speech understanding (e.g. speech
recognition, speaker identification, sound classification) and audio generation (e.g. text-to-speech, voice
cloning, music synthesis). We emphasize OpenAI’s contributions – notably the Whisper speech recognition
model and the Voice Engine for voice cloning – and compare them against contemporaneous developments
from Google’s Gemini platform and China’s DeepSeek models. We examine the technical architectures of
these systems, their performance, latency, data efficiency, and scalability, as well as their offline and real-
time  capabilities.  Real-world  applications  are  discussed,  from  conversational  voice  assistants  and
transcription  services  to  creative  tools  for  music  generation.  We  also  address  ethical  considerations
surrounding synthetic audio (such as voice cloning and AI-generated music) and outline current limitations
and future directions. The comparative analysis highlights how OpenAI, Google (Gemini), and DeepSeek
each approach the audio domain: OpenAI with specialized models and cautious deployment, Google with
integrated multimodal systems and extensive data, and DeepSeek with massive mixture-of-experts models.
Ethical safeguards like watermarking of AI audio and usage policies are increasingly crucial in parallel with
technical advances. The paper concludes by underscoring the balance between innovation in audio AI and
the responsible use of these powerful technologies. 
Introduction
Audio  is  a  rich  and  vital  modality  for  human  communication  and  expression.  Advances  in  artificial
intelligence have allowed machines to listen, understand, and generate audio in ways that approach human-
like  performance.  From  virtual  assistants  that  can  transcribe  and  respond  to  speech  in  real-time,  to
algorithms that synthesize natural-sounding speech or even compose music, AI-driven audio technologies
are rapidly evolving. In recent years, deep learning architectures (especially large-scale Transformer models)
and  vast  training  datasets  have  led  to  breakthroughs  in  speech  recognition  and  generation.  These
developments open up new possibilities for human-computer interaction, accessibility, content creation,
and more, but also raise new technical challenges and ethical concerns. 
OpenAI has been at the forefront of this audio AI revolution. In 2022, OpenAI introduced Whisper, a neural
network for automatic speech recognition (ASR) trained on 680,000 hours of multilingual audio data.
Whisper demonstrated robust transcription performance across many languages and acoustic conditions,
showcasing the power of large-scale supervised training. More recently, OpenAI developed a powerful text-
to-speech model called Voice Engine, capable of cloning a speaker’s voice from a few seconds of audio.
Voice Engine’s technology powers ChatGPT’s voice feature and can generate speech nearly indistinguishable

Meanwhile, other organizations have advanced their own audio AI systems. Google’s latest Gemini models

This paper provides an academic-style exploration of these developments in AI audio understanding and
generation. We begin by reviewing the technical architectures of leading models, focusing on how they
achieve tasks like transcription, speaker identification, and sound classification (audio understanding) and
tasks  like  text-to-speech,  voice  cloning,  and  music  synthesis  (audio  generation).  We  then  present  a
comparative analysis of OpenAI’s, Google’s (Gemini), and DeepSeek’s approaches along key dimensions:
model performance/accuracy, real-time latency, data efficiency, and scalability. The offline versus real-time
capabilities  of  each  platform  are  discussed  in  detail,  since  these  determine  practical  deployment  in
applications like live conversation or on-device processing. Next, we survey real-world applications enabled
by these audio AI systems – from voice assistants and transcription services to creative and assistive
technologies. Finally, we address the ethical considerations (e.g. the risk of deepfake voices, privacy and
copyright  issues)  and  limitations  of  current  models,  and  outline  future  directions  for  research  and
responsible innovation in this domain. By comparing the strategies of OpenAI, Gemini, and DeepSeek, we
aim to illuminate the state of the art in AI audio and the trade-offs involved in pushing towards more real-
time, high-quality, and safe audio AI systems.
Technical Background: AI Models for Audio Understanding
Speech Recognition and Audio Understanding Architectures
Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) – converting spoken audio into text – is a foundational task in audio
understanding. Modern ASR systems predominantly use neural network architectures trained on large
datasets of audio and transcripts. OpenAI’s Whisper is a representative state-of-the-art ASR model. Whisper
employs a  sequence-to-sequence Transformer architecture with an encoder-decoder design. Input audio is
first converted to a log-Mel spectrogram, which the encoder processes; the decoder then generates text
tokens (characters or word pieces) corresponding to the transcribed speech. Uniquely, Whisper was trained
on a massive 680,000 hours of supervised data from the web, spanning multiple languages and tasks. This
vast and diverse training set gave Whisper strong robustness to accents, background noise, and technical
jargon, and even enabled it to perform multilingual transcription and translation within a single model.
Because Whisper was not fine-tuned to specific datasets, it does not top specialized benchmarks like
Librispeech for English, but it achieves much more robust zero-shot results across varied real-world data

processes audio in 30-second chunks, which has implications for real-time use (discussed later). The open-
source release of Whisper (in models ranging from 39 million to 1.5 billion parameters) has allowed
developers worldwide to build speech-enabled applications on-device, marking a significant milestone in
accessible ASR technology.
Google’s approach to speech recognition, recently unified under its Universal Speech Model (USM), shares
similarities  but  also  key  differences.  USM  is  a  family  of  encoder-decoder  models  (around  2  billion
parameters each) capable of transcribing over 100 languages. Google leveraged self-supervised learning on
12 million hours of unlabeled speech to pre-train USM’s Conformer-based encoder, then fine-tuned on a
smaller amount of labeled data (28 billion text sentences and a few thousand hours of transcribed speech
per language). This two-stage training (unsupervised audio pre-training followed by supervised fine-tuning)
enabled  coverage of 300+ languages, including many low-resource languages. The Conformer encoder
architecture (a convolution-augmented Transformer) allows USM to handle long audio input efficiently and
even produce outputs via different decoders (CTC, RNN-T, or LAS) for streaming or offline modes. The result
of this training strategy is a model that not only generalizes to new languages with minimal data, but also
achieves state-of-the-art accuracy. On YouTube caption data, USM obtained under 30% word error rate on
average across 73 languages – a milestone for multilingual ASR. Notably, when directly compared on
overlapping languages, Google reported USM had a 32.7% lower WER than OpenAI’s Whisper (large-v2) on
those  languages,  demonstrating  the  gains  from  USM’s  massive  unlabeled  training  and  fine-tuning
approach. In practice, Google has deployed these models in services like YouTube captioning and Cloud
Speech-to-Text. The Gemini AI model incorporates such speech recognition capability as one modality of its
multimodal system. Gemini 2.5 is described as understanding streaming audio input and even combining it
with video input for richer context. Unlike Whisper’s fixed 30-second segmentation, Google’s production
ASR (e.g. the  Chirp model, an evolution of USM) supports continuous  streaming recognition, outputting
transcripts in real-time with low latency for interactive applications
. Additionally, Google’s ASR services
include  features  like  speaker  diarization (assigning  transcript  segments  to  different  speakers)  and
keyword spotting, though these are often handled by auxiliary models or pipelines alongside the core ASR
engine.
DeepSeek’s strategy for audio understanding is somewhat different, reflecting its origin as a large language
model with added multimodal capabilities. DeepSeek-V3 is fundamentally a gigantic  mixture-of-experts
(MoE) Transformer aimed at general AI tasks. It can process various data types – text, images, and audio –
by converting them into a machine-readable form and feeding them into the model’s layers. In practice,
DeepSeek’s audio understanding likely relies on converting audio to text (via ASR) as an intermediate step
for most language-heavy tasks like transcription or summarization. Indeed, developer guides illustrate
using DeepSeek-R1 in combination with an external streaming transcription API (like AssemblyAI) to build
voice agents, implying that DeepSeek may not yet have a dedicated built-in ASR as efficient as Whisper or
USM for raw audio. However, DeepSeek’s multimodal design suggests it can ingest audio features directly
for analysis if needed. The model’s architecture presumably includes an audio encoder (potentially similar to
a Transformer or conformer) feeding into the same latent space as text, enabling tasks such as transcribing
audio, answering questions about audio content, or classifying sounds within an audio stream. DeepSeek
documentation highlights “multimodal understanding” – for example, the ability to transcribe audio files
and then summarize or answer questions about them in one workflow. This indicates that DeepSeek treats
audio understanding as one facet of its general problem-solving, using its massive parameter count to
reason about audio-derived information. One consequence is that DeepSeek’s core model (especially V3
with MoE) is extremely large, which could impede real-time deployment for audio understanding unless

Beyond speech transcription, audio classification and speaker identification are also important tasks in
audio understanding. These involve recognizing what is present in an audio scene (e.g., classifying sounds
like  music,  applause,  sirens,  or  detecting  keywords)  and  who is  speaking  in  a  recording.  Traditional
approaches for these tasks use learned audio embeddings and classifiers (for example, Google’s AudioSet
and YAMNet models classify environmental sounds, and speaker ID often uses embedding networks like x-
vectors). In the context of our focus: OpenAI’s Whisper produces segment-level language ID and can output
timestamps, but it does not explicitly perform speaker identification by itself (users must apply separate
diarization algorithms to the transcript). Google’s ecosystem does incorporate speaker recognition: as
noted, the Cloud Speech API can perform speaker diarization, labeling transcript segments by speaker in
multi-speaker audio. This is achieved by an integrated speaker embedding model that runs alongside
transcription. The Gemini system, being conversational, is context-aware enough to distinguish the user’s
voice from its own responses, and it is trained to ignore background voices or interruptions (“conversation
context awareness”) – effectively a form of speaker/activity detection to decide when to listen and when to
talk. DeepSeek, if used in a voice assistant capacity, would similarly need to integrate voice activity detection
and possibly speaker recognition to function in the real world, though specifics are not published. Audio
classification (like detecting non-speech sounds or classifying music genres) is not a primary feature of
Whisper or the language-centric models, but large multimodal models can be prompted to categorize
sounds if given an audio input. For instance, one could transcribe audio with Whisper or USM and then ask
an LLM (like GPT-4 or DeepSeek) to interpret the content (e.g., “the sound of applause” or “classical piano
music”). Google Gemini’s multimodal training likely included some capacity to interpret audio events – as
evidenced by claims that it can handle “subtle auditory cues” in a conversation – though how far this extends
beyond speech is unclear. In summary, for audio understanding tasks, specialized models (Whisper, USM)
currently lead in transcription performance, while larger AI systems incorporate these capabilities to form
part of a bigger reasoning process. Table 1 (in the Comparative Analysis section) will summarize some
features of these models.
Technical Approaches to Real-Time Processing
An important aspect of audio understanding is whether it can be done in real time. Real-time ASR requires
processing  streaming  audio  with  minimal  delay  (typically  a  few  hundred  milliseconds).  This  often
necessitates  architectural  tweaks:  for  example,  using  online models  like  Recurrent  Neural  Network
Transducers  (RNN-T)  or  chunk-based  processing  with  Transformers.  Whisper,  as  originally  released,
processes audio in fixed 30-second blocks, introducing a delay equal to the block size. However, developers
have adapted Whisper for streaming by using shorter chunks (e.g., 5-second windows) at slight cost to
accuracy. OpenAI’s newer Whisper API model (“Whisper-1”) and especially the experimental Whisper large-
v3 Turbo show improved speeds – one community report indicates the Whisper large-v3-turbo model can
transcribe nearly in real-time with ~0.3 s latency on appropriate hardware. Google’s ASR models have been
designed for streaming from the outset: the RNN-T based models that powered Google Assistant could
output words as you speak, and the latest Conformer-based Chirp 2 model supports streaming recognition

Technical Background: AI Models for Audio Generation
Text-to-Speech (TTS) and Voice Cloning Architectures
On the generation side of audio,  text-to-speech (TTS) models convert written text into spoken voice.
Traditional TTS systems involved a pipeline of text front-end (linguistic analysis, phonetic transcription,
prosody prediction) and a back-end vocoder to produce audio waveforms. Modern AI-based TTS has shifted
to end-to-end neural architectures that can learn to synthesize speech directly from text, given enough
training data of human recordings. A landmark was WaveNet (DeepMind, 2016), a generative model for
raw audio that achieved unprecedented naturalness in synthetic speech, with Mean Opinion Scores (MOS)
approaching 4.2 out of 5 (where human speech is ~4.7). WaveNet, however, was slow due to sample-level
generation. Subsequent models (e.g., Tacotron 2 + WaveNet, FastSpeech, etc.) improved efficiency by
generating intermediate acoustic features (like mel-spectrograms) which are then converted to audio by
neural vocoders.
OpenAI’s  Voice Engine represents the cutting edge of TTS combined with  voice cloning. According to
OpenAI, Voice Engine is a “small model” that can produce natural, emotive speech in a voice that closely
resembles a given example recording. In a preview announced in 2024, OpenAI revealed that Voice Engine
allows a user to upload a 15-second sample of a target speaker’s voice and then generates speech in that
voice  from  any  text  prompt.  This  capability  is  similar  to  research  like  Microsoft’s  VALL-E,  which
demonstrated zero-shot voice cloning from a few seconds of audio. The model underlying Voice Engine had
been in development for two years and was hiding “in plain sight” – it actually powers the preset voices of
OpenAI’s text-to-speech API and the voice output in ChatGPT’s mobile app. In terms of architecture, OpenAI
hasn’t published detailed specifics, but we can infer it uses a sophisticated neural codec or embedding
approach to capture voice characteristics. It likely encodes the 15s sample into a speaker embedding, and
uses a multi-speaker TTS model (possibly an auto-regressive or diffusion-based model operating on audio
codec tokens) to generate new speech in that voice. The fact that it’s described as a “small model” suggests
OpenAI found an architecture efficient enough to run relatively quickly (important for real-time applications
like ChatGPT’s responses). The speech quality is reportedly highly realistic – the cloned voices preserve
accent, tone, and prosody of the original speaker, even expressing emotions to some degree. Critically,
OpenAI has not widely released Voice Engine to the public as a general tool, due to concerns about misuse
(e.g., impersonation and deepfakes). Instead, they have run a limited preview with trusted partners and
implemented safeguards: requiring explicit consent from any individual whose voice is cloned, prohibiting
certain use cases, and embedding an imperceptible watermark in generated audio to trace its origin. These
safety measures reflect the ethical challenges of voice cloning, which we discuss later. From a technical
standpoint, Voice Engine demonstrates that  few-shot voice cloning is now feasible with high fidelity – an
achievement  made  possible  by  combining  large  training  datasets  of  diverse  voices  with  advanced
generative modeling. OpenAI’s work in this area is a significant contribution, as it promises more natural
and personalized TTS systems (e.g., allowing users to have an AI speak in their own voice for assistive
technology).

Google’s TTS capabilities are likewise very advanced and have been integrated into the Gemini ecosystem.
Google has a history of research in expressive TTS, from WaveNet to Tacotron to more recent models like
AudioLM and VoiceFilter. In Gemini 2.5, Google introduced controllable TTS as a native feature. This means
developers can prompt Gemini to generate speech with specific styles, tones, or emotions using natural
language  instructions.  The  system  can  produce  anything  from  a  neutral  newscaster  voice  to  a  lively
storytelling tone, or mimic accents and even whisper, all at the user’s request. Gemini’s TTS supports
multilingual output (24+ languages) and can seamlessly switch languages or do code-switching in one
utterance. A striking feature is multi-speaker dialogue generation: Gemini can take text for a conversation
between two characters and generate an audio clip where two distinct voices perform the dialogue, making
the output more engaging. The underlying technology likely builds on Google’s research in cascaded audio
models – possibly using a Transformer-based language model to generate semantic or acoustic tokens
which are then rendered to speech via a learned codec (Google’s AudioLM, for instance, uses SoundStream
codec tokens and Transformers to generate coherent speech with long-term consistency). Google has
reported that their models move “beyond naturalness” to give unprecedented  control over style and
delivery. Unlike OpenAI’s Voice Engine, Google’s public offerings do not currently allow arbitrary voice
cloning from user-provided samples – instead, Google provides many preset high-quality voices and the
ability to modulate them. This difference stems from policy rather than technical inability; Google no doubt
has the technical means for voice cloning (they demonstrated Speaker Adaptation in TTS research), but like
OpenAI, they are cognizant of misuse risks. One important contribution from Google is the development of
SynthID for audio, a watermarking technology embedded in all AI-generated audio from Gemini 2.5. This
allows detection of AI-generated speech to help combat deception. In summary, Google’s Gemini approach
to speech generation emphasizes integration (TTS as part of a larger assistant that can think and then
speak) and fine-grained controllability in real time. Latency has been optimized to be  “very low” for fluid
conversation – likely by using a faster decoding model (Gemini 2.5 has a “Flash” mode for speedy responses,
in addition to a “Pro” mode for highest quality).
DeepSeek has been slightly less public about any text-to-speech or voice cloning capabilities. The DeepSeek
models are primarily known as text generators and multimodal analysts. However, given DeepSeek’s goal to
be a general AI, it could be extended to generate audio as well. There is no indication that DeepSeek can
clone arbitrary voices at present. It may rely on external TTS engines to speak its responses (for example, a
user could use DeepSeek to produce a text answer and then use a third-party TTS to vocalize it). That said,
one of DeepSeek’s headline features is music generation, which is a form of audio generation. DeepSeek-
V3 has been highlighted for its ability to generate music compositions with AI. This suggests DeepSeek
trained a specialized component (or expert) on music data, potentially similar to how OpenAI’s  Jukebox
model works or Google’s MusicLM. Jukebox (OpenAI, 2020) was a pioneering model that generated songs
with lyrics by modeling audio at the waveform level with a hierarchical VQ-VAE and Transformers. Jukebox
was extremely computationally heavy – taking about 3 hours to generate 20 seconds of music on a V100 GPU
– and its output, while often coherent in style, could be hit-or-miss in musical quality. By 2023, Google
introduced MusicLM, a text-to-music model that produces 24 kHz audio consistent with a given description
(and can even be conditioned on humming)
. MusicLM outperformed previous systems in quality and
could  maintain  musical  structure  over  several  minutes
.  However,  Google  initially  did  not  release
MusicLM publicly due to concerns about copyright (the model was trained on thousands of hours of music,
potentially memorizing riffs). In 2024, DeepSeek’s team seemingly managed to create an AI music model at
least on par with those Western efforts. The DeepSeek-V3 music generation model (and its open-source
distilled versions like R1-Distill) can be deployed locally, offering both cloud-based and offline generation of
music. While details are scant, it likely uses a combination of massive model size and mixture-of-experts to
capture different facets of music (melody, harmony, instrumentation). The significance is that DeepSeek’s

In summary, AI audio generation has reached a point where speech synthesis is nearly indistinguishable
from human speech and can be personalized (OpenAI and Google’s contributions), and music synthesis is
becoming realistic (Google’s and DeepSeek’s contributions). The architectures enabling this include large
Transformer models operating on learned audio representations, often with the help of discrete audio codes
(from neural codecs) to manage the high data rate of audio. These models can capture not only the content
of speech or music but also style and timbre, allowing for emotive and context-appropriate generation.
They increasingly operate in  real-time or near-real-time, making interactive voice AI feasible. The next
sections will compare these platforms on key metrics and discuss how they perform in live settings (real-

Real-Time and Offline Capabilities
A critical practical consideration for audio AI is whether it can run  offline (without internet/cloud) and
whether it can operate in real-time for live interactions. We examine each platform with respect to these
capabilities:
OpenAI (Whisper & Voice Engine): Whisper’s open-source release means it can be run offline on
local hardware. Many users run Whisper on laptops or even smartphones (the smallest Whisper
models) to transcribe audio privately. The trade-off is speed: Whisper-large, with ~1.5B parameters,
typically requires a GPU to run faster than real-time. On CPU, real-time transcription is achievable
only with the tiny or base models, or by tolerating some delay. Nonetheless, the ability to run
completely offline is a major advantage for privacy-sensitive applications (e.g. transcribing meetings
or voice notes without sending data to the cloud). OpenAI’s Voice Engine, on the other hand, is not
available for local use – it’s only on OpenAI’s servers for now. This means voice cloning with OpenAI
tech currently requires cloud access and is limited to approved partners. For real-time use, as
discussed, OpenAI has optimized its pipeline so that ChatGPT with voice feels interactive. When you
speak to the ChatGPT mobile app, your speech is transcribed (likely by Whisper or a variant) nearly
instantly, and GPT-4 responds; the response text is then spoken by Voice Engine with minimal delay.
This whole loop is near real-time but does involve cloud processing at each step (the app streams
your voice to the server). If connectivity is lost, these features would not function since the heavy
models aren’t running on the device. In summary, OpenAI enables offline use for ASR (via Whisper
open source) but not for voice cloning/TTS (Voice Engine is cloud-only). Real-time is achievable with
OpenAI’s cloud (they demonstrate it in ChatGPT voice conversations), but using Whisper offline in
real-time may require powerful local hardware or reduced model sizes.
Google Gemini (and Cloud Speech services): Google’s ecosystem historically offers both cloud and
some offline options. For example, GBoard’s voice typing can run on-device for a few languages
using a small recurrent model. The new Gemini audio capabilities, however, are currently cloud-
centric – they are accessed via Google’s AI Studio or API endpoints. Gemini is far too large to run on
common personal devices in its full form. Instead, Google might deploy smaller distilled models to
devices in the future (just as they did with earlier voice models). At present, we can consider Gemini’s
advanced  audio  understanding  and  generation  as  an  online  service.  This  means  an  internet
connection to Google’s servers is needed to use features like real-time multilingual conversation or
controllable TTS. Google’s Cloud Speech-to-Text API does have an on-premises version and even an
offline SDK (for embedded usage, Google released “Speech On-Device API” for some enterprise
applications), but those use older models (not the latest Gemini/USM). The real-time performance of
Google’s cloud is excellent: many developers use Google’s streaming ASR API to transcribe audio on-
the-fly with only a few hundred milliseconds of latency. Likewise, Cloud TTS can stream audio as it’s
generated. For instance, when using Google Assistant, after you finish speaking a question, the
assistant typically responds almost immediately; behind the scenes, Google’s ASR may only need
~200 ms after you stop talking to finalize the transcript (due to endpointing algorithms), and the
response is generated and spoken with perhaps ~500 ms of additional delay. These sub-second
timescales make the interaction feel fluid. Gemini 2.5’s own demonstrations at Google I/O 2025
showed live conversation where the system would speak with “remarkable quality... delivered with very
low latency”. Therefore, for real-time applications (like interpretation, live assistance, etc.), Google’s
platform is very well-suited  when online. If offline operation is required, Google’s most advanced
models are less accessible than Whisper – one would likely fall back to smaller models (which have

currently offers the best end-to-end latency and smoothness, with OpenAI a close second when using its
cloud (as evidenced by the new voice mode in ChatGPT). DeepSeek would rank third, suitable for near-real-
time at best (short delays), but not instantaneous responses. The choice often boils down to the use case:
for a live bilingual translator, one might lean on Google; for a privacy-sensitive transcription tool with no
internet, one would use Whisper; for an in-depth analysis of audio content (like extracting insights from a
recorded  meeting),  one  might  accept  a  slower  DeepSeek  pipeline  for  its  advanced  understanding
capabilities.

AI models for audio understanding and generation have already begun transforming numerous industries

Voice Assistants and Conversational AI: Perhaps the most visible application is in virtual assistants
(like Siri, Alexa, Google Assistant, and now ChatGPT’s voice mode). These systems rely on robust ASR
to understand user queries, natural language understanding to interpret and reason, and TTS to
reply in a friendly voice. Advances by OpenAI and Google directly enhance these assistants. For
example,  Google  Gemini’s  real-time  dialog  capabilities  will  likely  power  the  next  generation  of
Google Assistant, allowing it to handle complex queries with reasoning and respond fluidly in
multiple languages. OpenAI’s technology enables ChatGPT with voice, where Whisper transcribes
what the user says and GPT-4 plus Voice Engine respond with a human-like voice. This opens up
interactive chat with an AI as a hands-free, eyes-free experience – users can have a conversation with
an AI as if they were talking to another person. DeepSeek could be used in specialized assistants that
need higher-level cognitive functions – for instance, an AI doctor’s assistant that listens to a patient
consultation (transcribed by Whisper or USM) and then uses DeepSeek to suggest diagnoses or
questions. In customer service, these models can drive voice bots that handle calls, providing more
natural interactions. Speaker identification can also be used in this context for personalization
(recognizing a user’s voice to retrieve their preferences or authenticate identity, although caution is
needed as voice can be spoofed in the era of cloning).
Transcription  and  Media  Captioning: A  straightforward  but  impactful  application  of  ASR  is
transcribing  audio  and  video  content  into  text.  This  is  useful  for  creating  subtitles  for  videos,
generating  meeting  minutes  from  discussions,  or  enabling  search  within  audio  archives  (like
podcasts or call logs). OpenAI Whisper’s high accuracy and open availability have made it a go-to
tool for many media professionals – for instance, journalists transcribing interviews or YouTubers
auto-generating captions for their videos. Google’s ASR (through services like YouTube’s caption
auto-generation and Google Meet live captions) has scaled this to millions of users, providing real-

raw transcript of a meeting and produce an abstractive summary or identify key action items
(combining  its  language  understanding  with  the  transcription).  Indeed,  workflows  have  been
proposed where Whisper does the transcription and DeepSeek (R1 LLM) summarizes the result. This
division of labor plays to each system’s strength. Overall, transcription automation saves countless
hours of manual typing and enables content to be consumed in different forms (audio turned to
text).

languages, preserving the host’s own voice timbre. This is revolutionary for media localization –

Ethical Considerations
The rapid advancement of AI in generating and interpreting audio raises several  ethical and societal
concerns. It is crucial to address these issues proactively to ensure these technologies are used for benefit
and not harm. We discuss key ethical considerations, focusing on those pertinent to OpenAI’s, Google’s, and
DeepSeek’s audio AI:
Voice Cloning and Misinformation: The ability to clone a person’s voice from a small sample
(demonstrated by OpenAI’s Voice Engine and similar systems) poses a risk of  deepfake audio.
Malicious actors could impersonate someone’s voice – for example, generating fake audio of a public
figure saying something they never said, or tricking someone into thinking a family member called
requesting emergency money (a known phone scam scenario). The realism of today’s synthetic
voices means humans often cannot distinguish fakes by ear alone. This challenges the trust we place
in audio evidence. OpenAI and others are acutely aware of this risk. In response, OpenAI has not
released Voice Engine widely and has built in safeguards: any usage requires the explicit consent of
the person whose voice is cloned, and the generated audio is watermarked for detection. They
also call for  “voice authentication experiences” – essentially, if voice is used in an application,
there should be verification steps to ensure the real person is behind it. Google similarly embeds
identifiers (SynthID) in AI audio so that if an audio clip is in question, a detection algorithm can
confirm it as AI-generated. Despite these measures, the broader concern remains: bad actors won’t
necessarily respect ethical guidelines or use watermarking. Society will need to adapt by being
skeptical of audio recordings unless verified, similar to how deepfake videos have made us question
video evidence. Regulatory responses are being discussed – for instance, laws that criminalize
certain uses of voice deepfakes (especially if used to defraud or slander). The ethical imperative for
AI developers is to keep improving detection, share tools with the public to check authenticity, and
educate users that “hearing is not always believing” in the AI era.
Privacy and Surveillance: Ubiquitous audio understanding can be a privacy double-edged sword.
On one hand, users worry that devices or services are “always listening.” Models like Whisper can run
offline, which is better for privacy (no audio leaves your device). But cloud-based services (Google

data handling. If DeepSeek or others process personal conversations, there’s the issue of how those
transcripts might be used or stored. Companies must ensure strong privacy policies, limit data
retention, and possibly allow opt-outs. Another angle is surveillance: in theory, a government could
use mass AI listening to monitor citizens (e.g., identifying protest chants on streets from city CCTV
mics, or flagging keywords in phone conversations). This is not new (wiretap and keyword-spotting
tech existed), but AI makes it more scalable and accurate. Ethically, this use of AI is contentious – it
pits security against civil liberties. Democratic oversight and transparency are needed to prevent
abuse of such powerful listening capabilities. The companies themselves usually abide by privacy
regulations (like not storing user voice data without consent, etc.), but it’s a space to watch. 
Bias and Fairness: AI models can inherit biases present in their training data. For ASR, this often
manifests as differing accuracy for speakers of different demographics. For example, if a model was
trained  mostly  on  American  English,  it  might  struggle  with  heavy  accents  or  dialects  (as  was
historically the case – systems performed worse on African American Vernacular English, etc.).
Google’s USM tried to cover many languages and dialects to reduce this bias, and OpenAI’s huge
training set for Whisper similarly included diverse accents. Still, bias can creep in. It’s important to
evaluate models on diverse datasets (male/female voices, various ethnic accents, different ages) to
ensure equitable performance. For TTS, fairness might involve representing a wide range of voices –
not just having default voices that reinforce stereotypes (e.g., always providing a female assistant
voice by default, which some argue reinforces gender roles of women as assistants). With cloning,
there’s also a concern of misuse – e.g., could someone clone the voice of a minority figure to make
fake statements that incite hatred? That crosses with misinformation too. Ethical development calls
for including voices from different backgrounds in training and testing, and possibly giving users
control (like the ability to choose the gender or style of assistant’s voice that they are comfortable
with). Encouraging diversity in AI-generated media (for instance, not all audiobook AIs should have
the same standard accent) can also be positive.
Intellectual Property and Consent: Audio data often contains content protected by copyright –
songs, performances, etc. Training a model on someone’s recorded voice or music potentially raises
IP issues. OpenAI’s Voice Engine was trained on “licensed and publicly available data”, but details are
not public. There is an ongoing debate: does training on copyrighted audio (without permission)
count as fair use or infringement? Several lawsuits have been filed in related domains (e.g., Getty
Images sued Stable Diffusion’s makers for image training data). In music, artists are concerned that
AI may be learning their “style” without compensation and could produce new songs “in the style of” a
particular artist, which might compete with the artist’s work. One article described DeepSeek’s music
model as a “wake-up call” because the music industry data is being leveraged by AI without clear
frameworks. Ethically and legally, it will be important to establish norms – perhaps artists can opt out
of their works being used in training sets, or they could receive royalties if an AI-generated piece
clearly borrows from their creations. OpenAI’s voluntary commitments hint at respect for creators
(they allow artists to opt-out from image model training, for instance), and one could foresee similar
moves for audio. When it comes to personal voices, consent is paramount. It is generally unethical
(and in some jurisdictions illegal) to clone someone’s voice without their permission, especially for
public figures (impersonation). OpenAI’s usage policies for Voice Engine prohibit impersonating
individuals without consent
 and even suggest a “no-go voice list” (e.g., disallowing the cloning of
voices of prominent figures who haven’t agreed to it). This could be implemented by checking any

Impact on Human Labor and Creativity: The deployment of AI audio technologies will inevitably
affect certain jobs and creative processes. For example, transcriptionists and captioners might find
less  demand  for  their  services  as  ASR  automates  their  work.  Voice  actors  and  narrators  are
concerned that voice cloning could reduce opportunities – why hire a voice actor for a minor role
when an AI can mimic a famous actor’s voice or generate a generic voice cheaply? Musicians worry
that AI-generated music could flood the market or be used in place of human composers for
background music, jingles, etc. These concerns are similar to those raised in other fields with
generative AI (visual art, writing). Ethically, it’s important to ensure these technologies are used to
assist and not purely displace humans, and to find new roles for human expertise (for instance, a
voice actor might license their voice to an AI company and get paid for its usage, or musicians might
use AI to enhance their productivity rather than replace it). We should also value transparency: if
something is AI-generated, should it be labeled as such? Both OpenAI and Google have endorsed
labeling AI-generated content (hence watermarks), which helps maintain honesty with audiences
and preserves the value of human-made art when advertised as such. The creative landscape might
shift to one where human and AI collaboration is the norm – ethically, we should navigate that
transition in a way that respects the contributions of human creators and doesn’t deceitfully pass off
AI work as human or vice versa. 
Safety and Abuse Prevention: Both OpenAI and Google emphasize safety research in the context of
audio  AI.  Beyond  misinformation,  there  are  other  misuse  scenarios:  harassing  people  with
generated messages, creating obscene or hateful audio content, etc. While these companies cannot
control everything users do with the tech, they can implement guardrails. OpenAI likely has filters to
prevent Voice Engine from being used to generate certain content (for example, the API could reject
requests that seem to be generating hate speech in someone’s voice). Google’s Gemini, by virtue of
being  an  assistant,  has  conversation  safeguards  –  it  recognizes  user  tone  and  could  refuse  if
someone tries to make it say something malicious in audio form. DeepSeek being open-source (in
parts) could be used by anyone, which raises the usual open AI issue: how do you restrict misuse if
the model is out in the wild? Some argue open models empower good and bad actors equally, so
responsibility falls more on users or policy. Closed models allow companies to implement usage
policies, but then those policies need to align with societal values. The partnership with policymakers
is vital; indeed, OpenAI and others have made voluntary commitments to safety and external input.
For audio AI, this might involve working with organizations on deepfake detection, contributing to
standards (like audio watermarking formats), and staying ahead of emerging threats (for instance, if
voice AI is used to generate ultrasonic sounds as a weapon, an edge-case but worth thinking about).
In summary, the ethical landscape for AI in audio is complex and evolving. The major developers are aware
of these issues: OpenAI explicitly started public conversations on responsible deployment of synthetic
voices, and Google has built in transparency features. It will require continuous vigilance, interdisciplinary
collaboration (tech, law, ethics, media), and likely new laws or norms to ensure these powerful tools are
used in ways that enrich society while minimizing harm. As we develop even more capable audio AI (e.g.,
models that can generate complete AV experiences or perfectly mimic anyone), the ethical stakes will only
grow, making this an essential part of any discussion on audio AI advancements.

Limitations and Future Directions
Despite the impressive progress in AI for audio, current systems have several  limitations. Recognizing
these limitations is important for guiding future research and setting appropriate expectations. We also
discuss promising directions for future improvements, some of which are already on the horizon.
Current Limitations:
Accuracy in Adverse Conditions: Even the best ASR models can falter under tough real-world
conditions – heavy background noise, multiple people talking over each other, strong accents/
dialects that were underrepresented in training, or speech impaired by medical conditions. Whisper
and Google’s ASR have made strides in noise robustness, but anyone who’s used auto-transcription
in a crowded environment knows errors still occur (e.g., a live concert or a busy cafe yields messy
transcriptions). Likewise, speaker diarization is not perfect: it might confuse speakers if their voices
are similar or if many talk at once. Sound classification models might misidentify unusual noises.
These models lack common sense understanding of context that a human listener might use (for
example, knowing that at a beach, a certain muffled sound is likely wind or waves, not applause).
Handling cocktail-party scenarios (separating voices) is still a challenge, though techniques like
source separation (e.g., “unmixing” audio into individual speakers) are improving and might be
integrated into future ASR. 
Latency vs. Quality Trade-offs: To achieve real-time performance, models often operate with small
context windows or use approximations that can degrade output quality slightly. For instance,
streaming ASR might have lower accuracy than batch ASR because it can’t leverage future context as
much. In Google’s Gemini, the “Flash” mode sacrifices some fidelity for speed. So users face a trade-
off: either slight delays for the highest quality (Gemini Pro, Whisper large, etc.) or instant results that
might  be  slightly  less  accurate  or  less  natural  (Gemini  Flash,  Whisper  small).  There’s  ongoing
research to narrow this gap, but it’s a limitation that real-time and highest-quality are not always
simultaneously achievable with current technology, especially on edge devices with limited power.
Generative Quality and Coherence: For longer outputs, maintaining coherence is difficult. In TTS,
generating a very long passage can lead to odd prosody or loss of the speaker’s style unless carefully
controlled (some TTS systems break paragraphs into sentences to handle this). For music, models
like MusicLM or DeepSeek might do well for, say, a 3-minute piece, but would they hold structure for
a 10-minute symphony with evolving themes? Possibly not yet; they might regress into looping
patterns or lose musical development. Jukebox had this issue, often meandering or creating surreal
shifts beyond a certain duration. We should note that evaluation of generative audio quality is partly
subjective;  while  MOS  can  measure  naturalness,  judging  musical  quality  or  appropriateness  is
nuanced. So, current models might produce “technically correct” audio that still lacks the emotional
impact or creativity of human-produced content. For instance, AI music might sound bland or
derivative at times, since it’s essentially interpolating seen examples rather than truly innovating
(though some argue at sufficient complexity, the line blurs).
Limited  Understanding  of  Semantics  in  Audio: While  speech  AI  is  good  with  words,  AI’s
understanding of non-speech audio (like interpreting what a particular sound means in context) is
still limited. For example, if an audio has the sound of glass breaking and a dog barking, a human
inferential leap might be “perhaps a window broke and it scared the dog.” An AI might detect “sound

efforts  in  multimodal  models  to  handle  this  (e.g.,  image+audio  understanding),  but  it’s  early.
DeepSeek or GPT-4 with plugins could be tasked with reasoning about an audio scene transcript or
description, but a lot of high-level understanding is left to humans. This is a limitation when trying to
use AI for tasks like audio surveillance or content analysis in videos – it might miss the forest for the
trees.
Energy and Compute Consumption: The largest models, like DeepSeek’s, consume enormous
computational  resources  for  training  and  inference.  This  has  environmental  and  economic
implications. Whisper can run on smaller devices, but training Whisper took a lot of GPU time on
those 680k hours. Google’s 12M hours of training for USM is huge – albeit self-supervised, which is
efficient per unit data but overall massive. The energy cost of running these models at scale (e.g.,
millions of voice assistant queries per day) is non-negligible. Latency improvements often come by
throwing  more  compute  (like  running  on  big  clusters  to  parallelize  response  generation).  For
sustainable AI, there’s a need to make models more efficient – through model compression, better
algorithms, or hardware acceleration (like more efficient ASICs for audio processing). 
User Acceptance and Trust: This is more of a soft limitation – users may not fully trust or accept AI
in certain roles yet. For example, an AI-generated news report read by an artificial voice might be
met with skepticism, or a musician might be reluctant to use an AI-generated riff in their song due to
authenticity concerns. Some users simply prefer the human touch (e.g., many still enjoy audiobooks
narrated by humans because of the acting performance, something AI voices, while improving, may
not fully replicate the subtle artistry of a trained actor). Thus, even if the tech is capable, adoption
might lag due to human factors, and we must consider how to earn trust (through transparency,
gradual improvements, or hybrid approaches where humans oversee or fine-tune AI outputs).
Future Directions:
Advanced Multimodal Integration: One exciting direction is tighter integration of audio AI with
other modalities. Google Gemini already points this way: imagine an AI that can watch a video (visual
input), listen to its audio, and understand the whole scene holistically – that’s very useful for tasks
like video captioning or guiding a robot. For instance, future models could take in both sound and
vision to do  audio-visual scene understanding (e.g., see a door closing and hear a slam, then
deduce someone left the room angrily). OpenAI’s GPT-4 has vision input; perhaps an audio input
modality might come (we could foresee a “GPT-5” that you can speak to directly, and it hears the tone
of your voice, etc.). DeepSeek too would benefit from better sensory fusion – maybe having a
module that directly processes audio waveforms and interacts with its text modules. This multimodal
fusion would lead to richer AI assistants (ones that can, say, take a phone call and also look up
relevant info while listening). 
Personalization  and  Adaptability: We  anticipate  models  that  can  personalize  on  the  fly  to
individual users. For ASR, that might mean learning a user’s unique accent or vocabulary quickly
(imagine a personal ASR model that adapts to your voice after a few uses and gets significantly more
accurate for you than a generic model). For TTS, personalization could mean generating speech that
matches not just a given speaker but also a user’s preferred style (maybe you like your AI to talk
faster or to use a certain playful tone). Both OpenAI and Google have taken steps here – e.g., style
controls in Gemini TTS, and OpenAI’s focus on cloning specific voices. Future improvements might

allow everyone to have their own custom AI voice: you speak a few lines and your devices thereafter
talk back in your voice (some might find that eerie, but others could use it for continuity, say a GPS
navigation in your own voice). On the understanding side, adaptability also means  contextual
learning: if an AI is used in a workplace, feeding it domain-specific audio (like meetings of a
company) should help it learn jargon and speaker roles to better transcribe and summarize future
meetings. Techniques like on-the-fly learning, federated learning on device, or efficient fine-tuning
(like  Low-Rank  Adaptation,  LoRA,  applied  to  audio  models)  could  facilitate  this  personalization
without requiring full retraining.
Improving  Naturalness  and  Emotion: While  current  synthetic  voices  are  very  natural,  there’s
always room to be more human-like. One frontier is emotional expressiveness – not just adding a
happy or sad tone on command, but dynamically modulating emotion as a human would. This
involves understanding context deeply (so the AI knows when to sound excited vs. serious). Prosody
prediction is still an area of research; the nuances of stress and intonation sometimes escape neural
TTS, leading to speech that is good but subtly “flat” or off in emphasis. Future models might
incorporate better linguistic and world knowledge to inform prosody (almost like an actor who first
understands  the  meaning  of  a  line  and  the  subtext  before  reading  it).  Also,  laughter,  sighs,
hesitations – these paralinguistic features are part of natural speech. We may see TTS that can emit
a polite chuckle or a thoughtful “hmm” where appropriate (some current systems can insert a breath
sound or a sigh from a fixed library, but making it contextually appropriate is key). The Gemini article
mentions non-speech vocalizations like laughter as something it recognizes and presumably could
emulate. In generated music, increasing the  creativity and  originality of compositions is a goal –
avoiding plagiarism of training data melodies and perhaps introducing controlled randomness or
“inspiration” to create novel art. There is interesting work in AI that attempts to ensure generated
content is not just a regurgitation but has novelty; applying that to music (maybe by combining
influences in new ways) could produce truly interesting compositions.
Robustness and Verification: Future research will also focus on making models more robust and
secure. By robust, we mean resistant to adversarial attacks or weird inputs. For instance, adversarial
noise could trick an ASR into hearing words that aren’t there; making models robust to that is
important especially for security domains. Verification and detection tools will improve – perhaps
every AI audio will come with a cryptographic signature or hidden mark so that any platform (Twitter,
YouTube) can automatically flag “this audio was AI-generated” before it spreads. Watermarking
research is ongoing and making progress in making marks that survive compression and mild edits.
As audio deepfakes get better, detection must too; future detection might involve cross-modal
checks (e.g., an AI that listens to a voice and looks at a supposed video of the person speaking and
sees mismatches in micro-expressions vs. voice, etc.). This cat-and-mouse game will continue, and
involvement from organizations like Adobe (with their Content Authenticity Initiative) or coalitions of
AI firms could lead to standards for authenticity metadata in media files.
Hardware Acceleration and Edge AI: On the deployment side, the future will likely bring more
specialized chips for running AI audio tasks efficiently, enabling more on-device processing. Already,
some  smartphones  have  “neural  processing  units”  that  can  handle  tasks  like  voice  wake-word
detection with negligible battery impact. We may see high-end earbuds that do on-device noise
suppression and even live translation between two people in conversation without sending data to
the cloud. Tiny ASR models might live in appliances for offline voice control (privacy-first smart home
devices). There is also interest in low-power music generation – imagine a portable device that can

New Modalities and Capabilities: Future AI might handle audio in ways we haven’t fully explored –
like audio-to-image (creating images from sound, e.g., “draw what this soundscape sounds like”) or
audio-based reasoning (using clues in a speaker’s tone to gauge their mood or truthfulness –
though  fraught  with  uncertainty).  Also,  integrating  brain-computer  interfaces:  some  research

what someone is attempting to say via neural cues. This crosses into neuroscience and is probably
further out, but not science fiction given early experiments. In music, AI might help with mastering
and audio engineering tasks (automatically mixing tracks, adjusting equalization to professional
quality). These expanded uses will further blur the lines between creative tool and creative agent.
In summary, the field of AI audio is vibrant with opportunities for improvement. Many of the current
limitations (noise robustness, data efficiency, expressiveness) are active areas of research and engineering.
The future points toward AI systems that are more integrated, personalized, efficient, and trustworthy.
We can expect to converse with AI more naturally, hear AI-generated content that moves and entertains us,
and simultaneously rely on AI to keep the use of audio data honest and secure. Achieving all this will require
collaboration between researchers, industry, and users to guide these models in a direction that augments
human capabilities and experiences while mitigating downsides.
Conclusion
Artificial intelligence has rapidly advanced the frontier of what machines can hear and create in the realm of
audio. In this paper, we examined how AI models have achieved impressive levels of proficiency in audio
understanding (like  transcribing  speech,  identifying  speakers,  and  classifying  sounds)  and  in  audio
generation (synthesizing realistic speech and even music). We focused on the contributions of OpenAI,
Google’s Gemini, and DeepSeek – three leading efforts that encapsulate the trends in this field. OpenAI’s
Whisper ASR and Voice Engine demonstrate the power of specialized models trained on massive data to
achieve robust, near-human performance in transcription and voice synthesis. Google’s Gemini represents
the integration of those capabilities into a single multimodal AI system that can engage in real-time audio
dialog and controlled speech generation with remarkable quality. DeepSeek’s enormous mixture-of-experts
models illustrate a strategy of sheer scale and unified learning, yielding breakthroughs like high-fidelity AI
music generation
 and multimodal understanding across text and audio within one system.
Our comparative analysis shed light on the strengths and trade-offs of each approach. OpenAI’s models,
being  open  or  in  preview,  have  democratized  access  to  cutting-edge  audio  AI,  but  OpenAI  has  also
navigated the  ethical deployment challenges carefully (for instance, delaying public release of voice
cloning until safety is assured)
. Google’s approach, backed by its vast compute and data, currently leads
in multilingual and real-time capabilities, making its AI attractive for interactive and global applications.
DeepSeek’s approach pushes the envelope in model size and multimodality, hinting at a future where a
single AI can seamlessly handle speech, text, images, and more – though with present limitations in speed

DeepSeek’s music generation potentially leading a new wave in creative AI). For latency and deployment,
Google and OpenAI’s cloud implementations enable real-time voice AI for wide audiences, whereas offline
use at the edge is currently better supported by smaller models like Whisper or distilled DeepSeek variants. 
We explored how these technologies are being applied – from voice assistants that allow natural spoken
conversations with AI, to automatic captioning that makes media more accessible, to creative tools that can
compose music or generate voice-overs at the click of a button. The applications are broad and growing,
spanning  industries  from  entertainment  to  education  to  healthcare.  Each  application  brings  value  –
whether it’s saving time (through transcription), breaking language barriers (through translation and cross-
lingual voice cloning), or providing assistance and accessibility (through screen readers or voice for the

generated audio, usage policies restricting abuse

must  continue.  Policymakers,  the  AI  community,  and  society  at  large  will  need  to  collaborate  to  set

making it illegal to clone someone’s voice without consent). 
Looking forward, the trajectory of audio AI points to even more  immersive and intelligent auditory
experiences. We foresee AI that can truly understand context and emotion in voices, making interactions
more empathetic, and AI that can generate rich audio-visual narratives, blurring the line between listener
and  participant.  Future  models  will  likely  be  more  efficient,  addressing  the  current  dependency  on
enormous datasets and compute through algorithmic innovations so that high-quality audio AI can run
locally and securely. They will also likely be more collaborative, serving as creative partners to musicians,
translators for diplomats, tutors for students, and assistive friends to those in need. 
In conclusion, the field of AI audio has entered an exciting era where long-standing challenges like real-time
speech recognition and natural speech synthesis are effectively solved, and new frontiers like open-domain
audio dialog and AI-driven music creation are emerging. OpenAI, Google’s Gemini, and DeepSeek exemplify
the  diverse  strategies  to  advancing  this  field  –  from  open  models  enabling  widespread  adoption  to
integrated AI assistants redefining user interactions, to massive models pushing performance boundaries.
Each contributes to an ecosystem of tools that, if developed and deployed responsibly, can significantly
enhance human capabilities and connectivity. As we continue to refine these technologies and address their
societal impacts, we move closer to a world where speaking to an AI is as effortless as speaking to a person,
and where the creative and communicative potential of audio is amplified by artificial intelligence in a
multitude of positive ways.
