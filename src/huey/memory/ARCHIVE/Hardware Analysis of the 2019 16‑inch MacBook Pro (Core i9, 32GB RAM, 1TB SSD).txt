Hardware Analysis of the 2019 16‑inch MacBook
Pro (Core i9, 32GB RAM, 1TB SSD)
Introduction
The  2019 16‑inch Apple MacBook Pro represents a high-end mobile workstation of its era, combining
powerful Intel 9th-generation processors with Apple’s refined hardware design. This analysis examines the
key hardware components and design features of the 16″ MacBook Pro configured with an 8-core  Intel
Core i9-9880H CPU,  32 GB DDR4 memory, and a  1 TB NVMe SSD, alongside its integrated and discrete
GPUs. We explore detailed specifications and performance characteristics of each subsystem – from CPU
architecture and thermal management to memory bandwidth, storage throughput, GPU capabilities, and
beyond – with an eye towards how this hardware environment supports demanding tasks and operating
system  deployment.  We  also  compare  this  MacBook  Pro’s  hardware  to  contemporaneous  devices  to
contextualize its strengths and weaknesses for developers and engineers considering it as a platform.
CPU: 9th-Generation 8‑Core Intel Core i9 Specifications
The 2019 16″ MacBook Pro can be equipped with Intel’s  Core i9-9880H, a 9th-generation “Coffee Lake
Refresh” mobile processor. This CPU is manufactured on Intel’s 14 nm++ process and features 8 cores / 16
threads with support for Hyper-Threading. It runs at a base clock of 2.3 GHz and can turbo boost up to
4.8 GHz on a single core under Intel Thermal Velocity Boost
. The chip has 16 MB of L3 cache and
uses the Intel Coffee Lake microarchitecture (essentially an enhanced iteration of the Skylake core). It’s a
BGA packaged mobile processor (soldered to the logic board) with a default TDP of 45 W (and a configurable
TDP-down of 35 W for OEMs who choose to limit power)
. In this MacBook Pro, the i9 is paired with a
dual-channel memory controller supporting  DDR4-2666 memory (up to 128 GB theoretically)
. The
processor  also  integrates  an  Intel  UHD  Graphics  630 GPU  (discussed  later)  and  various  platform
controllers. In terms of thermal and electrical limits, the Core i9-9880H has a junction temperature max of
100 °C and relies on robust cooling to sustain its higher turbo frequencies.
Performance and Thermal Characteristics: The Core i9-9880H is one of the fastest laptop CPUs of 2019. It
delivers  strong  single-thread  performance  (thanks  to  high  turbo  clocks)  and  excellent  multi-thread
throughput for a mobile platform. For example, this MacBook Pro’s i9 scores on the order of  1,300+ in
single-core and 6,000–7,000 in multi-core Geekbench 5 CPU tests
 – a level rivaling many desktop-
class systems from a few years prior. Cinebench R20 (a pure CPU rendering benchmark) reports around
3057 points multi-core and 453 points single-core for the i9-9880H in macOS
, reflecting its capability
in sustained heavy workloads. Achieving the maximum 4.8 GHz on this chip is only feasible on one or two
cores under optimal conditions (utilizing Thermal Velocity Boost); under all-core loads, the chip typically
tops out around 4.1 GHz on 8 cores (or ~3.3–3.8 GHz after prolonged heavy usage when constrained by 45 W
TDP)
. The MacBook Pro’s improved cooling (discussed later) allows the i9 to maintain high clocks
remarkably well for a laptop – in a sustained compilation test (all cores maxed for an extended period), the
i9 stabilized around 3.3 GHz at ~94 °C without significant throttling
. Nonetheless, this CPU will run very
hot under full load – it is not unusual to see package temperatures in the mid-90s °C during intensive

Memory Configuration: 32 GB DDR4-2666
The analyzed configuration includes 32 GB of DDR4 SDRAM, which in Apple’s design is soldered onboard
(not socketed). The memory operates at  2666 MT/s (MHz) with a 64-bit dual-channel interface (2×64-bit
channels) as supported by the Intel CPU
. At 2666 MT/s, the dual-channel memory controller provides a
theoretical peak bandwidth on the order of  40–42 GB/s
, which is beneficial for feeding the 8-core
processor and the integrated GPU. Apple uses high-density chips to achieve 32 GB: in this model, there are
no SODIMM slots, but rather DRAM packages mounted directly on the logic board (eight ICs per channel,
for a total of 16 memory chips in the 32 GB configuration). In the iFixit teardown, the 16 GB base model was
populated by “16× Micron 8 Gb DDR4 SDRAM” chips
, and the 32 GB upgrade would use higher-density
16 Gb chips in a similar arrangement.
From  a  performance  standpoint,  32  GB  of  fast  DDR4  provides  ample  headroom  for  professional
workloads. It allows heavy multitasking, large image or video edits, running multiple virtual machines, and
other  memory-intensive  operations  to  run  without  paging.  The  dual-channel memory  configuration
doubles the theoretical bandwidth versus single-channel and helps maximize CPU performance (especially
for  memory-bound  tasks  or  integrated  GPU  usage).  The  RAM  operates  with  typical  DDR4  latency
characteristics; while not as high frequency as some desktop DDR4 (which could reach 3200+ MT/s in 2019),
2666 MHz is the maximum officially supported by this Intel platform, and it strikes a balance of throughput
and stability. The 32 GB capacity is particularly useful for developers compiling large codebases or running
Docker containers, and for video editors who work with high-resolution footage that can consume tens of
gigabytes in caches and frames. It’s worth noting that the memory is  not upgradeable post-purchase
(soldered), so what the machine is built with is final. Fortunately, 32 GB was the high-end option for this
model (with an even higher 64 GB option available from Apple as a CTO upgrade
), and it should satisfy
all but the most extreme mobile use cases. The tight integration of RAM on the board also has minor power
and space-saving benefits, which Apple leverages to maximize battery life and minimize physical volume.
Storage: 1 TB PCIe/NVMe SSD Analysis
Apple outfitted this MacBook Pro with a 1 TB solid-state drive that uses a PCIe 3.0 x4 NVMe interface. The
SSD is not a removable module but rather integrated flash storage chips on the logic board, managed by
Apple’s custom controller (embedded in the Apple T2 security chip). In the 16″ MacBook Pro teardown, iFixit
identified Toshiba (Kioxia) flash NAND chips on the board (the 512 GB model had two Toshiba packages
totaling 512 GB) along with the Apple T2 co-processor
. The 1 TB configuration likely uses a combination
of multiple NAND chips (e.g., four 256 GB dies or two 512 GB packages) operating in parallel to achieve high
throughput. Apple’s T2 chip serves as the SSD controller and encryption engine – the storage is encrypted
by  default  and  the  T2  presents  it  to  the  system  as  an  NVMe  APFS  volume with  high  security  and
performance. This tight integration means the SSD is soldered and not user-replaceable, similar to the
RAM.

In terms of performance, Apple’s SSDs have historically been among the fastest in laptops, and the 2019
MBP is no exception. Disk benchmarks show sequential read and write speeds around 2.8–3.2 GB/s. For
instance, using Blackmagic Disk Speed Test in macOS, the 1 TB drive hits roughly 2800 MB/s in both read
and write
. More granular benchmarks (like AS SSD or CrystalDiskMark in Windows via Boot Camp)
reveal the drive can even exceed 3.0 GB/s on sequential reads (e.g., ~3242 MB/s read, ~2727 MB/s write in
one test)
. Random 4K read/write performance is also strong, though the MacBook’s SSD sometimes
trails the very fastest desktop SSDs in certain 4K queued tests – for example, ~16 MB/s 4K random reads
and ~40 MB/s writes at low queue depths
, and much higher (~1.2–1.7 GB/s) at high queue depths
,
which  is  still  excellent.  Overall,  this  storage  easily  handles  large  file  transfers,  high-resolution  video
scrubbing, and rapid application load times. In real-world terms, the machine can copy multi-gigabyte files
in  mere  seconds  (internal  file  transfers  measured  around  2   GB/s  for  large  files)
.  The  NVMe
interface and  Apple’s  controller  also  mean  very  low  latency  and  high  I/O  operations  per  second,
contributing to the snappy responsiveness of the system.
One notable aspect of Apple’s SSD design is the reliance on the T2 chip: it provides hardware AES encryption
without  performance  loss  and  integrates  with  macOS  for  secure  boot  and  seamless  disk  encryption.
However, this design also ties the drive to the logic board – if the board fails, data recovery is non-trivial (one
must have a special DFU tool and access via the T2, since the NAND chips cannot simply be removed and
read due to encryption – even removal is impractical as they are soldered)
. For deployers of operating
systems, the storage subsystem appears as an “Apple SSD AP1024” (for 1 TB) and uses Apple’s NVMe driver.
It supports PCIe Gen3 x4 bandwidth, AHCI/legacy mode is not used. Thermal throttling of the SSD is rarely
an issue thanks to the efficient controller and the fact that the chips are spread out on the board (and
possibly covered by a shield or thermal pad – iFixit noted a  copper heat spreader over the SSD and
GDDR6 VRAM chips to dissipate heat
). The net result is an extremely fast and reliable storage solution
well-suited for heavy-duty tasks like 4K video editing (where multiple streams of data are read/written) or
compiling large code (lots of random reads/writes), which this MacBook handles with ease.
Graphics: Integrated Intel UHD 630 & AMD Radeon Pro 5500M
Integrated GPU (Intel UHD 630): The Core i9 CPU includes an Intel UHD Graphics 630 GPU, which has 24
execution units (EUs) running up to  1.20 GHz dynamic frequency
. This integrated graphics processor
shares system memory (it can address up to 1.5 GB or more of the RAM dynamically, and Intel specifies up
to 64 GB as VRAM maximum in theory
). In the MacBook Pro, the UHD 630 is used for light graphics
tasks, desktop display rendering on battery, and video decode/encode acceleration via Quick Sync. It
supports modern codecs (e.g. H.264, HEVC 8/10-bit decode) which helps in media playback and low-power
video conferencing. By itself, the UHD 630 is relatively modest in performance – adequate for driving the
high-resolution internal display and basic GPU tasks, but not suitable for intensive 3D gaming or GPU
compute. Its main advantage is power efficiency: the system automatically switches to the integrated GPU
when the discrete GPU isn’t needed (a feature called “Automatic Graphics Switching” in macOS) to save
energy. The Intel iGPU also provides Quick Sync video encoding/decoding which can significantly speed up
tasks like video exporting (especially H.264/H.265 encoding) while using much less power than the CPU
would. In summary, the UHD 630 handles everyday graphics, UI animations, and video playback efficiently
and hands off the heavy lifting to the AMD GPU when required.
Discrete GPU (AMD Radeon Pro 5500M): For demanding graphics workloads, this MacBook Pro relies on a
dedicated GPU: the AMD Radeon Pro 5500M. This GPU is built on AMD’s 7 nm RDNA architecture (first
generation Navi, similar to the desktop Radeon RX 5500 series) and was a new option introduced with the

. In GTA V (1080p, Very

. This means the
MacBook’s GPU can handle modern games at moderate settings and is well-suited for pro applications like
3D modeling, CAD, or GPU-accelerated compute tasks (OpenCL/Metal).
The  thermal design power (TDP) of the Radeon Pro 5500M is on the order of ~50 W (estimated; Apple
doesn’t quote a number, but the desktop RX 5500 XT was ~130 W for 24 CUs at much higher clocks, and the
mobile 5500M is significantly downclocked and optimized for mobile). The MacBook Pro’s cooling system
has to dissipate heat from both CPU and GPU when the latter is active. Apple uses a shared heat pipe
design with dual fans – when the GPU is under heavy use (e.g., rendering or gaming), one can expect
system temperatures to rise and fan noise to increase. Under a pure GPU stress test (e.g., looping a Unigine
Heaven benchmark), the 5500M stabilized at around  75 °C with no significant throttling observed
,
indicating the cooling can sustain the GPU’s workload. However, when both the CPU and GPU are stressed
simultaneously, they share thermal headroom; the system may downclock one or both to stay within safe
temperatures  (~100 W  combined  package  perhaps).  Apple’s  power  management  will  often  prioritize
workload on the GPU vs CPU depending on the task – for instance, during a video export, the GPU might
run near full while the CPU, though active, might not hit maximum turbo on all cores to keep the total
system draw in check. This balance is generally handled gracefully, and in professional apps like Final Cut
Pro or Adobe Premiere, the user benefits from both chips working together (CPU for compression, GPU for
effects/filters and encoding) to deliver fast results.
Usage Contexts: In day-to-day operation, macOS will use the integrated Intel GPU for most basic tasks
(web browsing, email, coding, etc., especially on battery power) and will automatically switch to the AMD
5500M when an application demands higher graphics performance (e.g., launching Adobe Photoshop,
running a game, using an external 4K monitor, or anything that triggers the use of Metal/OpenGL explicitly).
The switch is seamless, though users can force the high-performance GPU if needed. The  AMD 5500M
supports Metal 2, OpenGL 4.1, and OpenCL on macOS, and it fully supports modern APIs (DirectX 12/
Vulkan  in  Windows  via  Boot  Camp  as  well).  For  compute  tasks,  it  can  be  used  in  GPU-accelerated
frameworks (like Metal Performance Shaders or OpenCL-based tools). Creatives will find that the 5500M
significantly accelerates rendering and export tasks in pro software: for example, in Final Cut Pro X, effects
and timeline rendering that leverage Metal run much faster on the 5500M than on the integrated Intel GPU.
Similarly, DaVinci Resolve can use the 5500M for color grading and filters, and Adobe’s apps use it for GPU-
accelerated effects and playback. The 4 GB VRAM on this model is sufficient for typical pro workloads (even
4K video editing and moderate 3D scenes); however, Apple offered an 8 GB VRAM upgrade which could
better handle very large datasets or higher fidelity textures in 3D. There was also a higher-end Radeon Pro
5600M option with 40 CUs and 8 GB of HBM2 memory for those needing top-tier laptop GPU performance,

Thermal Design and Performance Throttling
Figure: The MacBook Pro 16″ employs two large blower-style fans (shown) and an enlarged heatsink to cool the
Core i9 CPU and Radeon GPU. The fans’ impellers are redesigned to increase airflow, a key part of the thermal
improvements in this 2019 model.
One of the headline improvements of the 16-inch MacBook Pro (2019) over its 15-inch predecessors is its
revamped thermal design. Apple acknowledged that the increasing core counts and power of Intel’s CPUs
demanded more aggressive cooling. Consequently, the 16″ model’s internal cooling system was enlarged
and optimized: the heatsink is roughly 35% larger in volume (with more surface area and mass for heat
absorption) compared to the 15″ design
, and the fans are upgraded with larger impeller blades and
bigger vents, capable of moving about 28% more air through the chassis
. The fan outlets on the back
of the machine are slightly wider, and the fan blades’ geometry was modified to increase airflow without a
proportional increase in noise. These changes allow the 16″ MacBook Pro to sustain about 12 W higher
thermal dissipation under load than before – an Apple engineering claim that means the cooling system
can handle roughly 12 more watts of heat continuously before temperatures hit throttling limits
. This is
significant in a system where the CPU and GPU combined can draw 80–100+ W under heavy use. Effectively,
the laptop can maintain higher clock speeds for longer durations on the CPU/GPU, rather than quickly
throttling down due to heat saturation as was observed in the 2018 Core i9 models. In the iFixit teardown,
these improvements were clearly visible: the fans and heatsinks are physically larger, and even touches like
thermal pads and a copper heat spreader were added on certain components (like VRAM) to aid heat
dissipation
.

under intensive workloads. Reviews and tests found that the  Core i9-9880H was able to run sustained

multi-core  tasks  without  significant  frequency  throttling  or  dips  in  performance  over  time.  For

also encoding video on the CPU), the MacBook Pro will reach a point where it must lower clocks to keep
component temperatures at or below 100 °C. But the threshold for this is much higher than before. In
balanced professional workflows (like editing video, where the GPU and CPU load seesaws, or compiling
code, which hammers the CPU but leaves the GPU idle, or vice versa), the MacBook maintains boosted
frequencies impressively well.
During intensive tasks, the fan noise and surface temperatures do increase. Users reported that under
full  load,  the  fans  can  reach  ~5500   RPM  and  become  quite  audible  (around  45–50   dB).  Rtings’
measurements showed that at idle the machine is cool and nearly silent, but under a heavy combined load,
the keyboard area (especially around the “I”/“O” keys above the CPU) can get very hot to the touch
and fan noise includes a noticeable high-pitched component
. This is an inevitable consequence of
pushing ~90+ W of heat in a slim laptop. However, Apple’s design ensures the internal components are kept
within safe limits – the chassis heat is a side effect, but the silicon is being cooled effectively enough to
avoid thermal shutdown or severe slowdowns. Notably, VRM and power delivery have also been sized to
handle the power; no reports of VRM overheating have surfaced, meaning the power management circuitry
is adequate.
In summary, the thermal design of the 16″ MacBook Pro represents a significant step up,  mitigating
throttling and enabling the Core i9 and Radeon 5500M to run at high performance for sustained
periods. Apple’s claim of a 35% bigger heatsink and 28% more airflow is substantiated by the machine’s
behavior in practice
. This gives developers and power-users confidence that intensive tasks (like back-
to-back code compilations, long video exports, or extended gaming sessions) can be handled without the
CPU quickly dialing down to base clocks as was sometimes seen in prior models. It is a balance – the laptop
will  run  hot  and  loud  to  achieve  this  –  but  that  is  the  trade-off  for  maintaining  performance.  Good
ventilation around the laptop and possibly a cooling pad can further help for marathon workloads, but even
out of the box, the 2019 MacBook Pro 16 maintains a much more stable performance profile under load
compared to its predecessors.
Battery and Power Management Hardware
The MacBook Pro 16″ has a built-in 100 W·h lithium-polymer battery, which is extraordinarily large for a
laptop and in fact the maximum size allowed on flights by the FAA (100 Wh is the limit before requiring
special approval)
. Apple utilized nearly every cubic millimeter of space for battery cells: this model’s
battery capacity is about 16 Wh (approx. 20%) greater than the previous 15″ MacBook Pro’s ~83.6 Wh pack
, thanks in part to a slightly thicker chassis and rearranged internals. The battery is composed of six
cells spread under the palm rest area (with one cell slightly thicker to add capacity). In iFixit’s examination,
the battery is rated at 11.36 V, 8790 mAh, which indeed comes out to ~99.8 Wh (Apple rounds to 100)
.
This is the largest battery ever put in a MacBook up to that point.

For battery life, Apple officially rated the 16″ MacBook Pro at “up to 11 hours” of wireless web or movie
playback on a charge
. Real-world endurance varies with workload, but for light-to-moderate use one
can often get 8–10 hours, which is impressive given the powerful components. The increase in battery
capacity from the prior generation yielded roughly an hour more runtime in Apple’s tests (10 → 11 hours)
. However, some of that extra battery was also allocated to feeding the hungrier CPU/GPU – the net
battery life gain was not directly proportional to the 20% capacity bump, indicating the new components
draw more power under load
. In practice, when doing non-trivial work on battery (programming,
compiling, editing photos, etc.), users report around 5–7 hours. Heavy use (like continuous 3D rendering or
gaming on battery) will drain it much faster (under 2–3 hours, as both the CPU and GPU can draw
substantial power). The system does intelligently throttle some when on battery to prolong usage – for
example, the Core i9 may not sustain full 4+ GHz clocks on battery if it would exceed safe thermal/power
limits without the adapter, and the discrete GPU will only run at levels that balance performance with
battery drain. Still, it’s possible to exhaust the battery in ~1 hour if one were to run a power virus test – that
scenario draws ~100 W (close to the battery’s energy content per hour).
The laptop comes with a 96 W USB‑C Power Adapter (charger) and a 2 m USB-C charging cable
. This
high wattage is necessary to both run the system at full tilt and charge the battery simultaneously.
When the MacBook is working very hard (CPU+GPU maxed, ~90–100 W consumption), the 96 W adapter can
essentially cover most of the load and still slowly charge the battery. The charging circuitry is managed by
Apple’s  power  management  ICs  in  conjunction  with  the  SMC  (System  Management  Controller,  now
integrated into the T2 chip). The T2 chip plays a significant role in power management and monitoring – it
communicates  with  the  battery  microcontroller  to  report  charge  levels,  controls  charging  currents
(including the “Battery Health Management” feature introduced in macOS which can limit max charge to
preserve battery longevity), and handles the power gating of various components. The SMC/T2 ensures the
system draws from the adapter or battery optimally, and in scenarios where instantaneous load exceeds the
adapter (rare, but say a quick spike to 110 W), the battery can supplement the adapter briefly (this prevents
voltage drop or system brown-out). This technique is common in high-performance laptops (sometimes
called “Hybrid Power”). The user doesn’t notice anything; it’s all seamless.
From a hardware perspective, the power delivery on the logic board consists of multiple voltage regulator
modules (VRMs): high-phase-count VRMs for the CPU and GPU to convert the battery’s ~11 V or adapter’s
~20 V (USB-C PD delivers ~20 V) down to the sub-1.2 V needed by the silicon, plus additional regulators for
memory, PCH, etc. These VRMs are high efficiency but do produce heat – they are placed near the CPU/GPU
and likely cooled indirectly by airflow. Apple’s design is robust enough that even when both CPU and GPU
draw maximal power, the system remains stable (VRM overheating was not documented as an issue). 
Battery Integration and Charging: The battery itself is glued into the top case with strong adhesive, as
per iFixit’s teardown
. Apple did make slight design changes – the 16″ battery has pull-to-remove
adhesive tabs (similar to iPhone batteries) which theoretically allow a technician to remove the battery
without prying solvent, a small improvement for service (though still not “easy”). The Touch Bar, keyboard,
and trackpad all have their own power requirements but are low-power components relative to the CPU/
GPU. The trackpad is large but uses very little energy; the keyboard backlight and Touch Bar can use a bit of
power when on, but negligible compared to the main chips. The  power management circuitry also
involves the Intersil/Renesas chips for charging (likely a buck converter for USB-C input) – these ensure USB-
C Power Delivery negotiation and safe charging at up to 96 W (20 V @ 4.7 A). Apple’s charging system can
fast-charge the MacBook Pro to ~50% in about an hour and a full charge in 2 – 2.5 hours (when the laptop is
asleep/off; faster than previous 85 W chargers due to the extra 11 W).

Display: 16″ Retina Panel Characteristics
The MacBook Pro 16″ features a 16.0-inch (diagonal) Retina display that is one of its standout features.
This is an IPS (In-Plane Switching) LCD panel with a native resolution of 3072 × 1920 pixels, yielding a pixel
density of about 226 ppi
. At this resolution and size, the display is extremely sharp, and macOS uses
HiDPI scaling (pixel-doubling for UI elements) to provide both crisp text and ample real estate. The aspect
ratio is approximately 16:10, which is standard for MacBook Pros, giving a bit more vertical space than 16:9
panels found in many other laptops. 
Brightness and Color: The display is rated for 500 nits maximum brightness
, which makes it usable
even in fairly bright ambient conditions. In practice, it indeed reaches ~500 cd/m² at max setting, which is
excellent for a non-HDR laptop screen. It supports Wide Color (P3 gamut)
, meaning it can display a
wider color range than the traditional sRGB gamut – specifically, the DCI-P3 color space commonly used in
professional media. This is great for photographers, video editors, and anyone doing color-critical work, as
the  panel  can  show  richer  saturation  in  reds  and  greens  that  sRGB  panels  would  clip.  According  to
reviewers’ colorimeter measurements, the panel achieves essentially 100% of sRGB and 100% of DCI-P3
coverage, with very good factory calibration (average color accuracy Delta-E around 1–2, which is excellent
and essentially imperceptible error). The display also maintains Apple’s typical gamma and white point
calibration, targeting ~6500 K white. Additionally, it features True Tone technology
 – a sensor-based
system that adjusts the white balance and color tone of the display based on ambient lighting warmth. True
Tone helps keep whites looking consistent (less bluish under warm indoor lighting, for example), which is
easier on the eyes for everyday tasks (though professionals often disable it when doing precise color work).
Refresh Rate and Response: Uncommonly for Apple, the 16″ MacBook Pro’s display allows selection of
multiple refresh rates: 47.95 Hz, 48.00 Hz, 50.00 Hz, 59.94 Hz, and 60.00 Hz
. This is a boon for video

I/O and Connectivity Hardware
The 2019 16″ MacBook Pro continues Apple’s trend of an all USB-C/Thunderbolt 3 I/O configuration. It
provides four Thunderbolt 3 (USB-C) ports – two on the left side and two on the right side
. Each of
these ports is a versatile connector supporting: Thunderbolt 3 (40 Gbps bi-directional bandwidth, used for
high-speed peripherals and eGPU, etc.),  USB 3.1 Gen 2 (10 Gbps, for USB devices),  DisplayPort 1.4 (for
video output, as TB3 essentially encapsulates DP signals), and of course charging input
. Practically, this
means any port can be your charging port, display output, or data port as needed. The machine has two
Thunderbolt 3 controllers (Intel “Titan Ridge” JHL7540 chips) on the logic board
, each controlling two of
the USB-C ports. This architecture ensures full Thunderbolt bandwidth on all ports (each pair shares a TB3
controller with 8x PCIe lanes total available). A developer using this MacBook Pro might connect multiple
devices – for instance, an external 10 Gb Ethernet adapter, a 4K monitor, a fast external SSD, and an audio
interface – and the Mac can handle it by allocating across the TB3 channels.
For legacy connections, since only USB-C physical ports are present, one must use adapters or docks. The
MacBook Pro does not have built-in USB-A, HDMI, SD card slot, or Ethernet. This minimalism is a known
point of contention – many Windows laptops of similar size in 2019 (Dell XPS 15, HP laptops, etc.) included at
least USB-A or HDMI. Apple’s philosophy was that Thunderbolt 3/USB-C could replace all those via adapters.
For example, an HDMI output requires a USB-C to HDMI dongle or a multi-port hub; an SD card requires a
reader;  Ethernet can  be  achieved  via  a  Thunderbolt  or  USB-C  adapter  (even  10  GbE  adapters  exist,
leveraging TB3’s bandwidth). While this approach adds some cost and complexity, it also future-proofs the I/
O and simplifies the machine’s lines. Developers who need to plug in various devices (JTAG debuggers, USB
keys, external displays) often carry a small USB-C hub/dock. It’s worth noting that Thunderbolt 3 ports
support daisy-chaining and very high-bandwidth devices (you could, for instance, connect an external GPU
chassis to greatly boost graphics performance, a use-case Apple supports in macOS).
Aside from the USB-C ports, the MacBook Pro 16″ does include a classic 3.5 mm headphone jack on the
right side
 – one of the last remaining dedicated ports. This jack is a 4-conductor TRRS port that supports
Apple’s earbud headsets with mic, etc. The internal audio DAC/amp is high quality, and the output can drive
typical headphones well. There’s no line-in jack (Apple dropped that years prior), so external mic input
would be via USB or the headset pinout on the combo jack.
Wireless Connectivity: This MacBook Pro features 802.11ac Wi-Fi (Wi‑Fi 5) with 3×3 MIMO support
. It
operates on dual-band 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz and is backward compatible with 802.11a/b/g/n. Apple did not
update to Wi-Fi 6 (802.11ax) in this 2019 model – that came later with Apple Silicon Macs. Some competing
2019 laptops (like the Dell XPS 15 7590) did start offering Wi-Fi 6 cards (e.g., Killer AX1650 based on Intel’s
AX200 chip)
, which can have higher throughput and better performance in dense environments. In
practice, the MacBook Pro’s 3-stream Wi-Fi 5 can achieve up to ~1.3 Gbps PHY rate and around 600–
800 Mbps of real-world throughput (as measured with iperf tests) which is plenty for most use cases
.
Unless one has a Wi-Fi 6 router and gigabit internet or local servers that can push beyond ac’s limits, the
lack of Wi-Fi 6 isn’t strongly felt. Still, it’s a point of comparison – in a lab or office with Wi-Fi 6 infrastructure,
those newer cards could get 40–50% higher real throughput. The Mac’s wireless card is likely a Broadcom or
Apple proprietary module integrated onto the logic board.
Bluetooth is 5.0, which was the latest at the time
. This provides improved range and bandwidth (LE up
to 2 Mbps) compared to older BT 4.2. It’s used for all manner of peripherals – keyboards, mice, AirPods/

Repairability and Component Integration
Figure: Logic board of the 16″ MacBook Pro, highlighting the high integration: the central Intel CPU (red outline)
and AMD GPU (yellow) are soldered BGA chips, surrounded by VRAM (green), system RAM (orange), the Apple T2
chip (blue), and other controllers. Almost all major components are soldered directly to the board, reflecting
Apple’s integration-first design.
The 2019 16″ MacBook Pro’s hardware is highly integrated, favoring a slim, compact design over modularity
or  easy  serviceability.  Repairability  is  thus  very  limited –  iFixit  awarded  this  model  a  1  out  of  10
repairability  score  (10  being  easiest  to  repair)
,  noting  that  most  components  are  either  glued  or
soldered in place. For a technical audience, it’s important to understand which parts can be replaced or
upgraded and which cannot:
Soldered CPU/GPU/RAM/SSD: The Intel Core i9 CPU and the AMD Radeon GPU are both BGA-
mounted and not removable (as is standard in laptops). More critically, the  memory (RAM) and
storage (SSD) are soldered to the logic board as well. There are no SO-DIMM slots or replaceable
SSD modules. The 32 GB RAM and 1 TB NAND flash storage in our configuration are permanently
attached. This means one cannot upgrade the RAM or SSD after purchase, and a failure of the logic
board usually means losing all those components in one swap. The SSD being onboard and tied to
the T2 chip also means data recovery is difficult if the board fails – one can’t simply remove an SSD;
Apple has tools to interface with the T2 for data transfer in some cases, but it’s not trivial for an end
user
. The upside of this integration is potentially better performance and compactness; the
downside is zero field-serviceability or upgradeability for these parts.
Battery  glued  to  chassis: The  100   Wh  battery  is  glued  in  using  adhesive  strips.  Apple  did
incorporate stretch-release adhesive tabs for this battery (a slight improvement over previous glued
batteries), but it’s still a delicate operation to remove and replace it. The battery is also quite large
and must be removed as a whole unit (though it’s composed of 6 cells, they are not individually
replaceable). Essentially, battery replacement is doable, but intended to be done by Apple or a repair

glued and not meant to be replaced on its own (it would be part of the top case assembly if serviced).
The Touch ID sensor is uniquely tricky: it’s cryptographically paired to the T2 security chip on the
logic board. If you replace the Touch ID button, it will not function as a fingerprint sensor unless
paired by Apple’s proprietary tools. Essentially,  Touch ID (which doubles as the power button)
cannot be replaced independently of the logic board

power button, but Touch ID functionality will be lost. This is a security feature to prevent tampering,
but it adds to the repair difficulty.
Speakers and other components: The 16″ MBP has speakers flanking the keyboard, which are
glued in with some adhesive (and possibly small screws). These can be replaced, but you have to
remove the battery first to get them out (since they’re tucked slightly under it). The Wi-Fi/Bluetooth
module is on the logic board (not a separate card in this model), so not replaceable individually. The
fans themselves (two fans) are modular – they can be unscrewed and replaced relatively easily (so if a
fan fails, that is one repair that is feasible without entire system replacement). The cooling system,
however, is mostly a single unit (heat pipes, heatsink) screwed onto the board; unless it’s damaged, it
wouldn’t need replacement, but it can be removed for re-pasting the CPU/GPU, etc., if one dares. The
Thunderbolt ports are soldered onto the logic board (some laptops use daughterboards for ports –
here it’s all one board).
In essence, Apple’s design tightly integrates everything for a thinner, sturdier machine – but this means
nearly all major parts are not user-serviceable or upgradable. For professionals, this has implications:
you must configure the laptop with sufficient RAM and storage from the get-go for future needs, and if
something like the logic board fails out of warranty, repairs will be very costly (since it means replacing the
board with CPU, GPU, RAM, SSD, etc. as one piece). It also means downtime in repairs – e.g., if your
keyboard has an issue, Apple will likely replace the whole top case assembly, which is a big operation.
From a positive perspective, integration yields some benefits: for example, the soldered RAM has slightly
better thermal contact and maybe lower power usage than socketed (very marginal gains); the soldered
SSD via T2 allows extremely fast encryption and reliability; the build is very rigid with less room for
connectors to come loose. The unibody chassis construction and the way components are fitted contribute
to the solidity and premium feel of the device.

Software Compilation (e.g., Xcode / LLVM builds): The 8-core Core i9, fast SSD, and ample RAM make this
laptop a compilation powerhouse relative to previous-generation notebooks. For instance, compiling a large

benchmarked Xcode builds on this MacBook Pro and found that it can approach the performance of some
desktop systems. For example, the MacBook Pro 16″ (i9) can compile a large project in Xcode in about 30

benchmark project on GitHub, the i9 MBP16 (2.4 GHz i9-9980HK, which is very close to the 9880H) achieved
a full build in 32 seconds, whereas older 2018 i7 MBPs took ~50 seconds

workstation-class  compile  performance  in  a  laptop  –  making  tasks  like  building  and  testing  large
applications noticeably quicker and more interactive. As a side effect, because compiling pushes the CPU,
the fans will ramp up during these tasks; but as noted, the machine maintains its performance (it doesn’t
slow down due to thermal throttling in the middle of a build, for example, as some earlier models might

specced Windows workstations from 2019 in raw performance while often surpassing them in sustained
workload handling (due to thermals). The takeaway for deployment scenarios is that this hardware can be
pushed hard and it will deliver, making it a reliable development and content creation platform.
Comparison with Contemporary Devices (Late 2019)
When evaluating the 16-inch MacBook Pro (2019) against other high-performance laptops from the same
period, it’s clear that Apple targeted the top tier of the market. Key competitors include models like the Dell
XPS 15 (7590), Lenovo ThinkPad X1 Extreme Gen 2, HP Omen 15 (or EliteBook workstation models),
and other 15–17″ slim workstations or gaming laptops (Razer Blade 15 Advanced, etc.). We’ll compare
several  aspects:  CPU/GPU  performance,  display  quality,  thermals,  battery  life,  connectivity,  and
upgradability.
CPU and Performance: Most competing 15″-class laptops in 2019 could be configured with similar Intel
45 W CPUs – for example, Dell’s XPS 15 offered the Core i9-9980HK (an 8-core with up to 5.0 GHz turbo). That
chip is basically a slightly higher-clocked variant of the i9-9880H in the MacBook. In short bursts, the XPS
15’s i9-9980HK could indeed outscore the MacBook’s i9 in benchmarks by a small margin (on the order of
~5-10% higher in Cinebench or Geekbench)
, due to its higher single-core turbo (5.0 GHz vs 4.8) and
possibly a higher all-core if it could maintain it. For instance, an XPS 15 (9980HK) might hit ~1600 points in
Cinebench R15 multi
 while the MacBook Pro (9880H) got ~1362, about a 15% difference – but it’s worth
noting the XPS result might have been with the system set to a high-performance mode and plugged in,
and some of those Windows laptops needed manual tuning to prevent throttling. Under sustained load,
many Windows thin laptops struggled with the 8-core heat: the XPS 15 had aggressive power management
to avoid overheating (often locking around 55–60 W CPU package power for short bursts then dropping to
~45 W). The MacBook’s advantage was its beefed-up cooling that allowed a bit more sustained power. In

Graphics Comparison: The MacBook Pro’s Radeon Pro 5500M (4 GB) falls in the same class as an NVIDIA
GTX 1650 (Max-Q) which was commonly found in Dell XPS 15 and Lenovo X1 Extreme models. In fact,
benchmark tests show near-parity: the 5500M’s performance is within a few percent of the GTX 1650 in

. The GTX 1650 might win slightly in some games due to driver
optimization, whereas the 5500M can pull ahead in GPU compute tasks that leverage its architecture. None
of these machines are gaming powerhouses per se – they target a middle ground where they can run
content creation tasks and moderate gaming. Some competitors (like the Gigabyte Aero 15 or Razer Blade
15 Advanced) offered higher GPU options (RTX 2060/2070, etc.), which would outperform the 5500M in
GPU-heavy scenarios (especially gaming or CUDA-specific workloads). But those laptops were more gaming-
oriented and often thicker or louder. Apple did later offer the Radeon Pro 5600M (with HBM2 memory) as an
upgrade, which was more akin to an RTX 2060/2070 Max-Q competitor in 2020. In late 2019, however, the
MBP16’s GPU choice was competitive with the mainstream discrete GPUs in other thin workstations. One
difference: NVIDIA GPUs had CUDA and certain AI/ML frameworks support that AMD lacked. For instance,
someone doing machine learning might have preferred an NVIDIA-equipped laptop to leverage CUDA or
Tensor cores – the MacBook with AMD GPU wouldn’t accelerate those (and Apple’s framework Metal
Performance Shaders wasn’t as widely used in ML at the time). This could be a consideration depending on
workload.
Display Quality: Apple’s 16″ Retina display had a 16:10 LCD with 500 nits and P3 color. Many Windows
competitors in 2019 offered 15.6″ 16:9 displays, often either 4K UHD resolution (3840×2160) or Full HD
(1920×1080) for base models. A notable point: some offered OLED 4K panels (Dell XPS 15, HP Spectre x360
15, etc.), which deliver true blacks and wide DCI-P3 or AdobeRGB color, and support HDR. For example, the
XPS 15’s optional 4K OLED had near 100% DCI-P3 and much higher contrast than the MacBook’s IPS (infinite
contrast effectively)
. That said, OLED laptop screens can be less uniformly calibrated, and they come
with concerns like potential burn-in and reduced battery life. The MacBook’s LCD is extremely high quality
for an IPS – with excellent factory calibration and no risk of image retention. It also has the higher refresh
rate options (the XPS’s OLED was fixed 60 Hz). Another competitor, the ThinkPad X1 Extreme, had a 4K IPS
option that covered AdobeRGB (~100%) – potentially a wider gamut than Apple’s P3 (which is roughly
AdobeRGB minus some cyans). But that panel wasn’t as bright (often ~400 nits) as Apple’s, and again 16:9 vs
16:10 aspect ratio difference (some professionals prefer 16:10 for the extra vertical pixels). Apple’s decision
to stick with ~3K resolution is interesting – it gives a bit better battery life and UI scaling than a full 4K
would. And since they control macOS, they ensured a perfect scaling experience. On Windows, a 4K 15″
screen at 250% scaling can be hit-or-miss with some apps. So the  MacBook’s display can be seen as
trading resolution for efficiency and reliability, whereas rivals might brag higher resolution or OLED
contrast. In summary, Apple’s display holds its own with very accurate color and high brightness, but lacks
HDR/OLED level blacks. If HDR video editing was a priority, one might prefer a laptop with true HDR output
(or more likely, use an external reference monitor).

than the 2018 MacBook. How does it compare to others? The Dell XPS 15 with Core i9 was known to run very
hot and often loudly – its chassis is slightly thinner than the MacBook and some reviewers noted VRM
throttling  and  high  skin  temperatures.  The  MacBook’s  larger  chassis  and  cooling  give  it  an  edge  in

whereas on Mac you get what Apple tunes.
Battery Life: Apple’s 100 Wh battery was class-leading. The XPS 15 had a 97 Wh battery (almost the same).
With similar hardware, actual battery life came down to display power and OS efficiency. macOS is known
for very efficient video playback (especially using the integrated GPU and hardware decoder) – Apple’s claim
of 11 hours movie playback would likely beat an XPS 15 playing the same movie on Windows (Windows
tends historically to yield slightly less due to background processes and less optimization in some cases). In
web browsing, MacBook Pro often achieved 1-2 hours more than an XPS 15 in independent tests, likely due
to macOS power management and perhaps the absence of some telemetry bloat that Windows 10 had. On
the flip side, macOS has had issues where certain background processes (like indexing) can consume
power; but generally, Apple has tight integration that benefits battery life. Another factor: the XPS’s 4K
panel (if chosen) would draw more power than Apple’s lower-res panel, so an XPS 15 4K might only get ~7–8
hours web, whereas the MBP16 gets 10–11 in similar conditions
. If the XPS had the 1080p screen, it
could get closer to the Mac’s longevity because of fewer pixels, albeit sacrificing resolution and gamut. So
Apple’s choice of a not-4K resolution was partly to maintain good battery life while still being Retina. In
short, the MBP16 tends to have equal or better battery life than similarly specced competitors, especially
those with 4K or OLED screens.
Connectivity  and  Ports: Here  the  MacBook  both  shines  and  falls  short  depending  on  perspective.
Thunderbolt 3 on all four ports is a great capability – many Windows laptops at the time only had one TB3
(two at most). For instance, the XPS 15 had one TB3, two USB-A, HDMI 2.0, and an SD card reader. From a
flexibility standpoint, the MacBook has more high-speed ports, but from a convenience standpoint, it lacks
the built-in variety (no USB-A or SD). Professionals often lamented the loss of the SD card slot on MacBooks
– the XPS and ThinkPad kept it (the X1 Extreme had a full-size SD, which photographers appreciate). The
need for dongles was seen as a negative for MacBook, whereas the PC users enjoyed plugging in an HDMI
cable directly for a presentation or a USB drive directly. However, Thunderbolt’s capabilities (external GPUs,
very fast external NVMe drives at 40 Gbps, multiple 4K displays through one port) were beyond what the
other laptops could do on a single port. If one invests in a good TB3 dock or two, the MacBook becomes a
neat single-cable connect to everything at a desk. In contrast, connecting dual 4K monitors to an XPS 15
required using both its TB3 and HDMI, and even then one might be limited by bandwidth on the HDMI 2.0
for 60Hz at 4K. The MacBook can do two 4K displays daisy-chained on one TB3. So connectivity trade-offs
are: MacBook Pro has a very modern, forward-looking I/O setup (great for high-end use with docks/hubs),
but it demands that ecosystem (adapters) for day-to-day legacy device connectivity, which many users still
need. The inclusion of 4 TB3 ports was unmatched by competitors in 2019.

unibody, very little flex, tight tolerances. The 16″ MBP continued that tradition. Comparatively, the Dell XPS
15 had an aluminum lid and bottom, but a carbon-fiber composite keyboard deck; it’s well built but some

creatives prefer Apple’s. 
Audio: Apple  invested  in  a  six-speaker  setup  with  force-cancelling  woofers  in  the  16″,  producing
surprisingly rich and loud sound with stereo separation. By many accounts, it was the best audio from any
laptop in 2019 – reviewers were impressed at the bass and fullness. The XPS 15’s speakers, for example, are
decent but down-firing and not nearly as full-sounding. The MacBook also has a high-quality 3-mic array
that Apple claimed was “studio quality” (overstated, but certainly above average for a laptop – good enough
for clear podcast recording in a pinch). Competing devices generally had okay dual-mic setups, nothing
special. So for built-in AV capabilities (for conference calls, watching movies without headphones, etc.), the
MacBook Pro had an edge.
Upgradability and Repair: Most Windows workstation laptops (XPS 15, ThinkPad X1 Extreme) offer at least
some upgradability – typically the RAM (often 2 SO-DIMM slots) and SSD (M.2 slot) are user-replaceable. For
instance, an X1 Extreme Gen 2 could be bought with 16 GB and later upgraded to 32 GB or 64 GB by the
user (two slots), and it had two M.2 SSD slots for storage RAID or expansion – a clear advantage for
longevity. In contrast, as we discussed, the MacBook Pro’s RAM and SSD are soldered
. So from a value
perspective, the PC laptops might start cheaper (one could buy a lower spec and upgrade aftermarket RAM/
SSD for less than Apple’s upgrade prices). Apple’s approach forces a higher upfront cost for the desired
spec. Also, if something like the SSD fails on a PC laptop, the user could swap it; on the Mac, it means a logic
board replacement. For repairability, the Mac scores lower (1/10)
 vs something like a Dell or Lenovo
which might be 7 or 8/10 (with replaceable battery, RAM, SSD, etc.). That can be significant in enterprise
scenarios – ThinkPads are known for easier servicing (even keyboards can be replaced easily). So if a
company needed to service laptops in-house, the MacBook would be more cumbersome. However, Apple’s
reliability in some aspects is quite high (for example, their SSDs seldom fail outright and have redundancy,
etc.), but when issues do occur, it’s a send-out repair typically.
Price: Not a hardware spec, but for context, the MacBook Pro 16 (2019) with i9, 32GB, 1TB, 5500M 4GB was
quite expensive (over \$3500 USD typically). A comparable XPS 15 or Lenovo might come in a bit lower,
though high-end configurations of those (4K OLED, i9, 32GB, 1TB, GTX 1650) also pushed \$2800+. Apple
tends to be at a premium, but given its included high-res display and other features, the gap isn’t as large
as it once was. For someone comparing devices in late 2019, it might have been a \$300–\$500 difference in
price for similar specs, with MacOS and Apple’s build as differentiators.

like industrial design, display color accuracy (though not in HDR/OLED contrast), audio quality, and
combined performance balance. It is roughly equal in CPU/GPU horsepower to the best of the Windows
