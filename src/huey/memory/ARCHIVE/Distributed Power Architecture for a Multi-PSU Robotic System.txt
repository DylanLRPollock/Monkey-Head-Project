Distributed Power Architecture for a Multi-PSU
Robotic System
Abstract
This report presents a conceptual overview of a distributed power architecture for a robotics prototype that
uses multiple consumer-grade power supply units (PSUs) to power an integrated system. Instead of a single
supply, several off-the-shelf PSUs are employed in parallel (by dividing the load, not by directly paralleling
outputs)  to  drive  three  interconnected  motherboard-based  subsystems  alongside  motors,  sensors,
microcontrollers,  and  auxiliary  devices.  All  PSUs  share  a  common  ground  reference.  We  discuss  the
rationale for this multi-PSU approach in a prototyping context – including cost benefits and capacity scaling
– and describe how power is distributed across subsystems. Key technical considerations are examined,
such as the implications of using a common ground without isolation, potential interference and electrical
noise between subsystems, and risks like backfeeding or instability in the absence of OR-ing diodes or
relays. The challenges inherent to this architecture are analyzed, and we outline how the system could be
refined in future iterations (for example, through improved grounding strategies, power management
circuitry, or consolidation of supplies). The tone of the report is technical yet accessible, aiming to provide
an academic-style perspective on leveraging multiple consumer PSUs in robotics prototyping. Citations are
included to support the discussion in multiple reference styles (APA, MLA, IEEE) where appropriate.
Introduction
Robotic systems typically require multiple voltage rails and substantial currents to support components
ranging from high-performance computing boards to motors and sensors. In professional designs, a
centralized power architecture might use one high-capacity supply (or battery) feeding all components, or
a  distributed  power  architecture with  a  single  high-voltage  source  and  point-of-load  converters  at
subsystems
.  However,  in  early-stage  prototyping,  engineers  often  repurpose  readily  available
consumer-grade  PSUs (such  as  PC  ATX  power  supplies)  to  meet  power  needs.  This  approach  offers
practical benefits: multiple smaller PSUs can provide combined current capacity across different voltages
and can be cheaper or more accessible than a single purpose-built supply of equivalent power
. For
instance, one case study found that using two standard ATX PSUs doubled the available 5 V current (from
~25–30 A to ~50 A) and spread the load and heat dissipation between units, all at roughly one-quarter the
cost of a single high-wattage supply
. In prototyping scenarios, leveraging spare PC PSUs “works in a
pinch” even if it’s a bit of a kludge compared to a unified supply
. 
Rationale for Multiple PSUs: The decision to employ multiple PSUs in the Huey robotic shell (a complex
system with three onboard motherboards and numerous peripherals) is driven by both practical and
technical factors. First, no single consumer PSU on hand may meet the aggregate power requirements –
especially if high currents are needed at standard voltages like 5 V and 12 V for motors, drives, and
computing components. Using two or three smaller PSUs allows splitting of loads, effectively increasing
total available current across rails
. This load-splitting approach has been observed in other high-power
systems; for example, Backblaze’s storage pods used dual ATX PSUs not for redundancy but to share the

System Architecture
Overview: The prototype robotic shell’s power architecture consists of multiple consumer-grade PSUs
powering different portions of the system in a distributed manner. Rather than tying all PSUs together on
the same output, each PSU is allocated to specific subsystems or sets of components. In this design, three
primary subsystems – each built around a motherboard (e.g., single-board computers or a server-grade
motherboard and two auxiliary control boards) – are each powered by one or more dedicated PSUs. All PSUs
share a common ground connection (0 V reference) but their positive outputs are largely kept separate,
each feeding its intended load group. This means, for example, PSU A might supply a 12 V rail to the main
motherboard  and  its  peripherals,  PSU  B  might  supply  another  motherboard  or  high-power  motors/
actuators, and PSU C might power a third control unit and various sensors or auxiliary electronics. By
partitioning the load in this way, the supplies are not directly paralleled on the same output node; instead,
they operate in parallel at the system level by each handling a portion of the total load devices
. This
avoids  the  complexities  of  current-sharing  between  PSUs  on  a  single  rail  and  instead  resembles  a
decentralized power distribution, where multiple sources feed different branches of the system.
Common Ground Strategy: A critical aspect of this architecture is that all subsystems must share a
common reference potential for their signals to interoperate. The ground (negative) terminals of all PSUs
are therefore  tied together, creating a single common ground plane across the robot. This common
ground ensures that any communication signals, sensor references, or control lines between devices
powered by different PSUs have a mutual reference and can operate correctly. In practice, the PSU ground
outputs are connected (often at a star point or common grounding bus in the chassis) so that 0 V is uniform
everywhere
. It’s worth noting that in ATX PSUs and similar, the DC ground is typically also connected to
earth ground; since all PSUs are plugged into the same mains circuit, their earth grounds are inherently
common. Nonetheless, explicitly tying their DC grounds together at a single point helps prevent differences
in ground potential from arising across the robot’s frame. This common ground bus approach is standard
when using multiple supplies – one expert advises to “cross-tie the 0 V ends” of all power supplies in such
multi-PSU setups
 to avoid floating references between them.

By not including any isolation or OR-ing diodes between the grounds, the design essentially treats all
subsystem grounds as one unified node. Signals (e.g., a PWM control from a microcontroller on one board
driving a motor driver on another supply) can flow reliably because the return currents all share that same
ground path. The decision to avoid  diode OR-ing or relay isolation on the outputs means there is  no
intentional voltage drop or separation between the supplies’ outputs and the loads – every PSU is directly
connected to its loads and to the common ground. This simplicity is part of the prototyping philosophy:
fewer components and complexities make it easier to assemble and reconfigure the power system. It is akin
to a rough implementation of a distributed power architecture: each PSU serves as a local power source for
a  cluster  of  components,  somewhat  analogous  to  point-of-load  converters  in  a  formal  distributed
architecture (albeit here the “point-of-load” source is a full PSU)
.
Power Distribution to Subsystems: Power wiring in this architecture is arranged so that high-current
devices are served by the nearest or most capable PSU. For example, power-hungry actuators or drive
motors (which run on 12 V DC) might be wired directly to the 12 V rail of the PSU that also powers a
particular  motherboard,  thereby  sharing  that  PSU’s  capacity.  Other  sensitive  electronics  like
microcontrollers or sensors requiring 5 V or 3.3 V could either be fed from the same PSU’s regulated outputs
or from small DC-DC converters attached to that PSU’s 12 V rail. The three motherboards themselves each
receive ATX power from their respective supplies. Importantly, because all grounds are common,  power
distribution and signal distribution become intertwined: a sensor powered by PSU A can safely send
data to a processor on PSU B, as their grounds are common. The architecture relies on the fact that each
PSU independently maintains its output voltage for its load; there is no centralized power management
ensuring equal load sharing or failover. In essence, the robot has multiple “power islands” (one per PSU)
that are connected at the ground level and via any inter-system signal wiring. 
This kind of segmentation requires careful planning of which PSU feeds which component group. A guiding
principle is to avoid having two different PSUs drive the same exact load or node. There are no parallel
connections of two supplies to one circuit, which means classic current-sharing problems are averted.
Instead, each device or module is clearly assigned to one PSU’s domain. The common ground is the only
intentional connection between the otherwise separate supply domains (aside from data connections). This
approach has precedent in other multi-supply systems. For instance, in large disk storage arrays, one half of
the drives can be run off one PSU and the other half off a second PSU – both PSUs’ grounds are common,
but each drive is powered by only one supply
. Such a scheme ensures that load distribution is achieved
without the supplies electrically fighting each other. It’s recommended in engineering forums that if one
must use multiple PSUs, “split the load into two halves” (or thirds, etc.) and dedicate each supply to a portion
of the devices, rather than directly paralleling outputs
.
No OR-ing Diodes or Redundancy: In this prototype configuration, diode OR-ing (i.e., using diodes or ideal
diode controllers to combine outputs) is deliberately not used. In a redundant power supply design, OR-ing
diodes allow one supply to take over if another fails, without backfeeding current into the failed unit
.
Here, because each PSU has its own dedicated outputs, redundancy was not a design goal – the aim was
increased total capacity, not fault-tolerance. Avoiding OR-ing components saves cost and avoids the voltage
drop that diodes introduce on the lines. It does mean, however, that if one PSU were to lose power, the
devices it feeds would shut down and there is no automatic failover. The other PSUs would continue
powering their sections, so the system as a whole might operate in a degraded state. For example, if the
PSU powering the primary computer fails, the motors and other boards might still have power, but the
“brain” is off – a potentially unstable scenario. Because of the common ground, a powered-down supply’s
output  node  will  be  at  0   V,  effectively  disconnected  from  its  load  (unless  some  other  still-powered

In summary, the system architecture can be viewed as a distributed multi-source power network inside
the robot: multiple PSU “sources” feed various “loads” with all sources joined at ground. This yields a
functional power solution for the prototype stage, leveraging the strengths of consumer PSUs (multiple
voltages,  high  current  on  5   V/12   V  rails,  built-in  protections)  while  acknowledging  their  independent
operation. Next, we explore the challenges and implications that arise from this design, particularly due to
the common ground coupling and lack of isolation between subsystems.
Challenges
While the multi-PSU architecture successfully powers the robot prototype, it introduces several electrical
challenges and risks that must be managed. These challenges stem from the interactions between the
multiple supplies and the connected subsystems, especially via the common ground. Key issues include
ground potential differences and loops, interference and noise coupling between subsystems, the risk of
backfeeding currents between PSUs, stability during load transients, and difficulties in power monitoring
and control. We discuss each of these in turn.
1. Ground Potential Differences and Ground Loops: With all PSUs sharing a common ground, it is crucial
to ensure that this ground is truly at the same potential throughout the system. In practice, if the ground
wiring is not ideal, high current flows can cause slight voltage differences between different ground points –
a phenomenon known as ground offset or ground bounce. For instance, when a heavy motor current
returns to its PSU, the finite resistance and inductance of the ground wire means that section of ground
might  rise  a  few  millivolts  (or  more)  above  the  rest  of  the  ground  plane.  If  at  the  same  time  a
microcontroller ground at a distant point is “quiet,” the difference in ground potential can disturb the
reference for signals between them
. One answer on Electrical Engineering Stack Exchange explains this
using Ohm’s law: any shared ground conductor can be modeled as a small resistor, so large currents through it
cause a voltage drop that appears as noise to other devices on the same ground
. The effect is analogous to
lights dimming in a house when a heavy appliance turns on – the wiring impedance causes a temporary
drop in the shared supply. In a robot, a sudden motor stall current could momentarily raise the local
ground, causing sensor readings or logic levels to shift unpredictably.
Moreover, with multiple PSUs, there is a possibility of ground loops if the ground is connected in more than
one topology. Ideally, all grounds should connect at a single common point (a star ground configuration) to
avoid loop currents. If, for example, PSU A and PSU B are both bolted to the metal chassis which itself is
used as ground, and additionally a cable connects their grounds, a loop is formed. AC currents or noise
could circulate in this loop. The prototype uses a common ground bus to minimize such loops, but care
must be taken in routing ground wires. A recommended practice is star grounding, where each PSU ground
and each major return path meets at one node (or bar) on the chassis
. This limits the ground current
paths such that high return currents (from motors, for example) do not flow through sensitive ground
reference points for electronics. Without isolation, conducted interference is a concern – one PSU’s noisy
ground currents (from a switching regulator or a motor driver it powers) can introduce noise into another
PSU’s ground reference. Indeed, engineers caution that tying grounds together is usually fine, but  “be
cognizant of what return currents are going where and by what path”, especially with high dI/dt loads like

Mitigation strategies for ground issues include using sufficiently thick, low-resistance ground wiring (or
ground planes) to carry heavy currents, thus reducing voltage drops. The prototype should also physically
cluster high-current devices closer to their PSU ground connection to shorten return paths. Star grounding
or a single robust ground bus bar is employed to collect all returns. Additionally, separating analog/sensor
ground returns from power ground returns until a single combine point can help keep noise away from
sensitive circuits (this is a form of ground isolation without an actual isolation barrier). In summary,
maintaining a solid common ground in a multi-PSU system is feasible but requires careful attention to
wiring layout to avoid ground loops and common impedance coupling of noise
.
2. Power Interference and Electrical Noise: A direct consequence of the common ground and the lack of
isolation is that electrical noise generated in one part of the system can propagate into others. Each PSU is
a switching power supply (in the case of ATX units), which inherently generates some switching noise on its
outputs. When multiple such supplies and various loads (especially motors, which generate electromagnetic
interference when their brushes commutate or their drivers chop currents) are all connected, the noise can
sum and transfer through the common connections. For example, if a DC motor (powered by PSU B) is
rapidly changing speed, the current surges and voltage spikes from that motor might inject noise onto PSU
B’s 12 V line and ground. Through the common ground, a portion of this noise could appear as ground
voltage fluctuations that superimpose on PSU A’s outputs as well, potentially disturbing the electronics
powered by PSU A. Similarly, any high-frequency ripple or transient from one PSU can couple into other
subsystems via the shared ground or any other wiring commonalities (like if subsystems share a signal line,
the reference noise can effectively appear as signal noise).
Interference can also occur via radiated paths – multiple PSUs with their own fans, transformers, and
switching circuits can create a complex EMI environment inside the robot’s chassis. Without careful cable
routing  or  filtering,  sensitive  sensor  lines  could  pick  up  this  electromagnetic  noise.  The  architecture
currently does not use isolation transformers or optocouplers between subsystems (since everything is
grounded together), meaning all electronics see each other’s noise. This is an acknowledged trade-off of the
simple approach: keeping circuits separate would eliminate conducted interference, but “unless there’s a
technical reason to have a common ground, it’s almost always best to keep circuits separate” one engineer
notes – in our case we do have a reason (intercommunication) to tie grounds, so we accept the coupling
and try to manage it
.
To manage noise, the prototype can incorporate decoupling capacitors, filters, and shielding. Each PSU
output feeding a load should have local capacitors to absorb transients (e.g., electrolytic and ceramic
capacitors near motor drivers, etc.). Ferrite beads or inductors can be used on supply lines to choke high-
frequency  noise.  In  more  advanced  iterations,  one  could  introduce  isolated  DC/DC  converters for
particularly noise-sensitive components (for instance, running a sensitive sensor array from an isolated
regulator fed by one PSU – the sensor would still ultimately reference ground for data lines, but its power
noise would be isolated). Additionally, good practices like twisting wire pairs and using shielded cables for
signal lines can reduce noise coupling. In the current conceptual design, these measures are noted as
necessary refinements: at the prototype stage, some increased noise floor might be tolerated, but it
underscores the need to refine power distribution in the future to ensure signal integrity.

3. Backfeeding and Cross-Regulation Risks: Backfeeding refers to current flowing from one power
source into another, which is a major risk when multiple supplies are connected without OR-ing diodes or
isolation. In our architecture, we intentionally avoid directly paralleling any two PSUs on the same supply
rail. This greatly reduces the chance of backfeeding under normal operation, since each PSU “sees” only the
loads assigned to it. However, subtle scenarios still exist where backfeed could occur. One scenario is if one
PSU is powered off (or fails off) while another PSU is still on, and there are connections through the loads or
signal lines that provide a path. For example, consider a microcontroller board powered by PSU A that has a
GPIO line connected to a motor driver powered by PSU B. If PSU A is off, the microcontroller is unpowered –
but the motor driver on PSU B might drive that GPIO line high. In doing so, it could inadvertently source
current through the protection diodes of the microcontroller’s input pin, effectively feeding 5 V back into
PSU A’s 5 V rail via the microcontroller. This kind of indirect coupling can power parts of a supposedly off
subsystem at a phantom low level (often called parasitic or “ghost” powering). It’s not the catastrophic type
of backfeed that drives large currents, but it can lead to unpredictable behavior (e.g., an MCU partially
powering through I/O, which can damage components).
The more dangerous form of backfeeding would be if two PSUs were accidentally tied together on a high-
current line (say two 12 V lines joined). Without diodes, the one set at slightly higher voltage would push
current into the other. ATX PSUs are not generally designed to sink current on their outputs; if forced, it
could damage the supply or trigger protective shutdowns
. A powered-off PSU typically presents near
0 V at its output, so if another live PSU is connected, it will drive current into the off PSU’s output capacitors
and circuitry. As an expert succinctly put it, “you can’t back-feed voltage/current into a powered-off supply… or
you can, but the power supply may get destroyed.”
. Therefore, in our design, we absolutely avoid wiring
two PSU outputs together at different potentials. The common ground is the only shared line; all positive
rails go to distinct loads.
However, even with these precautions, one must consider failure modes: if PSU A were to develop a fault
and output an abnormal voltage, could it feed into devices that connect to PSU B and then into PSU B? This
is unlikely unless there is a direct device bridging (which our load partitioning prevents), but it highlights
why in more mature designs OR-ing diodes or FET-based ideal diodes are used – they block reverse currents
entirely.  Our  prototype  takes  the  calculated  risk  of  running  without  those,  relying  on  careful  wiring
separation. The consequence is that there is no protection if a PSU outputs out-of-spec voltages or if
someone  accidentally  cross-connects  outputs.  For  the  prototype  stage,  the  team  is  presumably
managing this by design discipline (clear labeling, harness design) and accepting the risk in exchange for
simplicity.
4. Load Transient Handling and Stability: With multiple independent PSUs, system-wide power stability
can be an issue under fast load changes. Each PSU regulates its own outputs, but consider a scenario where
a large motor on PSU B quickly draws a surge of current (for instance, on startup or when hitting a
mechanical stop). PSU B’s voltage might dip momentarily under the sudden load (even if within spec, there’s
transient droop). PSU A, which might be powering the main computer, is unaffected in terms of its own
output voltage – however, the motor surge might have caused a ground bounce as described earlier, or
even a slight drop in mains voltage that affects all supplies. The result could be the main computer
experiencing a brown-out or a sensor giving erratic readings at the exact moment of the motor surge. In a
single-supply system, all loads share the same transient pool and the supply can be designed to handle it
(or brown-out the whole system uniformly). In a multi-PSU system, one part of the system browns out while
others remain up, which can be a worse failure mode (partial operation, possibly causing uncontrolled
behavior).

Additionally, the power-up sequencing can be a challenge: when turning on the system, if the PSUs are
switched on at slightly different times, some subsystems will come up before others. For instance, one PSU
might ramp to 12 V faster, powering its motherboard, while another PSU is a second behind. This could lead
to  the  first  subsystem  sending  signals  to  the  second  before  the  second  is  powered,  again  risking
backfeeding through signal lines or at least causing communication errors. In our prototype, without a
coordinated power-on mechanism (no relay or single master switch that guarantees simultaneous start),
the user must take care to turn on all PSUs together. There are products (like “Add2PSU” boards in PC
modding) that link PS_ON signals so that a primary PSU can auto-start a secondary – but the design
explicitly did not incorporate such relay triggers. Thus, an operator turning on the robot must remember
the correct procedure to energize all supplies. During operation, if one PSU were to shut down (due to
overload or overheat), there is no graceful handover of its load; that part of the system simply loses power.
This lack of power redundancy or sharing means the architecture is inherently less stable in the face of
individual PSU faults.
To improve stability, one might overspec each PSU (use only, say, 50% of each PSU’s max load in normal
operation) so that transient demands are easily met without significant voltage sag. Also, distributing the
most variable loads (like motors) on separate PSUs from sensitive loads (like computing) can localize
disturbances. This seems to have been done by dedicating different PSUs to different functions. If motors
and logic must run on one PSU, adding large capacitors or even supercapacitors on the motor supply can
buffer the surges. In terms of start-up sequencing, future revisions could employ a master switch or a small
microcontroller circuit that activates all PSUs synchronously (by shorting their PS_ON to ground at the same
time). For now, the prototype likely uses a manual or improvised method (e.g., jumping PS_ON pins and
using a power strip to turn on AC to all supplies simultaneously). These are workable but not ideal solutions,
underscoring that power management is rudimentary in the current setup.
5. Monitoring and Diagnostics: A subtle challenge is the lack of unified power monitoring in a multi-PSU
scheme. Each PSU has its own feedback and protections (shutdown on overcurrent, etc.), but there’s no
single dashboard telling the robot operator how power is distributed or if a PSU is nearing its limit. For
instance, one PSU could be running near maximum load (perhaps the one powering many motors), while
another is lightly loaded. Without monitoring, this imbalance might go unnoticed until the overloaded PSU
triggers a shutdown. In a one-supply system, it’s easier to instrument the current draw and voltage of that
one supply. In the multi-PSU system, one would need multiple sensors – one per PSU output – to get a full
picture. The prototype likely doesn’t include an elaborate power monitoring network (given the focus on
basic functionality). This introduces the risk that an overload condition on one supply is not detected in
time. It also complicates energy management: if the robot were to eventually run on battery power,
knowing how each subsystem draws power is important for efficiency, but with separate PSUs, the data is
siloed unless explicitly gathered.
Furthermore, if something goes wrong – say a short circuit in a sensor board – the corresponding PSU will
shut down to protect itself. The rest of the system stays on, but now part of the robot is unpowered. This
partial failure mode can be hard to troubleshoot if you don’t have indicators for each PSU’s status. It may
not be immediately obvious why a certain subsystem is unresponsive (is it its PSU tripped, or the board
itself died?). Including LED indicators or voltage readouts for each PSU’s output in the prototype can
alleviate this. In a refined design, a supervisory microcontroller could monitor all supply voltages and
perhaps communicate to the main system if any rail falls out of range.

Summary of Challenges: In essence, the multi-PSU architecture trades the convenience of using available
supplies for a set of challenges in electrical integration.  Grounding and noise issues require careful
mitigation to prevent interference between subsystems. The absence of  isolation/OR-ing means careful
wiring discipline is needed to avoid backfeed and to handle failures gracefully (which, in truth, they won’t be
handled gracefully – it’s a known limitation). Stability under dynamic loads is not as robust as it could be,
and there is no inherent redundancy or load-sharing beyond static assignment. Finally, monitoring multiple
independent sources is more complex. These challenges do not mean the architecture is unworkable – only
that it must be acknowledged as a prototype solution. Many of these issues can be managed with good
engineering  practices  (as  noted,  e.g.,  heavy  ground  conductors,  star  topology,  decoupling  capacitors,
prudent load distribution). In the next section, we discuss the implications of these challenges and how the
architecture could evolve to address them in future iterations.
Discussion
The use of multiple consumer-grade PSUs in a single robotic system highlights an interesting balance
between  short-term  pragmatism  and  long-term  design  ideals.  From  a  conceptual  standpoint,  this
architecture embodies a decentralized power approach: instead of one central supply, power conversion is
distributed across several units located near the loads they serve. In fact, it echoes some principles of
formal Distributed Power Architecture (DPA) used in high-end electronics, albeit in a cruder form. In a DPA,
a single high-voltage source feeds many local converters at the loads
. Here, we effectively have multiple
“local” sources (the PSUs) each directly powering certain loads. The benefits realized are immediate – ease
of using existing hardware, increased total capacity by splitting loads, and reduced need for custom power
electronics  at  the  prototype  stage.  The  rationale  discussed  earlier  for  prototyping  (cost,  availability,
flexibility) has indeed played out in practice: the team achieved a working power solution without waiting
for or designing a specialty power supply.
Why It Makes Sense in Prototyping: Prototyping often involves rapidly implementing a solution with
components on hand, then iterating. Consumer PSUs are modular and well-understood – one can add a
new PSU to the system to get an extra 12 V rail in a matter of minutes. This modularity is helpful when the
power budget of the robot is in flux (as features and hardware are added, power needs grow). Using
multiple smaller PSUs also sidesteps the need to immediately invest in a costly high-power supply. As noted
earlier, two standard PSUs can sometimes outperform a single larger unit in specific ways (more 5 V current,
more connectors, etc.)
. Additionally, the common ground scheme makes it straightforward to integrate
subsystems: there is no need for complex level shifting or isolation circuits for every signal, because
everything shares reference ground. This simplicity accelerates development – you can wire components
together freely knowing their grounds are common, which is often assumed in typical development setups.
However, as the previous section detailed, these conveniences come at the cost of potential reliability and
performance issues.  Electrically, the system is less elegant and less robust than it could be. During
prototyping, one might accept occasional quirks (like a noise-induced sensor glitch when a motor starts) as
long as the overall system works. But for a production or advanced prototype, those would need ironing
out.

Evolution and Refinement Opportunities: Looking ahead, there are several pathways to evolve this power
architecture for greater robustness:
Consolidation into a Single Supply or Unified Bus: One straightforward improvement is to replace
multiple PSUs with a single higher-capacity supply that can handle the entire load. By doing so, all
components naturally share the same power source, eliminating any ground differential issues
(beyond internal wiring drops) and removing the chance of inter-supply backfeed. An expert on an
electronics forum suggested exactly this when faced with a multi-PSU rack system, noting that a
single 750 W–850 W ATX supply can provide 60–70 A on 12 V and would “solve the grounding issue”
while  simplifying  replacement  and  reducing  complexity
.  For  the  robotic  shell,  if  a  suitably
powerful supply (or a custom-designed power distribution unit) is obtained, it could feed all three
motherboards and peripherals from one source. The downside is the initial reason multiple PSUs
were used – maybe no single supply was available or one supply might not provide different voltage
rails needed. A compromise is to have one central high-power DC source (say 48 V battery or 24 V
supply)  and  then  use  DC-DC  converters  at  each  subsystem  (basically  implementing  a  proper
distributed  architecture  with  point-of-load  regulators).  This  would  provide  isolation  between
subsystems in terms of noise (to some degree, depending on converter isolation) and simplify
grounding (only one ground reference from the single source). The intermediate bus architecture
approach,  common  in  industry,  could  be  a  model:  use  an  intermediate  voltage  and  multiple
converters
. Adopting this in the robot would be a significant redesign, but conceptually it’s the
direction to move once prototyping transitions to productization.
Incorporating OR-ing and Redundancy: If there is a desire to keep multiple PSUs for either
redundancy or increased capacity, then adding OR-ing diodes or ideal diode controllers is a logical
next step. For instance, two 12 V PSUs could be diode-ORed to feed a common 12 V bus – this would
allow one PSU to take over if the other fails, improving reliability. It also prevents backfeed because
the diodes block reverse currents
. The trade-off is the voltage drop and some efficiency loss
across the diodes (Schottky diodes drop ~0.3–0.5 V at high current, which can be significant). Modern
ideal diode circuits use MOSFETs to reduce this drop. Implementing this would require a bit of
custom circuitry or off-the-shelf OR-ing boards. Given this is a prototype, full redundancy might not
be a priority now, but if the robot’s design moves toward any kind of  hot-swappable power or
battery backup, OR-ing is essential. For example, integrating a battery as a backup would involve
OR-ing the battery with the mains supply so that one can seamlessly take over from the other. The
current architecture has no provisions for that, but it could evolve in that direction.
Improved Grounding and Isolation: To tackle interference issues, future designs can segment
grounds into analog and digital, or dirty (motor) and clean (logic) grounds, that meet at a single
point. Right now everything is tied together; introducing ground isolation techniques could help. While
optical or galvanic isolation of every signal is impractical (and contrary to having a common ground),
selective  isolation  might  be  used.  For  instance,  isolating  the  motor  driver  control  signals  via
optocouplers would allow one to physically separate the motor ground from the logic ground,
eliminating a huge noise source. If that’s too drastic, at least ensuring a star grounding scheme (if
not already in place) should be formalized in the design. Additionally, using differential signaling for
any long data lines (so that they are less sensitive to ground noise) would be beneficial.

advanced iteration could include a  power management board that interfaces with all PSUs. This

Addressing Thermal and Mechanical Integration: An often overlooked aspect of multiple PSUs is
the physical space and heat. Three PSUs will take more space and likely produce more waste heat
(with lower overall efficiency) than a single supply sized for the job. As the design iterates, one might
consider using  server power supply modules which are compact and efficient. These often support
current sharing out-of-the-box and are designed for parallel operation (for example, two server PSUs
each capable of full load, in an N+1 redundant config). Replacing consumer ATX units with industrial
or server supplies could both save space and add reliability. The trade-off is that those might need
custom breakout boards and lack the multiple voltage rails (often server PSUs just give 12 V,
requiring separate DC-DC converters for 5 V, etc.). In the long run, a custom power distribution unit
could be built, but in the interim, choosing higher-quality PSUs intended for parallel use is an
improvement path.
Safety Enhancements: The current approach, lacking isolation, means that a fault in one part could
potentially propagate. For instance, if a high-voltage spike (from a motor driver fault) occurs on one
PSU’s output, it could travel through common ground or signal lines to other subsystems. To
improve safety, transient voltage suppressors (TVS diodes) could be placed on supply lines to clamp
spikes, and fuses or circuit breakers can isolate faults. Each PSU’s output to its loads should ideally
have fusing so that a short in one branch doesn’t drag the PSU (or others) down. It’s likely the
prototype uses the PSUs’ internal protection and maybe some fuses, but as currents and complexity
grow, a coordinated protection scheme is valuable. For example, a polyfuse on the 5 V line to a
sensor cluster can prevent one bad sensor board from shorting out that 5 V line and pulling down
the whole PSU.
Academic Perspective: Conceptually, this multi-PSU architecture is a case study in leveraging distributed,
heterogeneous  power  sources in  an  integrated  system.  It  invites  comparison  with  classical  power
distribution topologies. We can analyze it through the lens of system engineering: it achieves  functional
power delivery and some degree of modularity, at the expense of violating some design rules about isolation
and coupling. In an academic context, one could ask: what does this approach mean for system reliability
and  performance?  We  have  identified  that  it  creates  multiple  single  points  of  failure  (each  PSU  is
independent), whereas a proper redundant design might eliminate single points of failure but at cost of
complexity. It also highlights a scaling issue: this approach might work for 2–3 PSUs, but scaling to many
more would become unmanageable in terms of ground wiring and coordination. Thus, one insight is that
while using multiple small PSUs is expedient early on, there is a crossover point where designing a
consolidated power system becomes necessary.

the multi-PSU approach is perhaps less optimal for final performance, but possibly more adaptable during

Conclusion
In this report, we examined the conceptual design of a distributed power architecture employing multiple
consumer-grade PSUs in a complex robotic shell prototype. This multi-PSU approach was chosen for
pragmatic reasons: it allowed the use of readily available hardware to meet the system’s considerable and
diverse power needs, and enabled partitioning the load across several supplies. We detailed how the
architecture is arranged – with three PSUs supplying three motherboard-centered subsystems plus motors
and devices, all tied together with a common ground. This design achieves the basic goal of powering the
robot’s components and has parallels to distributed power strategies seen elsewhere in computing and
robotics prototypes (for example, splitting loads between PSUs as done in certain server pods
). The use
of  a  common  ground  simplifies  subsystem  integration  but  also  emerges  as  a  focal  point  for  many
challenges.
Our  exploration  of  the  challenges highlighted  important  considerations.  The  common  ground,  while
necessary, can lead to ground loops and noise coupling, especially with high-current motor loads causing
ground bounce
. The lack of isolation or diode OR-ing means there is a very real risk of backfeed
damage if supplies are not perfectly kept to their own loads
. Stability issues can arise since the supplies
operate independently – a surge on one rail can indirectly perturb another subsystem. Monitoring multiple
supplies  is  more  complicated,  potentially  leaving  blind  spots  in  power  management.  These  factors
underscore that the architecture, as implemented, is a compromise solution characteristic of a prototype:
it favors immediate functionality and flexibility over refined performance and fault tolerance. In academic
terms, it sacrifices some system robustness (violating the ideal of a single coordinated power source) in
exchange for modularity and convenience.
We also discussed how this architecture could be iterated upon and improved. Future refinements might
include consolidating power into a single source or a more formalized bus distribution to eliminate inter-
supply issues. Alternatively, if multiple PSUs remain, adding proper OR-ing circuits and control logic would
make the system more resilient and safe. Improved grounding practices (like star topology) and filtering will
be critical to manage interference as the system scales. Essentially, as the project matures, the power
system would need to evolve from an ad-hoc collection of PC supplies into a purpose-designed power
distribution network befitting a complex robotic platform. This could involve custom hardware but would
yield benefits in reliability and performance. Recommendations from experienced engineers – such as using

In  conclusion,  using  multiple  consumer  PSUs  with  a  common  ground  has  proven  to  be  a  workable
strategy in the prototype stage, demonstrating the viability of quickly mobilizing available resources to
power a multifaceted robotic system. The approach is conceptually simple and provides a learning platform
to identify power requirements and interactions in the system. However, it comes with clear limitations in
terms of electrical elegance and safety. As this distributed power architecture is refined, the lessons learned
– about load distribution, interference management, and failure modes – will guide the design toward a
more robust solution. The experience underscores a common engineering theme: the solutions that get a
prototype up and running (multiple cheap PSUs tied together) are often not the same solutions one will use
in the final implementation, yet they are a critical step in the development process. This report provided a
detailed overview of the concept, supported by technical insights from literature and practice, in an effort to
bridge that understanding towards eventual implementation of a safer, more stable power architecture for
the robotic system.
