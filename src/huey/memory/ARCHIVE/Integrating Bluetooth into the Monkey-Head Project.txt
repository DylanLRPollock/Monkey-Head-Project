Integrating Bluetooth into the Monkey-Head
Project
This technical report evaluates the use of Bluetooth wireless technology within the Monkey-Head Project (MHP). It
covers the fundamentals of Classic Bluetooth vs. Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE), short-range performance metrics,
device connectivity methods, and how Bluetooth could be applied to MHP’s hardware and workflows. We also
propose a design for a Bluetooth-based redundant file transfer layer, and examine security implications. All
information  is  up-to-date  as  of  mid-2025,  drawing  on  official  Bluetooth  SIG  documentation  and  reputable
technical sources.
1. Overview of Bluetooth Protocols (Classic vs. BLE)
Classic Bluetooth (BR/EDR): “Classic” Bluetooth refers to the original specification (Bluetooth versions 1.x
to 3.x) encompassing Basic Rate and Enhanced Data Rate modes
. It operates in the 2.4 GHz ISM band
with 79 channels and was designed for continuous, high-throughput connections such as audio streaming
or file exchange
. Classic Bluetooth forms point-to-point links (a  piconet architecture with one
master  and  up  to  7  active  slaves)  to  create  personal  area  networks
.  This  protocol  excels  at
supporting legacy devices and high data-rate applications (e.g. wireless headphones, file transfer, classic
HID devices) but at the cost of higher power consumption
.
Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE): Introduced in Bluetooth 4.0, BLE (also known as Bluetooth Smart) is a distinct,
incompatible protocol optimized for low power operation
. BLE also uses 2.4 GHz but with 40
channels and a different frequency-hopping scheme
. It was designed for devices that send small
amounts  of  data  infrequently,  enabling  coin-cell  battery  operation  for  months  or  years
.  BLE
connections incur very short active periods and can remain in sleep mode most of the time, drastically
reducing average power draw
. In terms of topology, BLE is more flexible: it supports one-to-one
communication  like  Classic,  but  also  one-to-many  (broadcast  mode)  and  many-to-many  via  mesh
networking
. This makes BLE well-suited for IoT sensor networks, beacons, and other applications
where energy efficiency and scalability matter more than raw throughput
.
Protocol Evolution: Both Classic and BLE continue to evolve under the Bluetooth SIG. Modern Bluetooth 5+
devices  often  support  dual  mode (both  Classic  and  BLE  radios).  Bluetooth  5  introduced  significant
improvements for BLE – doubling the maximum data rate and increasing range – without changing Classic
Bluetooth’s fundamentals
. However, the two protocols address different use cases: Classic for high-
bandwidth continuous streams (audio, large data) and BLE for low-bandwidth intermittent data (sensors,
control signals) with minimal power usage
.

2. Bluetooth Short-Range Data Transmission Capabilities
In evaluating Bluetooth for short-range data links in MHP, key performance metrics include  bandwidth, 
latency, range, and reliability, especially under real-world environmental conditions. Below we summarize
these characteristics for Classic Bluetooth and BLE:
2.1 Bandwidth and Data Rates
Classic Bluetooth Throughput: Classic BR/EDR supports a theoretical maximum gross air data rate
of 3 Mb/s (with Enhanced Data Rate), which yields roughly up to ~2 Mb/s of practical throughput for
user data
. In practice, real file transfer speeds over Classic Bluetooth (e.g. via the Serial Port
Profile or OBEX file transfer) tend to be on the order of a few hundred kilobytes per second. This
higher throughput enables use cases like audio streaming and bulk file transfers that would be
impractically slow over lower-bandwidth links
.
BLE Throughput: BLE was initially limited to 1 Mb/s physical bit rate, but Bluetooth 5 introduced a
2 Mb/s PHY option for higher speed, as well as a long-range coded PHY (500 kb/s or 125 kb/s)
.
Even  with  the  2   Mb/s  PHY,  protocol  overhead  (advertising  intervals,  connection  events,
acknowledgments) means effective BLE data throughput is typically far below the radio bit rate. In
Bluetooth 4.x (1 Mb/s), practical data throughput is on the order of 100–250 kb/s
. With Bluetooth
5’s 2 Mb/s mode and optimized parameters, real-world throughput up to ~1 Mb/s (around 0.8 Mb/s
or ~100 kB/s) can be achieved under ideal conditions, though ~0.5 Mb/s is more common.  In
summary, Classic Bluetooth still exceeds BLE in sustained data rate (roughly 2–3× higher),
making  Classic  a  better  choice  for  large  file  transfers  or  high-fidelity  streams
.  BLE’s
bandwidth, while lower, is usually sufficient for periodic sensor readings, control messages, and
other low-data-rate tasks in MHP.
Impact on MHP: For the Monkey-Head Project, this means that using Classic Bluetooth would allow
faster bulk data transfers (e.g. sending firmware updates or large log files) compared to BLE. On the
other hand, BLE’s throughput is adequate for telemetry or command/control data, and its lower data
rate comes with lower power usage. If the requirement is to occasionally send multi-megabyte files
as a redundancy measure, Classic Bluetooth (or the Bluetooth Classic mode of a dual-mode module)
may be preferable to meet time constraints.
2.2 Communication Latency
Classic Bluetooth Latency: Classic connections have relatively higher latency, on the order of
~100 ms for typical link setups
. The base Bluetooth BR/EDR physical layer uses time slots of
625 µs and a polling scheme; a device may take tens of milliseconds to respond depending on
scheduling and any retransmissions. In practical terms, human interactions like audio have ~100 ms
latency  over  Classic  profiles,  and  simple  data  exchange  pings  are  often  in  the  few  tens  of
milliseconds at best. Classic’s latency, while low enough for audio and many control tasks, is not as
low as BLE’s in optimized cases.
BLE Latency: BLE was designed for low latency. It supports connection intervals as short as 7.5 ms,
meaning a peripheral can send data to a central with latency on the order of 10 ms or less in ideal
conditions
. In practice, BLE can achieve ~6–20 ms latency for a data packet from one device to

Latency Considerations for MHP: Low latency is important if Bluetooth is used for control signals

2.3 Range and Signal Propagation
Typical Range: Bluetooth is inherently a short-range wireless technology. Classic Bluetooth devices
(Class 2 radios, ~2.5 mW) have a reliable range of roughly 10 meters indoors, often up to ~30 m in
open space
. BLE devices in practice achieve slightly longer range in open air – about 50 m
reliably, and up to ~150 m line-of-sight under ideal outdoor conditions with Bluetooth 5 long-range
features
. Notably, Bluetooth 5’s coded PHY (125 kbps mode) enables extended range (4×) at the
cost of throughput, and high-power Class 1 Bluetooth radios (100 mW) can approach 100 m range in
open environments. 
Environmental Factors: Real-world range is highly dependent on environmental conditions. The
2.4 GHz signals are strongly attenuated by obstacles such as walls, metal surfaces, and even the
human body (water content)
. Indoors, Bluetooth range may drop to a single room or adjacent
room if dense walls or metal equipment are present. Multipath fading and interference can also limit
practical range and reliability. Antenna design and placement are critical: a well-designed antenna in
the Monkey-Head Project’s hardware, mounted with clear line-of-sight, can maximize range, whereas
an internal or obstructed antenna may reduce it
. Device orientation and enclosure materials (for
example, a metal chassis) will likewise impact the signal propagation
. In summary, achieving the
high-end of Bluetooth range (tens of meters) requires optimal conditions – open air, minimal RF
noise, and good antenna performance.
Interference and Reliability: Bluetooth shares the 2.4 GHz ISM band with Wi-Fi, microwaves, and
other devices, so interference is a key consideration. Both Classic and BLE use frequency hopping
spread spectrum (FHSS) across their channels to mitigate interference; in fact, Bluetooth adaptively
hops 1600 times per second and can blacklist crowded channels (Adaptive Frequency Hopping) to
avoid Wi-Fi bands
. This gives Bluetooth decent robustness in noisy environments. However,
under heavy Wi-Fi traffic or RF noise, Bluetooth throughput can degrade and latency can increase
due to packet loss and retries. From a reliability standpoint, Bluetooth connections are generally
stable at short range, but in the worst-case environment (e.g. an industrial area with lots of 2.4 GHz
noise, or if the Monkey-Head robot is surrounded by multiple active Wi-Fi APs), throughput might fall
well  below  peak  values  and  occasional  dropouts  could  occur.  Careful  testing  in  the  intended
deployment environment is thus recommended. 
Range/Environment  in  MHP: The  Monkey-Head  Project  should  expect  solid  Bluetooth
communication within the same room or a radius of several meters, which is appropriate for most

2.4 Reliability Under Various Conditions
Line-of-Sight vs. Obstructions: In line-of-sight open conditions, both Classic and BLE can maintain
very  reliable  links  across  the  specified  ranges  (tens  of  meters).  With  obstructions,  reliability
diminishes. For example, a concrete wall might reduce the signal strength by an order of magnitude,
shortening reliable range to a few meters. Human bodies or crowds around the devices can absorb
signal (since 2.4 GHz is absorbed by water), causing variability if people move around
. In the
context of MHP, if the robot’s Bluetooth link is to a wearable or handheld device, simply turning or
having the robot’s body between the two devices could momentarily affect the signal strength.
Planning antenna placement (e.g. possibly mounting a Bluetooth antenna on the robot’s exterior or
head) can mitigate these issues.
Adaptive Features: Modern Bluetooth implementations have features to enhance link reliability.
Both Classic and BLE perform power control – devices can adjust transmit power to the minimum
needed, and increase it if error rates rise (within their class limits). BLE’s coded PHY uses error-
correcting codes to successfully deliver packets at longer distances or in noisy environments at the
expense of speed
. For critical data, BLE also offers an Acknowledged mode (for GATT writes or by
using notifications with application-layer ack) to ensure data receipt. These features mean that even
under suboptimal conditions, Bluetooth will often  throttle down (either by reducing speed or by
retransmitting) rather than completely dropping the connection. From a reliability standpoint, this
translates to graceful degradation under interference: the data will get through, but latency or
throughput might suffer.
Reliability for MHP: The key takeaway for the Monkey-Head Project is that Bluetooth can provide a
reliable short-range link for data and control as long as its limitations are respected. It should be
treated as a local wireless extension of the system, with expected variability in throughput. For non-
critical  telemetry  and  backup  data  transfer,  occasional  slow-downs  due  to  interference  are
acceptable. If using Bluetooth for any safety-critical control (e.g. an emergency stop signal), it’s
important  to  test  that  signal  gets  through  quickly  even  in  worst-case  RF  conditions  (or  use
redundancy, such as sending repeated stop commands). Generally, the fast reconnect times and
robust error handling of BLE make it suitable for IoT-style reliability (devices can disconnect and
reconnect within seconds or less), which aligns with intermittent use in a redundant system.

3.1 Pairing Mechanisms and Connection Setup
Classic Bluetooth Pairing: Classic Bluetooth typically requires an explicit pairing process to establish trust
between devices. Modern implementations use  Secure Simple Pairing (SSP) which was introduced in
Bluetooth 2.1. SSP utilizes an ECDH (Elliptic Curve Diffie-Hellman) key exchange and supports different
association models: Just Works, PIN/passkey entry, or Numeric Comparison on devices with a display
.
In practice, for devices without a UI (like many embedded modules), a fixed PIN or Just Works pairing is
used, which is convenient but offers no man-in-the-middle protection. Once paired, devices generate a link
key and enable encryption for all communications. The pairing is usually one-time; afterwards, devices
“bond” and recognize each other to reconnect without re-pairing. Classic’s pairing process is user-centric
(often requiring approval or a PIN), which suits peripheral accessories. In MHP, if Classic Bluetooth is used
(e.g. via a USB Bluetooth dongle on Huey), we’d likely configure a fixed PIN or use Just Works for ease of
pairing a headless robot with a laptop/phone. Alternatively, implementing Numeric Comparison (displaying
a code on the connected computer and confirming on a client) would provide stronger authentication if a
user interface is available during pairing.
BLE Connection and Pairing: BLE offers more flexibility – devices can communicate in two modes: -
Unpaired, broadcast mode: A BLE peripheral can send advertising packets to any scanner without needing
pairing. This is useful for one-way data like beacons, simple sensor broadcasts, or discovery of devices. -
Paired (bonded) connections: For two-way data exchange or secure data, BLE devices go through a pairing
process to exchange keys and enable encryption. BLE’s pairing in modern Bluetooth 5+ devices uses  LE
Secure Connections (also ECDH-based, similar in concept to SSP) to derive a 128-bit Long Term Key for
encryption
. BLE pairing has association models analogous to Classic: Just Works, Passkey, Numeric
Comparison, or Out-of-Band. The appropriate method is chosen based on device IO capabilities, and
developers are advised to choose the most secure method possible (avoiding unauthenticated Just Works if
feasible)
.
One notable difference is that BLE does not strictly require pairing if the data is meant to be open. For
example, a temperature sensor could simply advertise its readings, and any BLE scanner (within range) can
receive  it  without  formal  bonding.  In  the  context  of  MHP,  this  means  certain  data  like  non-sensitive
telemetry could be broadcast for convenience (no need to pair every time a new diagnostics tool wants to
listen).  However,  any  control  commands  or  file  transfers  would  necessitate  a  connected  mode  with
encryption, implying a pairing step.
Connection Setup Speed: BLE has an edge in how quickly connections can be established. It was designed
for fast connect/disconnect cycles; a BLE device can advertise and connect in a fraction of a second. Classic
Bluetooth pairing and connection establishment is slower (sometimes several seconds to discover, pair, and
open a RFCOMM channel). If MHP envisions connecting on the fly (for instance, a technician approaches the
robot with a tablet and quickly links via BLE to download logs), BLE’s quicker handshaking is beneficial
.
On the other hand, maintaining a continuous link (e.g. during a long file transfer) is well within both
protocols’ capabilities once the connection is up.

active slave devices in a piconet
. In theory, by time-sharing, one device could communicate with
multiple peripherals (for example, a computer connected to several sensors via SPP simultaneously). In

practice, however, Classic Bluetooth is most often used in one-to-one or one-to-two scenarios (e.g. a phone
connected to a headset and maybe a smartwatch concurrently). More complex “scatternet” configurations
(a device participating in multiple piconets to extend the network) were defined in the spec but are rarely
implemented in consumer devices. Essentially, Classic Bluetooth’s multi-device networking is limited and
often sequential (the master polls each slave in turn). For MHP, if we rely on Classic Bluetooth, the system
could pair with a handful of devices at once (say, a laptop, a phone, and a sensor module), but bandwidth is
shared among them. Each additional active connection will consume time slots and reduce throughput per
device, and beyond 7 devices the master cannot maintain more simultaneous links.
BLE Multi-Device: BLE was built with flexible multi-connection in mind. A BLE Central device (like a hub)
can connect to a large number of Peripherals concurrently – the limit is mostly up to the BLE stack
implementation and available memory/processing. Dozens of simultaneous BLE connections are possible
on modern hardware, and effectively there isn’t a fixed 7-device limit
. For example, an IoT gateway
might maintain 20 BLE peripheral links at once (e.g. to 20 sensors). Additionally, BLE supports  broadcast
communication: one device can advertise data that unlimited observers could receive, and observer devices
can listen to multiple broadcasters. This means one-to-many data distribution is inherently supported
(unlike Classic, which always pairs). 
For the Monkey-Head Project, if multiple subsystems or sensors were to use BLE, a single central hub (e.g.
the main robot’s computer with a BLE adapter) could gather data from all of them concurrently. Similarly,
the robot could broadcast a status packet and multiple monitoring devices (laptops, phones) could pick it up
simultaneously. Bluetooth Mesh (covered below) further extends the multi-device story for BLE by enabling
beyond-radio-range and many-to-many communications.
3.3 Mesh Networking with BLE
One of the compelling features introduced by the Bluetooth SIG in recent years is the  Bluetooth Mesh
profile (released in 2017). Bluetooth Mesh operates on top of BLE and allows formation of large-scale device
networks in a many-to-many topology
. In a Bluetooth Mesh network, devices (called nodes) relay
messages to each other such that devices need not be in direct radio range of every other device; messages
hop across intermediate nodes. This is ideal for scenarios like building automation (lighting systems, sensor
arrays) where potentially tens or hundreds of devices are spread out.
Mesh  Characteristics: Bluetooth  Mesh  uses  a  flooding  technique  (with  managed  flooding  to  control
message  count)  and  is  optimized  for  relatively  low-rate,  small  payload  data  (like  sensor  readings,
commands). It is not designed for high-bandwidth transfers; rather, it excels at reliability and scale. The
mesh specification supports up to thousands of nodes (practically 100+ is common, with a theoretical
limit around 32,000 nodes) in one network by using 16-bit addresses for nodes. All communications in
mesh are addressed (to groups or single nodes) and are encrypted with network and application keys,
ensuring security and message authenticity across the mesh.
Relevance to MHP: If the Monkey-Head Project envisions many distributed devices or modules that need to
coordinate via wireless (for instance, separate sensor pods or multiple robots working in concert), Bluetooth
Mesh is a candidate. A mesh could allow sensors on different parts of a facility to send data to the robot, or
allow multiple robots to share data, without requiring direct point-to-point links for every pair. However,
implementing mesh adds complexity – it requires all participating devices to run the Bluetooth Mesh stack
and tends to be used for fixed infrastructure (like stationary sensors) due to latency (mesh hops add delay,

3.4 Integration with Monkey-Head Project Hardware
The Monkey-Head Project’s hardware platform (“Huey” robotic shell) can integrate Bluetooth in several
ways. Given Huey’s core uses a Supermicro X9 server-class motherboard
 (which does not include
onboard wireless radios), adding Bluetooth will involve external hardware or modules:
USB or PCIe Adapter: The simplest path is to use a Bluetooth 5.x USB dongle on Huey’s main
computer. These adapters typically support dual-mode (Classic + BLE) and are inexpensive. Driver
support on the HostOS (Windows or Linux) is excellent – for example, on Linux the BlueZ stack can
manage the adapter, and on Windows the built-in Bluetooth stack would handle it. Using a standard
adapter leverages the full Bluetooth stack of the OS, meaning we can utilize high-level APIs to
implement profiles (RFCOMM sockets for Classic, GATT APIs for BLE, etc.). For MHP, a USB adapter
plugged into Huey and mounted with an external antenna (to avoid the metal case attenuating
signals) would likely be the fastest way to get Bluetooth connectivity.
Dedicated Bluetooth Module: Another approach is adding a microcontroller or wireless module
with Bluetooth capability and interfacing it to the main system. For instance, an Arduino or STM32
with a Bluetooth (or an SoC like ESP32 that has built-in Bluetooth) could serve as a subordinate
device that handles Bluetooth communication and forwards data to the main system via UART, USB,
or SPI. Classic modules like the HC-05 (Classic SPP) or HC-06 exist, as do BLE modules like nRF52-
based boards. However, since Huey already runs a full OS, using the OS’s stack via a dongle is
probably more straightforward than managing an external module’s firmware.
Integration with Subsystems: If certain subsystems in MHP (say, a sensor cluster or a wearable
controller) already have Bluetooth support, we should ensure compatibility. For example, if the
project uses a Raspberry Pi for a SubOS layer (Raspberry Pis have Bluetooth Low Energy built-in),
that could directly be leveraged. The “Test Hardware” or any legacy integration mentioned in the
project  should  be  surveyed  for  Bluetooth  capability.  According  to  the  documentation,  MHP
emphasizes legacy hardware integration (even things like vintage computers), but those likely do
not involve Bluetooth. So integration will mainly focus on modern components.
Pairing & User Interface: Integrating Bluetooth means we need a strategy for pairing devices with
the robot. On the software side, one could implement a command-line or GUI utility to put the robot
in pairing mode or to initiate pairing with a known device. Alternatively, for BLE, the robot can
advertise a custom service and accept connections without manual pairing if security is not a
concern for some data. Given MHP’s focus on ethical and secure design, we should plan to handle
pairing in a controlled manner (perhaps a secure pairing with a known passkey for authorized
engineer devices, etc.). The integration should also include an indication (LED or log message) when
Bluetooth is connected, to aid in debugging and trust.

4. Potential Bluetooth Use Cases in the Project
With Bluetooth added to MHP, there are several use cases where it could enhance or complement existing
capabilities. Below are key scenarios envisaged for Bluetooth within the project:
Wireless Sensor Telemetry: MHP could use BLE to collect data from wireless sensors on or around
the robot. For example, small BLE sensor nodes (measuring temperature, orientation, battery levels,
etc.) attached to moving parts of the robot or in its environment could transmit their readings to
GenCore. This would reduce cabling and allow easy addition of new sensors. BLE’s support for
broadcasting and star networks fits this use – the robot’s central system can listen to periodic
broadcasts from multiple sensors. As an illustration, many industrial and medical devices use BLE for
exactly this purpose – periodic, low-volume telemetry with years of battery life
. In MHP, a BLE
sensor could broadcast, say, the joint torque reading of an appendage, and the central system could
adjust control parameters accordingly in near real-time.
Remote Control and Command Signals: Bluetooth can serve for  direct remote control of the
robot or its subsystems over short range. For instance, an engineer’s smartphone or a dedicated
Bluetooth remote could pair with the robot to send control commands (movement instructions,
mode switches, calibration commands). BLE’s low latency (~10 ms) means even interactive control
(joystick, gamepad, or gesture control via a BLE wearable) is feasible without perceptible lag
.
Indeed,  many  hobby  and  consumer  robots  are  controlled  via  Bluetooth  from  phone  apps  or
controllers. Classic Bluetooth could also be used with standard HID profiles to connect a game
controller or keyboard for manual control in the field. Using Bluetooth for control has the benefit of
not requiring network infrastructure (useful in a lab or workshop, or emergency stop via a nearby
link). However, it’s best limited to short-range scenarios (line-of-sight, within a few meters) to ensure
reliability.
Diagnostics  and  Maintenance  Interface: Bluetooth  provides  a  convenient  way  to  access
diagnostic data from the robot for maintenance. For example, when a technician is physically near
the robot, they could connect via Bluetooth to pull system logs, error reports, or performance
metrics without needing a wired link or network connection. The robot could advertise a BLE
diagnostics  service that,  when  connected,  allows  reading  various  status  characteristics
(temperatures, CPU load, sensor health, etc.). This approach is common in IoT devices – many
appliances and machines have a BLE maintenance app that technicians use on-site. In MHP, an
advantage  is  isolation:  a  Bluetooth  maintenance  link  can  be  kept  local  (not  exposed  over  IP),

5. Proposal: Bluetooth as a Redundant File Transfer Layer
One specific application of Bluetooth within MHP is as a  redundant layer for the current file transfer
system. We propose introducing a Bluetooth-based file transfer capability that can serve as a backup to the
primary method (which may be Ethernet/Wi-Fi or physical media). This redundancy would enhance fault
tolerance and provide an alternative path for data exchange when the primary link is unavailable or
impractical. Below, we outline the benefits, challenges, and an implementation plan for this Bluetooth file
transfer layer.
5.1 Benefits of a Bluetooth Redundant Channel
Fault Tolerance: If the main file transfer mechanism fails (e.g., Wi-Fi network is down or cable
disconnected), a Bluetooth link ensures that critical data (configuration files, small binary updates,
log files, etc.) can still be exchanged locally. This is particularly useful for emergency situations or
troubleshooting when other networks are unreliable. The robot could automatically fall back to
Bluetooth to send an alert or receive a command file when it detects primary network loss.
Mobility and Convenience: A Bluetooth link does not require existing infrastructure (like a router)
or physical tethering. Team members can approach the robot with a laptop, tablet, or even a

demos, where time and mobility are factors.
Isolation  from  Network  Issues: Bluetooth  being  a  local  point-to-point  connection  means  it’s
immune to broader network outages or firewall issues. The file transfer over Bluetooth can be done
even in secure facilities or isolated environments where the robot might not be allowed on the main
network. It’s a self-contained channel between the engineer’s device and the robot.
Security (Local Scope): While we must consider Bluetooth’s own security (see Section 6), using a
short-range direct link can be safer in some contexts than sending files over a larger network. For
instance, if the team wants to update the robot without exposing it to the internet, a Bluetooth
transfer from a nearby trusted device is an option. The limited range also means an attacker would
need to be physically nearby to even attempt interference.
Independent Band: Bluetooth operates on its own stack and frequencies; if the primary system is
Wi-Fi at 5 GHz or an Ethernet connection, Bluetooth at 2.4 GHz provides a path that won’t be affected
by  issues  on  those  other  channels  (except  general  EMI).  Even  if  the  robot’s  main  networking
hardware fails, as long as power is on and the Bluetooth module is functional, some communication
persists.
5.2 Challenges and Limitations
Throughput Limitations: As discussed, Bluetooth (especially BLE) has much lower throughput than
wired  or  Wi-Fi  networks.  Transferring  large  files  (e.g.  multi-megabyte  logs  or  datasets)  over
Bluetooth will be slow. For example, a 10 MB file might transfer in a few seconds over Wi-Fi, but
could take on the order of a few minutes via Classic Bluetooth, and even longer over BLE. This is a
significant limitation for using Bluetooth as a file transfer medium
. The team must decide what
file  sizes  and  types  are  reasonable  for  the  Bluetooth  channel  (e.g.  perhaps  limit  it  to  critical
configuration files, small patches, or summary logs, rather than full software images).
Range and Connectivity Constraints: Bluetooth’s short range means the transferring device must
be near the robot. Unlike the primary file transfer which might work over a facility network or
remotely, Bluetooth requires physical proximity. If the robot is inaccessible or in operation far from
the operator, the Bluetooth backup channel won’t help until someone can get close. This makes it a
local backup only, not a remote failsafe.
Interference and Bandwidth Sharing: The 2.4 GHz band can be crowded. If multiple Bluetooth
devices are in use or heavy Wi-Fi interference is present, file transfer performance will degrade
further. Additionally, Bluetooth is half-duplex and shares bandwidth among any other connected
devices; if we have multiple Bluetooth links active (say, sensor streams plus a file transfer), they will
contend for airtime and slow each other. We need to manage usage (perhaps pausing non-essential
BT traffic during a file transfer).
Integration Complexity: Implementing a robust file transfer over Bluetooth adds complexity to the
system. We must handle the connection management, file segmentation, error recovery, and ensure

5.3 Implementation Plan
To implement Bluetooth as a redundant file transfer layer in MHP, we propose the following steps:
Hardware Setup: Install a Bluetooth 5.x dual-mode adapter on the MHP main system (Huey’s
HostOS). For example, a USB Bluetooth dongle with an external antenna for better range. Ensure the
driver (BlueZ on Linux SubOS or the Windows stack on HostOS, depending on where the file service
will run) is properly configured. Verify that the adapter can operate in both Classic and BLE modes as
needed.
Protocol Selection: Decide whether to use Classic Bluetooth or BLE for the file transfer service, or
possibly support both for flexibility. 
Classic Option: Use the Serial Port Profile (SPP) or the Object Push/File Transfer Profile (OPP/FTP).
SPP would create a virtual COM port over RFCOMM – we can then run a simple file transfer protocol
(even as simple as XMODEM/ZMODEM or a custom framing) over this serial link. OPP/FTP would
leverage existing Bluetooth profiles for file exchange (OBEX protocol), which might be integrated
with OS tools (for instance, a laptop could just browse the robot’s exposed Bluetooth filesystem).
Classic will generally offer higher throughput than BLE and might be simpler to interface with PC
tools.
BLE Option: Define a GATT service for file transfer. For example, create a service with characteristics
for “file name”, “file data chunk”, “transfer control”, etc. The laptop or phone app can connect as a
BLE central, discover the service, and perform a sequence to upload/download a file in chunks (likely
20–512 bytes per chunk, depending on MTU negotiated). BLE would have the advantage of lower
energy (not critical for a robot) and easier multi-platform support (since all modern phones support
BLE well). However, achieving good throughput needs tuning (increase ATT MTU, use notifications for
bursty data transfer).

wait for a Bluetooth connection. The team can initiate the Bluetooth transfer manually when needed
(since Bluetooth won’t be constantly active sending files unless invoked). In practice, this might
mean documenting a procedure: “If Wi-Fi transfer fails, connect via Bluetooth with our tool to
retrieve the file.” Alternatively, more automation: the robot could detect lack of network and then
advertise via BLE “I have log files ready” – an engineer’s device could pick that up and then connect.
The exact integration depends on how autonomous we want it, but at minimum there should be a
clear operational mode for Bluetooth transfer that the team can trigger.

Measure throughput by sending files of known sizes and timing them at different ranges (close by
vs. at 5m, etc.).
Introduce interference (e.g. heavy Wi-Fi usage or multiple BLE devices) to see how it affects transfer
reliability.
Test boundary cases like dropping the connection mid-transfer (does our protocol resume or at least
fail gracefully without corrupting data?).
If BLE, test with different smartphone models and OSes if a mobile app is intended, ensuring broad

6. Security Considerations for Bluetooth Data Transmission
Security  is  paramount  when  introducing  any  wireless  channel,  and  Bluetooth  is  no  exception.  While
Bluetooth protocols have built-in security features, they also have known vulnerabilities if not configured
properly. In the context of MHP, where potentially sensitive AI modules and control systems are at play, we
need to address Bluetooth security on multiple levels: pairing security, link encryption, data access control,
and operational policies.
Encryption  and  Pairing  Security: Modern  Bluetooth  standards  mandate  strong  encryption.  BLE
connections using LE Secure Connections employ 128-bit AES-CCM encryption for all traffic, with session
keys derived via ECDH key exchange
. This provides a high level of cryptographic security, comparable to
internet protocols. Classic Bluetooth SSP (since v2.1) likewise uses ECDH (P-192 elliptic curve) to generate
link keys and can achieve 128-bit equivalent security. The important factor is the pairing method: if “Just
Works” pairing is used (no passkey or user confirmation), the link is encrypted but not authenticated – an
attacker could theoretically perform a man-in-the-middle during the initial pairing. Therefore, for MHP: -
Use authenticated pairing whenever possible. For example, require a passkey or numeric comparison for
pairing any official laptops/phones with the robot. This might involve displaying a PIN on the robot’s
console or having a fixed passkey shared among the team. - If the robot has no interface to confirm a
pairing code, a compromise is to use a pre-shared PIN code (something not trivial like “0000”) and compile it
into the robot and the client tool – this at least keeps casual devices out. -  BLE Privacy Features: BLE
provides address randomization to prevent tracking
. The robot’s BLE adapter should use a resolvable
private address when advertising to avoid exposing a static MAC address to eavesdroppers. This doesn’t
directly secure data, but it helps with privacy. - After pairing (bonding), ensure the robot remembers bonded
devices and will not accept new connections from unknown devices without pairing. This prevents an
attacker from bypassing security by directly connecting if they somehow spoof the MAC address.
Access Control: Not all data should be freely accessible even to paired devices. We should enforce access
control on the application level. For example, if using Bluetooth to fetch logs, perhaps require a command

Vulnerabilities  and  Updates: Be  aware  of  known  Bluetooth  exploits:  -  BlueBorne  (2017): a  set  of
vulnerabilities that allowed malicious devices to take control without pairing. Patches have been released
for most platforms. Ensure the robot’s OS and Bluetooth stack are updated to include these patches. -
KNOB  Attack  (2019): an  attack  on  Classic  Bluetooth’s  key  negotiation,  potentially  downgrading  the
encryption key strength. The fix is to enforce not accepting extremely short encryption keys. Modern OS
stacks  have  this  fix  –  verify  the  dongle  and  stack  are  Bluetooth  5+  compliant  which  should  include
mitigation.  -  BIAS  (2020): an  impersonation  attack  where  a  previously  bonded  device  could  be
impersonated. The countermeasure is also updated firmware that enforces stricter role switching checks.
Again, using a fully updated Bluetooth 5.1+ controller should have this covered, but we should use latest
firmware. - Sniffing: Bluetooth’s encryption can be broken if the pairing was insecure. Using secure pairing
prevents passive eavesdropping. However, one should operate under the assumption that a determined
attacker with specialized hardware (Bluetooth sniffer arrays) could capture and possibly crack older pairing
or any undisclosed flaws. Mitigate this by  limiting the sensitive data sent over Bluetooth or adding an
application layer encryption for extremely sensitive payloads (e.g., if transferring credentials or critical AI
secrets, double-encrypt with AES-256 at the file level in addition to Bluetooth link encryption).
Operational Security: - Only keep Bluetooth discoverable when needed. The robot’s Bluetooth should not
be perpetually discoverable to everyone. We can have it in non-discoverable mode by default and only enter
discoverable/pairing mode on a specific user action or during a maintenance window. This reduces the
attack surface (an attacker won’t even see the device unless they time it when we allow). - Implement
timeouts for connections. If a device is paired but not actively doing anything, disconnect it after a period of
inactivity. This minimizes the window for any hijacking or misuse. - For BLE, utilize the lowest necessary
privilege on characteristics. If some characteristics (like a sensor reading) truly need to be accessible
without encryption (not recommended, but for argument’s sake), partition them so that critical controls or
file  transfers  absolutely  require  encryption  and  perhaps  an  authentication  token.  BLE  has  attribute
permissions; we should set all write operations and sensitive reads to require encryption & authentication. -
Train team members to not pair random devices. They should know which devices are authorized. If the
robot suddenly shows a pairing request from an unknown phone, they should reject it and investigate.
Testing Security: We should perform a mini penetration test for the Bluetooth interface. This includes: -
Scanning for the device from an unauthorized phone (it should not be visible unless in pairing mode). -
Trying a brute-force PIN pairing (the robot should have a limit or a long PIN to thwart this). - Using a
Bluetooth sniffer during pairing to ensure our chosen method (numeric comparison or passkey) indeed
prevents MITM (in practice, a MITM should not succeed if we do it right). - If possible, test the file transfer
service for resilience (does it allow directory traversal? buffer overflow if sending a malformed file name?
etc.). Treat it like any network service in terms of hardening.
Device Security: Ensure the Bluetooth module’s firmware is updated. Many Bluetooth controllers can get
firmware updates (for example, Intel or Broadcom Bluetooth chips on adapters). These updates often patch
protocol vulnerabilities. Make checking for Bluetooth firmware updates part of the maintenance schedule.
By addressing the above points, the Monkey-Head Project can confidently utilize Bluetooth’s convenience
without compromising security. In summary, use strong pairing (LE Secure Connections / SSP with

Conclusion
Integrating Bluetooth technology into the Monkey-Head Project stands to provide significant benefits in
flexibility, redundancy, and user interaction. We have reviewed how Classic Bluetooth and BLE differ and
where each might fit in MHP’s architecture: Classic offers higher throughput for tasks like file transfer,
whereas BLE provides low-power, low-latency links ideal for sensor data and control signals. Bluetooth’s
short-range capabilities (on the order of 10–50 m) align well with on-site operations and debugging, and its
reliability  can  be  maintained  through  careful  attention  to  environmental  factors  and  interference
management. 
We outlined various connectivity modes – from simple one-to-one pairings to complex mesh networks –
concluding that while mesh networking is an impressive feature of BLE for IoT, the immediate needs of
MHP can be met with point-to-point and star topologies (with the central robot communicating to multiple
peripherals or user devices). Integration at the hardware level is straightforward using standard Bluetooth
adapters,  and  the  software  stack  (whether  leveraging  existing  profiles  or  custom  protocols)  can  be
developed with readily available libraries.
Crucially, the proposal to add a Bluetooth-based redundant file transfer layer offers a new degree of fault
tolerance. By enabling local wireless transfers of key data, the team can recover or update the system even
when primary channels are down, all while untethered. The implementation plan provided gives a step-by-
step guide to achieving this, from hardware selection to service development and testing.
Finally, we stressed that security must be woven into every step of Bluetooth adoption. When configured
with modern security practices – authenticated pairing, encryption, limited discoverability – Bluetooth
communication can be made robust against eavesdropping and unauthorized access
. The team
should remain vigilant with updates and best practices, treating the Bluetooth interface with the same care
as any network interface.
In conclusion, adding Bluetooth to the Monkey-Head Project complements its adaptive, modular ethos. It
will enable engineers and subsystems to interact with the system in new ways (like a “virtual tether” for
diagnostics and control) and serve as a safety net in data delivery. With the information and plan detailed in
this report, the team can proceed to prototype and integrate Bluetooth functionality, thereby enhancing the
resilience and versatility of the MHP platform.
